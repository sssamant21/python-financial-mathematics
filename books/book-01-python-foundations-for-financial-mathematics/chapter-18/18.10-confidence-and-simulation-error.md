## Learning objectives

Calculate and interpret Monte Carlo standard errors and confidence intervals; distinguish simulation uncertainty from outcome volatility and model error; evaluate event probabilities and rare-event limitations; design a sample-size study; and report uncertainty with appropriate assumptions.

## 1. Why report simulation error?

A Monte Carlo estimate changes from sample to sample. Reporting only a point estimate hides how much variation can arise from finite sampling. A standard error and a suitable interval help communicate that variation under the specified model and sampling method.

Sampling uncertainty is only one source of error. Input estimates, model assumptions, discretization, implementation defects and market changes can dominate it. More simulated paths do not automatically address these sources.

## 2. Outcome volatility versus estimator uncertainty

For IID observations Xᵢ with finite variance σ², the sample mean X̄ estimates E[X]. Individual outcomes have standard deviation σ; the mean has standard error **σ/√N**. Estimate the latter with **s/√N**, where s is the sample standard deviation.

Example: σ=20 and N=10,000 gives theoretical SE=.2. Individual outcomes remain variable with SD 20; the average is estimated more precisely. Four times as many independent observations approximately halves SE.

The formula requires independent observations or an appropriate independent unit. Correlated paths, weighted observations and variance-reduction schemes need a suitable estimator-specific uncertainty calculation.

## 3. A fixed-sample confidence interval

For a sample mean, a common large-sample normal approximation is:

**X̄ ± 1.96 s/√N** for a nominal 95% confidence interval.

If X̄=100.1 and SE=.2, the interval is **[99.708,100.492]**. In frequentist terms, under the method's assumptions, approximately 95% of intervals produced by repeated samples would cover the fixed model mean. The observed interval either contains that mean or does not.

The normal approximation may be poor for small, highly skewed or heavy-tailed samples. For IID normal observations with unknown variance, a Student-t interval using N−1 degrees of freedom is an exact classical interval under that normal model. Other settings require an appropriate method.

## 4. Confidence level and width

For the same estimate and SE, a higher nominal confidence level generally uses a larger critical value and produces a wider interval. For a fixed method and sample distribution, increasing N tends to narrow the interval at rate 1/√N.

A narrow interval indicates precision about the **model estimand** under the sampling assumptions. It does not demonstrate that the model, inputs or financial assumptions are correct.

## 5. Analytical benchmark

Suppose a payoff X is 80, 100 or 120 with probabilities .25, .50 and .25. Its exact mean is **100** and variance is **200**. For N=10,000 independent draws, theoretical SE=√200/100≈**.14142**; a normal interval centered on an observed estimate has approximate half-width 1.96×.14142≈**.27719** if the theoretical SE is used.

This is a theoretical benchmark. A practical estimated SE uses sample variation and will itself vary.

## 6. Probability estimates and rare events

For an event indicator Iᵢ∈{0,1}, p̂=ΣIᵢ/N estimates the model probability. Under independent Bernoulli sampling, theoretical SE is **√[p(1−p)/N]**; the plug-in estimate uses p̂.

For P(X<100)=.25 and N=10,000, theoretical SE≈.00433. The normal approximation can be unreliable near 0 or 1 or for rare events. Zero observed events does not establish zero probability; the plug-in SE of zero is misleading as a full uncertainty statement. Use an appropriate binomial interval, such as Wilson or exact binomial, and assess the sampling design.

## 7. Wilson interval

For k events in N independent Bernoulli trials, p̂=k/N and normal critical value z, the Wilson interval is:

**center = (p̂+z²/(2N))/(1+z²/N)**

**half-width = z√[p̂(1−p̂)/N+z²/(4N²)]/(1+z²/N)**.

The endpoints are center ± half-width. For k=0, N=100 and z≈1.96, the interval is approximately [0, .0370], illustrating uncertainty despite zero observed events. Its coverage is not exact for every p and N; assumptions and method selection matter.

## 8. Discounted payoffs

If a one-year payoff has model expectation 100 and is discounted at a constant annual effective rate of 5%, its discounted expectation is 100/1.05≈95.2381. Its standard deviation and SE are also divided by 1.05.

Specify the measure and discounting assumptions. Discounting a real-world expectation does not automatically produce a market-consistent price. A confidence interval for a numerical model estimate is not an interval for future realized payoff.

## 9. Sample-size planning

Given a preliminary SD estimate σ, target approximate 95% half-width h and an appropriate normal approximation, **N≈(1.96σ/h)²**, rounded upward. With σ=20 and h=.5, N=6,147 after rounding up.

This is planning guidance: σ may be uncertain, observations may be dependent and a skewed payoff may require more careful analysis. Decide the sample size or a valid sequential method before examining the result.

## 10. Sequential stopping

Repeatedly computing a fixed-N confidence interval and stopping when it first becomes narrow can change its coverage. Use a prespecified N or a confidence sequence / sequential procedure designed for that stopping rule. Record any adaptive procedure and assumptions.

## 11. Independent units and variance reduction

With antithetic samples, compute uncertainty from independent pair averages when pairs are the independent units. For common random numbers, compare models using paired differences and their standard error. Reusing the same stream accidentally across workers can duplicate paths and understate uncertainty.

Different arbitrary seeds do not prove independence. Use documented stream-spawning mechanisms and retain generator and worker assignments.

## 12. Error budget and reporting

Report the point estimate, sample count, method, standard error, interval and confidence level, units, seed, generator, probability measure, model assumptions and known limitations. State whether an interval addresses sampling uncertainty only. Separate input uncertainty, discretization, model and numerical error from sampling variability; a single interval usually does not quantify all of them.

Validate against analytical cases, inspect boundary behavior, use independent replications where appropriate, and retain actual results and software versions. Do not interpret a small SE as model validation.

## 13. Reference implementation

Save as `simulation_error.py`. It calculates an illustrative normal interval for a mean and a Wilson interval for an independent Bernoulli event. It uses only the standard library.

~~~python
import math
import statistics
import unittest

def finite_number(x):
    return type(x) in (int,float) and math.isfinite(x)

def mean_interval(values, z=1.96):
    vals = list(values)
    if len(vals) < 2 or not all(finite_number(x) for x in vals):
        raise ValueError("at least two finite observations required")
    if not finite_number(z) or z <= 0:
        raise ValueError("positive finite critical value required")
    mean = statistics.mean(vals)
    se = statistics.stdev(vals)/math.sqrt(len(vals))
    return {"count":len(vals), "mean":mean, "standard_error":se,
            "interval":(mean-z*se,mean+z*se), "critical_value":z}

def wilson_interval(events, trials, z=1.96):
    if type(events) is not int or type(trials) is not int or trials < 1:
        raise ValueError("integer events and positive trials required")
    if events < 0 or events > trials or not finite_number(z) or z <= 0:
        raise ValueError("invalid events or critical value")
    p = events/trials
    z2 = z*z
    denominator = 1+z2/trials
    center = (p+z2/(2*trials))/denominator
    half = z*math.sqrt(p*(1-p)/trials+z2/(4*trials*trials))/denominator
    # Clamp tiny rounding excursions at the probability boundaries.
    return max(0.0,center-half), min(1.0,center+half)

def planned_count(sd, half_width, z=1.96):
    if any(not finite_number(x) for x in (sd,half_width,z)):
        raise ValueError("finite inputs required")
    if sd < 0 or half_width <= 0 or z <= 0:
        raise ValueError("nonnegative SD and positive width and z required")
    return math.ceil((z*sd/half_width)**2)

class ErrorTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_mean(self):
        self.assertEqual(mean_interval([80,100,120])["mean"],100)
    def test_se(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(mean_interval([80,100,120])["standard_error"],20/math.sqrt(3))
    def test_constant(self):
        self.assertEqual(mean_interval([100,100])["interval"],(100,100))
    def test_wilson_boundary(self):
        lo,hi=wilson_interval(0,100)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(lo,0)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(hi,.0369948,places=6)
    def test_wilson_center(self):
        lo,hi=wilson_interval(50,100)
        self.assertAlmostEqual((lo+hi)/2,.5)
    def test_wilson_bounds(self):
        lo,hi=wilson_interval(100,100)
        self.assertGreaterEqual(lo,0)
        self.assertLessEqual(hi,1)
    def test_planning(self):
        self.assertEqual(planned_count(20,.5),6147)
    def test_bad_count(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): wilson_interval(1,0)
    def test_bad_events(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): wilson_interval(11,10)
    def test_bad_width(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): planned_count(20,0)
    def test_nonfinite(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): mean_interval([1,float("nan")])
    def test_short_sample(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): mean_interval([1])

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("mean example:",mean_interval([80,100,120]))
    print("zero events Wilson:",wilson_interval(0,100))
    print("planned paths:",planned_count(20,.5))
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
~~~

The planning example yields **ceil((1.96×20/.5)²)=6,147**; the unrounded value is 6,146.56. The code's interval methods assume independent observations and do not quantify model or parameter uncertainty.

## 16. Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Possible cause | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| Narrow interval but wrong benchmark | Bias or model error | Review payoff, inputs and independent target |
| Zero estimated event error | No observed events | Use a suitable binomial interval |
| Different SE across replications | Sampling variation | Compare assumptions and repeated results |
| CI coverage appears poor | Small or skewed sample, dependence | Assess method and sampling design |
| Workers return duplicate paths | Reused stream | Review stream spawning and assignments |
| Interval widens unexpectedly | Higher variability or confidence level | Check units, parameters and critical value |
