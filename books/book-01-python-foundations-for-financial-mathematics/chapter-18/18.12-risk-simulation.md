## Learning objectives

Define loss consistently; estimate loss probability, Value at Risk (VaR) and Expected Shortfall (ES) from simulated outcomes; explain empirical quantile and tail conventions; distinguish model and sampling uncertainty; and validate risk calculations against transparent examples.

## 1. Define the risk question

Risk simulation generates possible future outcomes under a stated model and summarizes their consequences. Define the position, valuation date, horizon, units, currency, probability measure, cash flows, costs and the specific risk measure before running it.

This section defines **loss L=V₀−V₁** for a one-period position with no external cash flows. A positive L is a loss; a negative L is a gain. If cash flows or fees occur, incorporate them consistently. A simulated distribution is conditional on assumptions; it is not a guarantee about future losses.

## 2. Loss probability

Estimate P(L>0) by the fraction of independent simulated losses strictly greater than zero. Zero loss is excluded by this definition. State the threshold and inequality: P(L≥0) can differ, especially with discrete outcomes.

If N=1,000 and 250 paths have L>0, the estimate is .25. Its uncertainty depends on sampling design; zero observed losses do not prove loss probability is zero.

## 3. Value at Risk

For loss L and confidence level α in (0,1), define the lower quantile:

**VaRα = inf{x: P(L≤x)≥α}.**

Under a continuous distribution, the model probability of exceeding this quantile is generally 1−α. For discrete distributions, probability mass at the quantile means P(L>VaRα) can be smaller than 1−α. VaR is a threshold, not the maximum possible loss and not the average severity of losses beyond it. Depending on the loss distribution, VaR may be negative.

Always report confidence level, horizon, currency, position and quantile convention.

## 4. Expected Shortfall

Define ES at level α as the average of upper loss quantiles:

**ESα = (1/(1−α)) ∫α¹ VaRᵤ du.**

For a continuous distribution with suitable integrability, this corresponds to the conditional mean loss in the upper tail. For distributions with mass at VaR, simply averaging observations strictly above VaR or at/above VaR can give a different answer; boundary mass must be handled consistently.

ES describes average tail loss under the model, not a maximum loss. Both VaR and ES depend on the distribution, horizon, valuation and position.

## 5. A transparent discrete example

Consider losses −10, 0, 20 and 40 with probabilities .25 each. Their mean is 12.5. Using the lower quantile definition:

- VaR at α=.75 is **20** because P(L≤20)=.75.
- ES at α=.75 is **40**, the average of the highest 25% of the distribution.
- P(L>0)=.5 and P(L>20)=.25.

At α=.80, VaR is 40 and ES is also 40. Here P(L>VaR)=0 because of the mass at 40. This illustrates why continuous-distribution intuition can fail for discrete outcomes.

## 6. Empirical conventions

For sorted observed losses L₍₁₎≤…≤L₍ₙ₎ and equally weighted observations, this chapter uses nearest-rank VaR with **k=ceil(αN)** and VaR=L₍ₖ₎ (one-based indexing).

For empirical ES, integrate the empirical quantile function over [α,1]. Equivalently, average exactly the highest **N(1−α)** observation-equivalents, using a fractional weight on the boundary observation when that number is not an integer.

For sorted [−10,0,20,40], α=.75 gives k=3, VaR=20 and ES=40. At α=.625 the upper tail contains 1.5 observation-equivalents: 40 plus half of 20, divided by 1.5, giving **ES≈33.3333**. Other software may use interpolation or different tail conventions; document the chosen method before comparing outputs.

## 7. Simulation workflow

1. Define exposures, horizon, loss sign, risk factors and valuation.
2. Specify probability measure, joint distribution, dependence and parameters.
3. Simulate paths or scenarios and calculate P&L including defined costs.
4. Convert P&L to losses with a consistent sign.
5. Calculate risk measures using documented empirical conventions.
6. Quantify Monte Carlo uncertainty with an appropriate method.
7. Compare with analytical or independently constructed benchmarks.
8. Test stresses, assumptions and sensitivity; retain evidence and limitations.

Do not assign an implied probability to a deterministic stress unless a probabilistic model supports it. A Monte Carlo frequency estimates a probability under its modeled distribution.

## 8. Tail sampling and uncertainty

Tail estimates may be noisy because relatively few observations lie in the tail. At α=.99 with 10,000 independent paths, roughly 100 observation-equivalents contribute to empirical ES under the convention above. This is not a guarantee of accuracy.

Quantile and ES uncertainty is not generally the same as s/√N for a sample mean. Use a method appropriate to the estimator and sampling design, such as independent replications or a carefully designed bootstrap, and investigate stability. Rare-event methods can help but require correct weights and validation.

Zero observed exceedances do not prove the true probability is zero. A plug-in Bernoulli standard error of zero at the boundary is not a complete uncertainty statement.

## 9. Dependence and model risk

Joint asset behavior, correlations, tails, volatility and liquidity can change under stress. An incorrectly specified joint distribution can give misleading VaR or ES even with millions of paths. Validate exposures, model calibration and assumptions; complement simulation with transparent stress scenarios.

A normal simple-return model may produce returns below −100% for limited-liability assets. Clipping changes the distribution; choose and document an appropriate model rather than hiding invalid outcomes.

## 10. Horizon and scaling

A one-day VaR and a ten-day VaR are different estimands. Square-root-of-time scaling requires restrictive assumptions and is not universal, particularly with changing positions, dependence, jumps or illiquidity. Prefer modeling the desired horizon with documented assumptions.

Include fees, funding and cash-flow timing as appropriate. Report whether P&L is marked to market and whether rebalancing or liquidation is modeled.

## 11. Validation and evidence

Check loss signs and units, quantile level, sorting, boundary cases, fractional tail mass and probability weights. Test against independently calculated discrete examples and compare with a separate implementation using the same definition.

Record code and model versions, positions, data timestamps, assumptions, generator, seed, path count, confidence level, quantile method and results. Backtesting and model review are separate activities; passing a few examples does not establish production suitability.

## 12. Reference implementation

Save as `risk_simulation.py`. It calculates nearest-rank empirical VaR and fractional-tail empirical ES from equally weighted losses. It does not simulate market dynamics, calculate confidence intervals or support unequal scenario weights.

~~~python
import math
import unittest

def finite(x):
    return type(x) in (int,float) and math.isfinite(x)

def validate_losses(losses, alpha):
    values=list(losses)
    if not values or not all(finite(x) for x in values):
        raise ValueError("nonempty finite numeric losses required")
    if not finite(alpha) or not 0 < alpha < 1:
        raise ValueError("alpha must be finite and in (0,1)")
    return sorted(values)

def nearest_rank_var(losses, alpha):
    values=validate_losses(losses,alpha)
    rank=math.ceil(alpha*len(values))
    return values[rank-1]

def empirical_es(losses, alpha):
    values=validate_losses(losses,alpha)
    tail_count=(1-alpha)*len(values)
    full=math.floor(tail_count)
    fraction=tail_count-full
    tail_sum=math.fsum(values[-full:]) if full else 0.0
    if fraction > 0:
        tail_sum += fraction*values[-full-1]
    result=tail_sum/tail_count
    if not math.isfinite(result):
        raise ValueError("nonfinite expected shortfall")
    return result

def loss_frequency(losses):
    values=list(losses)
    if not values or not all(finite(x) for x in values):
        raise ValueError("nonempty finite numeric losses required")
    return sum(x>0 for x in values)/len(values)

def losses_from_values(initial, terminal_values):
    if not finite(initial) or initial <= 0:
        raise ValueError("positive finite initial value required")
    vals=list(terminal_values)
    if not vals or any(not finite(v) or v < 0 for v in vals):
        raise ValueError("nonempty nonnegative finite terminal values required")
    losses=[initial-v for v in vals]
    if not all(math.isfinite(x) for x in losses):
        raise ValueError("nonfinite loss")
    return losses

class RiskTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_loss_sign(self):
        self.assertEqual(losses_from_values(100,[90,100,110]),[10,0,-10])
    def test_loss_frequency(self):
        self.assertEqual(loss_frequency([-10,0,20,40]),.5)
    def test_var(self):
        self.assertEqual(nearest_rank_var([-10,0,20,40],.75),20)
    def test_es(self):
        self.assertEqual(empirical_es([-10,0,20,40],.75),40)
    def test_fractional_es(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(empirical_es([-10,0,20,40],.625),100/3)
    def test_boundary_mass(self):
        self.assertEqual(nearest_rank_var([-10,0,20,40],.8),40)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(empirical_es([-10,0,20,40],.8),40)
    def test_sorting(self):
        self.assertEqual(nearest_rank_var([40,-10,20,0],.75),20)
    def test_constant(self):
        self.assertEqual(empirical_es([5,5,5],.9),5)
    def test_bad_alpha(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): nearest_rank_var([1,2],1)
    def test_empty(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): empirical_es([],.95)
    def test_nonfinite(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): loss_frequency([float("nan")])
    def test_negative_terminal(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): losses_from_values(100,[-1])

if __name__ == "__main__":
    losses=[-10,0,20,40]
    print("loss probability:",loss_frequency(losses))
    print("VaR 75%:",nearest_rank_var(losses,.75))
    print("ES 75%:",empirical_es(losses,.75))
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
~~~

The functions use ordinary floating-point arithmetic, and quantile boundaries near representational rounding should be reviewed in a production implementation. Negative ES is possible when the entire relevant tail consists of gains. The examples use equal weights, not a weighted scenario distribution.

## 15. Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Possible cause | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| VaR sign is reversed | P&L treated as loss | Verify loss=−P&L |
| ES differs across tools | Different quantile or boundary convention | Align definitions and sample weights |
| No tail events | Event is rare or sample too small | Assess uncertainty and sample design |
| Stable estimate but implausible tail | Model misspecification | Review dependence and stress scenarios |
| VaR changes with horizon | Different risk period or scaling assumptions | Model and document the target horizon |
| Repeated paths | Duplicate random streams | Review seed and worker assignments |
