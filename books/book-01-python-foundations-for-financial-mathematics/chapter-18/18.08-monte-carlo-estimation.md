## Learning objectives

Define an estimand and estimator; estimate means and probabilities using simulated samples; calculate sample variance and standard error; interpret an approximate confidence interval; distinguish sampling error, bias and model risk; and validate results against analytical financial examples.

## 1. The estimation problem

Monte Carlo estimation approximates a model quantity using random samples. First define the **estimand**, the quantity of interest, such as the expected discounted payoff E[X] or the probability P(X<0). An **estimator** is the rule applied to a sample; its realized value is an **estimate**.

The calculation estimates a quantity under the specified model. A precisely estimated model value can still be inappropriate if the model, inputs or intended probability measure are wrong.

## 2. The sample mean

For N independent, identically distributed observations X₁,…,Xₙ with finite mean μ, use:

**X̄ = (1/N) Σ Xᵢ.**

The sample mean is unbiased for μ under these assumptions. With a finite mean, the law of large numbers supports convergence to μ as N increases. Individual estimates can move away from μ as the sample grows.

If Var(X)=σ² is finite and observations are independent, Var(X̄)=σ²/N and its standard deviation, the standard error, is σ/√N. Estimate it by **s/√N**, where s uses the sample variance with denominator N−1.

## 3. Worked discrete benchmark

Suppose terminal payoff X is 80, 100 or 120 with probabilities .25, .50 and .25. Then:

**E[X]=100**, and **Var(X)=.25(−20)²+.50(0)²+.25(20)²=200**.

For 10,000 independent observations the theoretical standard error of the mean is √200/√10000≈**0.14142**. This is the standard deviation of the estimator across repeated samples, not the standard deviation of individual outcomes, which is √200≈14.1421.

## 4. Sample variance

Given observed values, the unbiased sample variance is **s²=Σ(Xᵢ−X̄)²/(N−1)** for N≥2. For [80,100,120], the mean is 100, sample variance is 400 and sample standard deviation is 20. Its estimated standard error is 20/√3≈11.547.

The population variance in Section 3 and the sample variance in this example use different inputs and denominators; they should not be conflated.

## 5. Confidence intervals

When a normal approximation is appropriate for the sampling distribution, a nominal 95% interval for the mean is:

**X̄ ± 1.96 s/√N.**

For an estimate 100.1 and SE .14142, the interval is approximately [99.823,100.377]. The frequentist interpretation is that the procedure covers the fixed model mean in approximately 95% of repeated samples under its assumptions; the realized interval either covers that value or it does not.

This approximation may perform poorly for small samples, rare or highly skewed outcomes, or dependent draws. It does not cover model misspecification, parameter uncertainty or numerical bias automatically.

## 6. Probability estimation

For an event A, define Iᵢ=1 if A occurs and 0 otherwise. The estimator **p̂=ΣIᵢ/N** is the event frequency. For independent Bernoulli draws with true probability p, its standard error is √[p(1−p)/N]. A common plug-in estimate substitutes p̂.

For the discrete example, P(X<100)=.25. At N=10,000, theoretical SE is √(.25×.75/10000)≈**.00433**. The plug-in SE becomes zero if no event is observed; this does not prove p=0. For rare events or boundary counts use an appropriate binomial interval and investigate sample size.

## 7. Discounted payoff example

Suppose X in Section 3 is paid in one year and the specified annual effective discount rate is 5%. Define Y=X/1.05. Its exact model expectation is **100/1.05≈95.2381**, and its standard deviation is √200/1.05≈13.4687. For 10,000 independent draws, the theoretical SE of the discounted mean is approximately .13469.

The probabilities and discount rate must be appropriate for the intended purpose. A real-world expectation discounted at a chosen rate is not automatically an arbitrage-free price; risk-neutral valuation requires consistent assumptions.

## 8. Bias, precision and model risk

**Bias** is the difference between an estimator's expectation and the target. **Sampling variability** describes variation across samples. Mean squared error equals variance plus squared bias when these quantities exist.

More independent samples typically reduce the variance of a sample mean but do not eliminate a biased payoff approximation, wrong input distribution, time-step bias or implementation mistake. A narrow confidence interval can describe a precisely estimated wrong model.

## 9. Sample size planning

If a reasonable preliminary standard deviation estimate is σ and a normal approximation is appropriate, a target approximate 95% half-width h suggests **N≈(1.96σ/h)²**, rounded upward. With σ=√200 and h=.5, this gives approximately 3,074 paths.

This is a planning approximation, not a coverage guarantee. The preliminary σ may be inaccurate; tail behavior, dependence and stopping rules matter. Repeatedly checking an ordinary fixed-sample interval and stopping when it looks narrow can alter coverage; use a method designed for sequential stopping if needed.

## 10. Variance reduction and dependence

Antithetic sampling, control variates and common random numbers can improve efficiency in appropriate settings. They change the structure of observations, so uncertainty must be calculated for the resulting estimator, such as independent **pair averages** for antithetic pairs or paired differences for comparisons.

Using the same random stream accidentally across parallel workers can duplicate paths and invalidate an independence-based standard error. Use documented stream management and retain assignments. Different seeds alone do not prove independence.

## 11. Reproducibility and validation

Record the estimand, payoff, distribution, probability measure, seed, generator, sample count, software versions, code revision and tolerances. Verify against analytical benchmarks, test boundary cases and compare independent replications. A seed supports replay, not correctness.

A standard error describes sampling variability under stated assumptions; it is not a bound on the true market outcome or an investment-risk measure.

## 12. Reference implementation

Save as `monte_carlo_estimation.py`. The code samples the three-state payoff, reports a sample mean, standard error, nominal normal interval and loss frequency. It uses a local standard-library generator and is educational.

~~~python
import math
import random
import statistics
import unittest

def draw_payoffs(count, seed):
    if type(count) is not int or count < 2 or type(seed) is not int:
        raise ValueError("count >= 2 and integer seed required")
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    result = []
    for _ in range(count):
        u = rng.random()
        result.append(80.0 if u < .25 else (100.0 if u < .75 else 120.0))
    return result

def estimate_mean(values):
    vals = list(values)
    if len(vals) < 2 or any(type(x) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(x)
                            for x in vals):
        raise ValueError("at least two finite observations required")
    mean = statistics.mean(vals)
    sample_sd = statistics.stdev(vals)
    se = sample_sd / math.sqrt(len(vals))
    return {"count": len(vals), "mean": mean, "sample_sd": sample_sd,
            "standard_error": se, "normal_95_interval": (mean-1.96*se, mean+1.96*se)}

def event_frequency(values, threshold):
    vals = list(values)
    if not vals or type(threshold) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(threshold):
        raise ValueError("nonempty observations and finite threshold required")
    if any(type(x) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(x) for x in vals):
        raise ValueError("finite observations required")
    return sum(x < threshold for x in vals)/len(vals)

def discount(values, rate):
    if type(rate) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(rate) or rate <= -1:
        raise ValueError("finite annual effective rate greater than -1 required")
    vals = list(values)
    if any(type(x) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(x) for x in vals):
        raise ValueError("finite payoffs required")
    result = [x/(1+rate) for x in vals]
    if any(not math.isfinite(x) for x in result):
        raise ValueError("nonfinite discounted payoff")
    return result

class EstimationTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_replay(self):
        self.assertEqual(draw_payoffs(20,42),draw_payoffs(20,42))
    def test_support(self):
        self.assertTrue(all(x in (80,100,120) for x in draw_payoffs(100,1)))
    def test_known_mean(self):
        self.assertEqual(estimate_mean([80,100,120])["mean"],100)
    def test_known_sd(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(estimate_mean([80,100,120])["sample_sd"],20)
    def test_known_se(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(estimate_mean([80,100,120])["standard_error"],20/math.sqrt(3))
    def test_constant(self):
        self.assertEqual(estimate_mean([100,100])["standard_error"],0)
    def test_frequency(self):
        self.assertEqual(event_frequency([80,100,120,80],100),.5)
    def test_strict_threshold(self):
        self.assertEqual(event_frequency([100,100],100),0)
    def test_discount(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(discount([100],.05)[0],100/1.05)
    def test_bad_count(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): draw_payoffs(1,1)
    def test_bad_rate(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): discount([100],-1)
    def test_nonfinite(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): estimate_mean([1,float("nan")])

if __name__ == "__main__":
    payoffs = draw_payoffs(10000,42)
    print("terminal:",estimate_mean(payoffs))
    print("loss frequency:",event_frequency(payoffs,100))
    print("discounted:",estimate_mean(discount(payoffs,.05)))
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
~~~

The interval is a teaching example of a fixed-sample normal approximation; it is not a universal guarantee. The code does not calculate a binomial confidence interval or handle dependent samples.

## 15. Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Possible cause | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| Mean differs from 100 | Sampling variation or implementation error | Compare estimated SE and benchmark |
| Interval is narrow but result is implausible | Model or input bias | Review assumptions independently |
| Probability SE is zero after no events | Plug-in estimate at boundary | Use a suitable interval and review sample size |
| Repeated runs are identical unexpectedly | Duplicate seed or streams | Inspect generator and stream assignment |
| SE does not fall as expected | Dependence or changing distribution | Check independent units and estimator |
| Discounted result treated as market price | Measure and discount assumptions unclear | State purpose and valuation framework |
