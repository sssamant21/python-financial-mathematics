## 1. Finance is about uncertain future outcomes

The future value of an investment is generally unknown. Prices can rise or fall; borrowers may repay or default; interest rates can change; and an insurer may or may not pay a claim.

Probability provides a mathematical language for describing this uncertainty. It does **not** remove uncertainty or guarantee that the most likely outcome will occur.

A financial probability model specifies:

- **Outcomes:** Possible future states, returns, or cash flows.
- **Events:** Collections of outcomes, such as “the portfolio loses money.”
- **Probabilities:** Numbers from 0 to 1 that describe how likely the outcomes are under the model.
- **Payoffs:** Financial consequences associated with each outcome.

For a complete set of mutually exclusive outcomes, probabilities must sum to one.

## 2. A first financial scenario model

Consider a hypothetical one-period investment with the following dollar payoffs:

| Market state | Probability | Net payoff |
|---|---:|---:|
| Weak market | 25% | −$20 |
| Normal market | 50% | +$5 |
| Strong market | 25% | +$30 |

All three outcomes are mutually exclusive and exhaustive in this simplified model. Their probabilities sum to **0.25+0.50+0.25=1**.

The expected net payoff is:

**E[X] = 0.25(−20)+0.50(5)+0.25(30) = −5+2.5+7.5 = $5.**

This $5 is a probability-weighted average, **not** a guaranteed payment. None of the three outcomes actually pays exactly $5 in every realization.

The probability of a loss is **25%**, because only the weak-market outcome has a negative payoff.

## 3. Expected value and financial interpretation

For a discrete random payoff X with possible values x_i and probabilities p_i:

**E[X] = Σ p_i x_i.**

The expectation represents the probability-weighted center of the model's payoff distribution.

Expected value is useful for analyzing investment returns, insurance losses, credit losses, and project cash flows. But the expected payoff alone does not determine whether an investment is suitable: timing, liquidity, risk tolerance, taxes, fees, and extreme outcomes also matter.

### Example — Expected return

Suppose a stock's one-period return is +20% with probability 60% and −10% with probability 40%.

**E[R] = 0.6(0.20)+0.4(−0.10)=0.08=8%.**

This is an expected one-period **simple return** under the assumed probabilities. It is not a promised annual return, and it should not be compounded as though it were certain.

## 4. Measuring dispersion around expectation

Probability models also quantify how far outcomes may deviate from their mean.

**Var(X) = Σ p_i (x_i−E[X])².**

**SD(X) = sqrt(Var(X)).**

For the three-state dollar-payoff model, the mean is $5:

| Outcome | Deviation from $5 | Squared deviation | Weighted contribution |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| −$20 | −$25 | 625 | 156.25 |
| +$5 | $0 | 0 | 0 |
| +$30 | +$25 | 625 | 156.25 |

**Variance = 312.5 dollars²** and **standard deviation = sqrt(312.5) ≈ $17.6777**.

A positive expected payoff can therefore coexist with substantial uncertainty and loss probability.

## 5. Why probability matters across financial domains

### Investment returns and portfolio construction

Return scenarios help analysts estimate expected returns, dispersion, downside risk, and the potential benefits of diversification. Portfolio risk depends on the joint behavior of assets, not merely their standalone expected returns.

### Credit risk

A lender cares about the probability of default, exposure at default, and loss severity. A simplified expected credit loss is:

**Expected loss ≈ Probability of default × Loss given default × Exposure at default.**

For a $10,000 exposure, 2% default probability, and 40% loss given default:

**Expected loss ≈ 0.02×0.40×10,000 = $80.**

This illustrative formula does not capture timing, dependence, recoveries, or all accounting requirements.

### Insurance and risk pooling

Insurers use probability models to estimate claim frequency and severity. Pooling many exposures can reduce relative fluctuations under appropriate assumptions, but correlated catastrophes remain a major risk.

### Derivatives and contingent claims

An option's payoff depends on an uncertain underlying price. Pricing may use **risk-neutral probabilities**, which are not necessarily the same as real-world event probabilities. Expected payoff alone is not generally a valid derivative price: discounting, replication, market prices, and model assumptions matter.

### Capital budgeting and project analysis

A project may have optimistic, base, and adverse cash-flow scenarios. Probability-weighted cash flows can inform decisions, but decision makers must also consider the distribution of outcomes and the timing of cash flows.

## 6. Probability is not prediction certainty

A probability of 10% does not mean an event will happen exactly once in every ten observations. A 10% event can occur several times in a short sample or not at all.

Probabilities describe a model of uncertainty. Their usefulness depends on the quality of assumptions, data, and validation.

**Historical frequency** can inform probability estimates but does not guarantee that future probabilities remain constant. Financial markets change, and rare events may be underrepresented in historical data.

## 7. Real-world and risk-neutral probabilities

Two distinct probability concepts are important in finance:

- **Real-world (physical) probability:** A model for how often outcomes may occur in the actual economy.
- **Risk-neutral probability:** A mathematical pricing measure used in arbitrage-free valuation under specified assumptions.

Risk-neutral probabilities are calibrated to pricing relationships and do not automatically represent observed event frequencies. A later chapter can develop their mathematical foundations; this section uses real-world-style hypothetical scenario probabilities.

## 8. Conditional probability and dependence

Financial events are often connected. A borrower's default probability may rise during a recession; multiple assets may fall together in a market shock.

For events A and B with P(B)>0:

**P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B).**

If P(default and recession)=6% and P(recession)=20%, then:

**P(default | recession)=0.06/0.20=30%.**

This does not imply that the unconditional default probability is 30%.

Independence requires **P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)**. Never assume independence solely because risks belong to different companies or instruments.

## 9. Scenario analysis versus Monte Carlo simulation

**Scenario analysis** evaluates specified possible outcomes and their consequences. It is transparent and useful for stress testing.

**Monte Carlo simulation** repeatedly samples outcomes from a probability model to approximate distributions and statistics. It is useful when exact formulas are difficult or when multiple uncertainties interact.

Simulation is not evidence that the input probabilities are correct. Running more trials reduces sampling noise but does not fix a biased or incomplete model.

### Reproducibility

For teaching and testing, use a fixed pseudorandom seed so that the same inputs generate a repeatable sequence. A seed supports debugging and auditability; it does not make a model predictive.

```python
from math import fsum, isfinite
from random import Random
import unittest

def _validate_scenarios(scenarios):
    if not scenarios:
        raise ValueError("at least one scenario is required")
    pairs = []
    for probability, payoff in scenarios:
        if any(isinstance(x, bool) or not isinstance(x, (int, float)) or not isfinite(x)
               for x in (probability, payoff)):
            raise ValueError("finite numeric inputs required")
        if probability < 0 or probability > 1:
            raise ValueError("probability outside [0, 1]")
        pairs.append((float(probability), float(payoff)))
    if not abs(fsum(p for p, _ in pairs) - 1.0) <= 1e-12:
        raise ValueError("probabilities must sum to one")
    return pairs

def expected_payoff(scenarios):
    pairs = _validate_scenarios(scenarios)
    return fsum(p * x for p, x in pairs)

def outcome_variance(scenarios):
    pairs = _validate_scenarios(scenarios)
    mu = expected_payoff(pairs)
    return fsum(p * (x - mu)**2 for p, x in pairs)

def probability_of_loss(scenarios):
    pairs = _validate_scenarios(scenarios)
    return fsum(p for p, x in pairs if x < 0)

def expected_shortfall_below_zero(scenarios):
    pairs = _validate_scenarios(scenarios)
    return fsum(p * max(0.0, -x) for p, x in pairs)

def sample_scenarios(scenarios, count, seed=42):
    pairs = _validate_scenarios(scenarios)
    if isinstance(count, bool) or not isinstance(count, int) or count <= 0:
        raise ValueError("count must be a positive integer")
    if isinstance(seed, bool) or not isinstance(seed, int):
        raise ValueError("seed must be an integer")
    rng = Random(seed)
    weights = [p for p, _ in pairs]
    payoffs = [x for _, x in pairs]
    return rng.choices(payoffs, weights=weights, k=count)

def simulated_mean(scenarios, count=10000, seed=42):
    observations = sample_scenarios(scenarios, count, seed)
    return fsum(observations) / len(observations)

class ProbabilityFinanceTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def setUp(self):
        self.s = [(.25, -20), (.5, 5), (.25, 30)]
    def test_01_expectation(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(expected_payoff(self.s), 5)
    def test_02_variance(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(outcome_variance(self.s), 312.5)
    def test_03_loss_probability(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(probability_of_loss(self.s), .25)
    def test_04_expected_shortfall(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(expected_shortfall_below_zero(self.s), 5)
    def test_05_constant_payoff(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(outcome_variance([(1, 7)]), 0)
    def test_06_zero_probability(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(expected_payoff([(1, 8), (0, -999)]), 8)
    def test_07_negative_expectation(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(expected_payoff([(.5, -10), (.5, 4)]), -3)
    def test_08_reproducible(self):
        self.assertEqual(sample_scenarios(self.s, 30, 7),
                         sample_scenarios(self.s, 30, 7))
    def test_09_sample_count(self):
        self.assertEqual(len(sample_scenarios(self.s, 21)), 21)
    def test_10_simulation_approximation(self):
        self.assertLess(abs(simulated_mean(self.s, 50000, 42) - 5), .5)
    def test_11_invalid_probability(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError):
            expected_payoff([(.7, 1), (.4, 2)])
    def test_12_invalid_input(self):
        for fn in (lambda: expected_payoff([]),
                   lambda: expected_payoff([(1, float("nan"))]),
                   lambda: sample_scenarios(self.s, 0),
                   lambda: sample_scenarios(self.s, 5, True)):
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError):
                fn()

if __name__ == "__main__":
    scenarios = [(.25, -20), (.5, 5), (.25, 30)]
    print("Expected payoff:", expected_payoff(scenarios))
    print("Variance:", outcome_variance(scenarios))
    print("Probability of loss:", probability_of_loss(scenarios))
    print("Expected loss below zero:", expected_shortfall_below_zero(scenarios))
    print("Simulated mean:", simulated_mean(scenarios))
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
```

## 11. Twenty-two worked exercises

1. **Certain outcome.** A Treasury payment of $100 is modeled as certain in a simplified scenario. **Answer:** Probability 1, expected payment $100; real-world default, inflation, and settlement risks are excluded by assumption.

2. **Two-state expectation.** A stock returns +20% with probability .6 and −10% with probability .4. **Answer:** Expected simple return = .6(.20)+.4(−.10)=8%.

3. **Two-state loss probability.** Same stock. **Answer:** Probability of negative return = 40%.

4. **Two-state variance.** Same stock; mean 8%. **Answer:** Variance=.6(.20−.08)^2+.4(−.10−.08)^2=.0216; standard deviation≈14.6969%.

5. **Expected payoff.** A claim pays $100 with probability .3, otherwise $0. **Answer:** Expected payoff=$30.

6. **Fair price concept.** A zero-interest, risk-neutral simplified payoff has expectation $30. **Answer:** Its simplified expected-value price is $30; actual pricing requires discounting and risk adjustments.

7. **Probability complement.** Probability of default is 2%. **Answer:** Probability of no default is 98%.

8. **Mutually exclusive events.** Three market states have probabilities .2,.5,.3. **Answer:** They sum to 1 and form a valid discrete probability model if exhaustive.

9. **Invalid probabilities.** Scenarios have probabilities .6 and .6. **Answer:** Invalid: sum 1.2 exceeds 1.

10. **Expected portfolio loss.** Loss $1000 with probability .01; otherwise $0. **Answer:** Expected loss = $10.

11. **Tail loss.** A rare loss is −50% with probability .01 and return 0 otherwise. **Answer:** Probability of loss=1%; expected negative return contribution=−0.5%.

12. **Zero-mean risk.** Returns +10% and −10% with equal probability. **Answer:** Mean 0%, variance .01, volatility 10%.

13. **Diversification.** Two assets always move identically. **Answer:** Correlation 1; combining them does not remove common risk.

14. **Independent defaults.** Two independent loans default with probabilities .02 and .03. **Answer:** Both default with probability .02×.03=.0006=0.06%.

15. **Nonindependent defaults.** Two borrowers share a major employer. **Answer:** Independence may be inappropriate; common exposure can increase joint default risk.

16. **Conditional probability.** P(A∩B)=.06 and P(B)=.20. **Answer:** P(A|B)=.06/.20=.30.

17. **Event union.** P(A)=.3, P(B)=.4, P(A∩B)=.1. **Answer:** P(A∪B)=.3+.4−.1=.6.

18. **Expected value versus certainty.** An investment has expected payoff $100. **Answer:** That does not imply a guaranteed $100 payoff.

19. **Volatility of constant payoff.** An outcome is always $5. **Answer:** Variance and standard deviation are 0.

20. **Simulation frequency.** A scenario has probability .25. **Answer:** In 1000 trials the expected count is 250; actual count is random.

21. **Reproducibility.** Two Monte Carlo runs use the same algorithm, seed, and inputs. **Answer:** They should generate the same pseudorandom sequence in a fixed environment.

22. **Model risk.** Historical default frequency was 1%. **Answer:** It is not necessarily next year's true default probability; assumptions may change.

## 12. Applied mini-project — Financial scenario and loss monitor

You are asked to build a lightweight probability dashboard for a hypothetical investment committee.

### Input dataset

| Scenario | Probability | Portfolio net return |
|---|---:|---:|
| Severe downturn | 5% | −35% |
| Moderate downturn | 20% | −10% |
| Stable market | 50% | +5% |
| Strong rally | 25% | +18% |

### Tasks

1. Verify that the four probabilities sum to one.
2. Compute expected one-period return.
3. Compute return variance and standard deviation.
4. Compute the probability of a negative return.
5. Compute expected loss contribution below zero.
6. Simulate 10,000 outcomes using a fixed seed.
7. Compare simulated mean return with theoretical expected return.
8. Repeat with a different seed and explain sampling variability.
9. Introduce an invalid probability and verify rejection.
10. Explain why the model cannot guarantee future investment performance.

### Worked checkpoints

The expected return is:

**0.05(−0.35)+0.20(−0.10)+0.50(0.05)+0.25(0.18)**

**= −0.0175−0.0200+0.0250+0.0450 = 0.0325 = 3.25%.**

The probability of loss is:

**5%+20%=25%.**

The expected shortfall contribution below zero, measured as an unconditional positive loss amount, is:

**0.05(0.35)+0.20(0.10)=0.0375=3.75 percentage points.**

This **3.75%** is *not* expected shortfall (conditional value at risk) in its formal tail-risk sense; it is the unconditional expectation of the negative part of the return.

### Acceptance criteria

- Probabilities sum to one within a documented tolerance.
- The theoretical expected return is 3.25%.
- Loss probability is 25%.
- Unconditional expected negative-return contribution is 3.75%.
- The simulation uses a reproducible seed and a documented sample size.
- All 12 reference tests pass when executed.
- The report clearly distinguishes model assumptions from observed facts.

## 13. Operational and data-quality considerations

For production-oriented analytics:

- Record scenario definitions and their sources.
- Version probability estimates and stress assumptions.
- Validate probabilities, payoff units, and event exclusivity.
- Monitor drift in market regimes and historical calibration.
- Flag missing observations and stale scenario weights.
- Distinguish expected payoff, loss probability, and tail-loss metrics.
- Preserve reproducible simulation seeds and software versions.
- Avoid presenting scenario output as guaranteed investment results.
- Require independent review before using a model in consequential decisions.
