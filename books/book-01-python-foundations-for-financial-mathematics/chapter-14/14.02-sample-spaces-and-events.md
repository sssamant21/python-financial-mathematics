## Learning objectives

Define a sample space and event, distinguish elementary and compound outcomes, construct unions, intersections, complements, and partitions, and assign probabilities to finite financial scenarios. Implement set-based event analysis in Python and recognize limitations of discrete models.

## 1. What is a sample space?

A **sample space**, denoted Ω, is the collection of all possible outcomes of a specified random experiment.

For a hypothetical one-period equity investment, suppose the model allows three outcomes:

**Ω = {up, flat, down}.**

A sample space must be clearly defined and exhaustive **within the assumptions of the model**. A real asset has many possible prices; a three-state representation is a deliberately simplified approximation.

### Elementary versus compound outcomes

An elementary outcome is one particular result, such as *down*. If two assets each have outcomes up or down, the joint sample space is:

**Ω = {(up,up),(up,down),(down,up),(down,down)}.**

Each ordered pair is an elementary outcome of the **joint** experiment. The first component refers to asset A, the second to asset B.

## 2. What is an event?

An **event** is a subset of the sample space.

For Ω={up,flat,down}:

- **A = {up}**: the stock rises.
- **B = {flat,down}**: the stock does not rise.
- **C = {up,flat}**: the stock does not fall.

An event may contain one, several, all, or none of the possible outcomes.

The **certain event** is Ω itself. The **impossible event** is the empty set ∅.

## 3. Sample-space completeness and financial modeling

Before calculating probabilities, define exactly what each state represents.

Consider a credit-risk model that includes only **default** and **no default**. This is a valid binary sample space if the modeled question is whether a loan defaults during a specified horizon.

However, the same two-state model may be insufficient for estimating loss severity, because defaults can produce different recovery amounts. A richer model might include default with high recovery, default with low recovery, and no default.

The appropriate sample space depends on the **decision question**.

## 4. Union of events

The union **A ∪ B** includes outcomes belonging to A, B, or both.

For A={up,flat} and B={flat,down}:

**A ∪ B = {up,flat,down} = Ω.**

In finance, the union might represent “the market falls **or** a borrower defaults,” including scenarios where both happen.

## 5. Intersection of events

The intersection **A ∩ B** includes outcomes belonging to both A and B.

For A={up,flat} and B={flat,down}:

**A ∩ B = {flat}.**

For a two-loan credit model, the intersection of “loan A defaults” and “loan B defaults” is the event that **both** default.

## 6. Complement of an event

The complement **Aᶜ** includes all outcomes in Ω that are not in A.

For A={down}:

**Aᶜ = {up,flat}.**

If A represents a portfolio loss, Aᶜ represents no loss **under the specified outcome definitions**.

A fundamental identity is:

**P(Aᶜ)=1−P(A).**

## 7. Mutually exclusive and exhaustive events

Two events are **mutually exclusive** (disjoint) if they cannot occur together:

**A ∩ B = ∅.**

Events are **collectively exhaustive** if their union covers the sample space:

**A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ ... ∪ Aₙ = Ω.**

A **partition** consists of pairwise disjoint events whose union is Ω. The three states {up}, {flat}, and {down} form a partition of the three-state equity model.

Disjointness and independence are different concepts. Two nonempty disjoint events with positive probabilities cannot be independent.

## 8. Assigning probabilities to finite sample spaces

For a finite discrete sample space, each elementary outcome ω receives a probability p(ω) satisfying:

**0 ≤ p(ω) ≤ 1** and **Σω∈Ω p(ω)=1**.

The probability of event A is:

**P(A)=Σω∈A p(ω).**

### Worked example A — Three market states

| State | Probability |
|---|---:|
| Up | 50% |
| Flat | 20% |
| Down | 30% |

The probability of a decline is **30%**.

The probability of no decline is **50%+20%=70%**.

The probability of any market state in Ω is **100%**.

## 9. Event probability identities

For any events A and B:

**P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B).**

If A and B are disjoint, their intersection has probability zero, so:

**P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B).**

### Worked example B — Overlapping risk events

Suppose P(market decline)=40%, P(credit stress)=30%, and P(both)=10%.

Then:

**P(market decline or credit stress)=0.40+0.30−0.10=0.60=60%.**

Adding 40% and 30% without subtracting the overlap would double-count the 10% joint event.

## 10. Cartesian products and joint scenarios

If asset A has three states and asset B has two states, their joint sample space has:

**3×2=6 elementary outcomes.**

This is a counting fact, not an independence assumption. The probability of a joint outcome cannot generally be calculated by multiplying marginal probabilities unless independence or a suitable conditional probability model is justified.

### Worked example C — Two loans

Let S mean survive and D mean default:

**Ω={(S,S),(S,D),(D,S),(D,D)}.**

The event **exactly one default** is:

**E={(S,D),(D,S)}.**

The event **at least one default** is:

**F={(S,D),(D,S),(D,D)}.**

Their probabilities depend on the specified joint distribution.

## 11. Finite versus continuous sample spaces

A sample space need not be finite. A stock return may be modeled as a continuous quantity. In that case, an event might be:

**A={R:R<−0.10}**, representing a loss greater than 10%.

For continuous models, probabilities are typically computed using distributions or integrals rather than summing a short table of elementary outcomes. Later sections develop random variables and continuous distributions.

```python
from itertools import product
from math import fsum, isfinite
import unittest

def sample_space(*dimensions):
    if not dimensions or any(not d for d in dimensions):
        raise ValueError("at least one nonempty dimension required")
    return tuple(product(*dimensions))

def event(space, predicate):
    return frozenset(outcome for outcome in space if predicate(outcome))

def validate_distribution(weights):
    if not weights:
        raise ValueError("empty distribution")
    result = {}
    for outcome, probability in weights.items():
        if isinstance(probability, bool) or not isinstance(probability, (int,float)):
            raise ValueError("probability must be numeric")
        if not isfinite(probability) or not 0 <= probability <= 1:
            raise ValueError("probability outside [0,1]")
        result[outcome] = float(probability)
    if abs(fsum(result.values()) - 1) > 1e-12:
        raise ValueError("probabilities must sum to 1")
    return result

def probability(weights, outcomes):
    distribution = validate_distribution(weights)
    selected = frozenset(outcomes)
    if not selected.issubset(distribution):
        raise ValueError("event contains outcome outside sample space")
    return fsum(distribution[o] for o in selected)

def complement(space, selected):
    universe = frozenset(space)
    selected = frozenset(selected)
    if not selected.issubset(universe):
        raise ValueError("event outside sample space")
    return universe - selected

def union(a,b):
    return frozenset(a) | frozenset(b)

def intersection(a,b):
    return frozenset(a) & frozenset(b)

def disjoint(a,b):
    return not intersection(a,b)

class SampleSpaceTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def setUp(self):
        self.s = ("up","flat","down")
        self.p = {"up":.5,"flat":.2,"down":.3}
    def test_01_space(self):
        self.assertEqual(len(sample_space(("up","down"),("pay","default"))),4)
    def test_02_event(self):
        self.assertEqual(event(self.s,lambda x:x!="flat"),frozenset({"up","down"}))
    def test_03_singleton(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(probability(self.p,{"down"}),.3)
    def test_04_complement(self):
        self.assertEqual(complement(self.s,{"down"}),frozenset({"up","flat"}))
    def test_05_complement_probability(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(probability(self.p,complement(self.s,{"down"})),.7)
    def test_06_union(self):
        self.assertEqual(union({"up"},{"down"}),frozenset({"up","down"}))
    def test_07_intersection(self):
        self.assertEqual(intersection({"up","flat"},{"flat","down"}),frozenset({"flat"}))
    def test_08_disjoint(self):
        self.assertTrue(disjoint({"up"},{"down"}))
    def test_09_overlap(self):
        self.assertFalse(disjoint({"up","flat"},{"flat","down"}))
    def test_10_empty_event(self):
        self.assertEqual(probability(self.p,set()),0)
    def test_11_certain_event(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(probability(self.p,self.s),1)
    def test_12_invalid(self):
        for fn in (lambda:sample_space(),
                   lambda:validate_distribution({"a":.7,"b":.4}),
                   lambda:probability(self.p,{"missing"}),
                   lambda:complement(self.s,{"missing"})):
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError): fn()

if __name__ == "__main__":
    weights={"up":.5,"flat":.2,"down":.3}
    print("Sample space:",tuple(weights))
    print("Probability of loss:",probability(weights,{"down"}))
    print("Probability of no loss:",probability(weights,{"up","flat"}))
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
```

## 13. Twenty-four worked exercises

1. **Define a sample space:** One stock can rise, stay flat, or fall. **Worked answer:** Ω={up,flat,down}.

2. **Count states:** Two assets each have three possible states. **Worked answer:** 3×3=9 ordered joint outcomes.

3. **One-coin analogy:** A fair coin has heads and tails. **Worked answer:** Ω={H,T}; each outcome has probability 1/2.

4. **Event definition:** The event that the stock does not fall. **Worked answer:** A={up,flat}.

5. **Complement:** A={down} in Ω={up,flat,down}. **Worked answer:** Aᶜ={up,flat}.

6. **Empty event:** An event contains no possible outcomes. **Worked answer:** ∅ with probability 0.

7. **Certain event:** Every possible outcome is included. **Worked answer:** Ω with probability 1.

8. **Singleton event:** A market has outcomes up,flat,down. **Worked answer:** {down} is a singleton event.

9. **Union:** A={up,flat}, B={flat,down}. **Worked answer:** A∪B=Ω.

10. **Intersection:** Same A and B. **Worked answer:** A∩B={flat}.

11. **Disjoint events:** A={up}, B={down}. **Worked answer:** A∩B=∅; the events are disjoint.

12. **Overlapping events:** A={up,flat}, B={flat,down}. **Worked answer:** They overlap in {flat}.

13. **Event probability:** P(up)=.5, P(flat)=.2, P(down)=.3. **Worked answer:** P({up,flat})=.7.

14. **Loss probability:** Same probabilities; only down is a loss. **Worked answer:** P(loss)=.3.

15. **No-loss complement:** P(loss)=.3. **Worked answer:** P(no loss)=1−.3=.7.

16. **Union probability:** P(A)=.6, P(B)=.5, P(A∩B)=.2. **Worked answer:** P(A∪B)=.6+.5−.2=.9.

17. **Invalid distribution:** Weights .4,.4,.4. **Worked answer:** Invalid: sum 1.2.

18. **Two defaults:** Loan A and B each can default or survive. **Worked answer:** Ω={(S,S),(S,D),(D,S),(D,D)}.

19. **Exactly one default:** Use the two-loan sample space. **Worked answer:** {(S,D),(D,S)}.

20. **At least one default:** Use the two-loan sample space. **Worked answer:** {(S,D),(D,S),(D,D)}.

21. **Independence caveat:** Two borrowers share an employer. **Worked answer:** The joint sample space is valid, but product probabilities require an independence assumption.

22. **Finite partition:** A={up}, B={flat}, C={down}. **Worked answer:** They are pairwise disjoint and their union is Ω.

23. **Continuous outcomes:** A stock return can take any real value above −100% in a simplified long-only model. **Worked answer:** The sample space may be continuous rather than a short list of discrete states.

24. **Model completeness:** The sample space includes only up and down but the asset can be flat. **Worked answer:** Incomplete model; include flat or explicitly define why it is excluded.

## 14. Applied mini-project — Joint credit and market event monitor

A risk team models two binary dimensions: market state (**normal**, **stress**) and borrower status (**survive**, **default**).

The sample space is:

**Ω={(normal,survive),(normal,default),(stress,survive),(stress,default)}.**

Assume the following illustrative **joint** probabilities:

| Market | Borrower | Joint probability |
|---|---|---:|
| Normal | Survive | 72% |
| Normal | Default | 8% |
| Stress | Survive | 15% |
| Stress | Default | 5% |

### Tasks

1. Verify that the joint probabilities sum to one.
2. Define event A: market stress.
3. Define event B: borrower default.
4. Calculate P(A), P(B), and P(A∩B).
5. Calculate P(A∪B).
6. Calculate P(default | stress), explaining the denominator.
7. Test whether A and B are independent.
8. Implement the events in Python using tuples and sets.
9. Add an invalid state and verify validation rejects it.
10. Generate an auditable event report with definitions and source assumptions.

### Worked solution

**P(stress)=15%+5%=20%.**

**P(default)=8%+5%=13%.**

**P(stress and default)=5%.**

**P(stress or default)=20%+13%−5%=28%.**

**P(default | stress)=5%/20%=25%.**

If the events were independent, their intersection would have probability **0.20×0.13=2.6%**, but the actual joint probability is **5%**. Thus they are **not independent** in this model.

### Acceptance criteria

- Joint distribution validates to 100%.
- Event probabilities match the worked results.
- Union/intersection identities reconcile.
- Conditional probability uses a nonzero conditioning probability.
- Dependence is tested against the joint probability.
- The report distinguishes hypothetical model probabilities from measured real-world frequencies.
- All 12 reference tests pass when independently executed.

## 15. Common mistakes and production considerations

- **Incomplete sample spaces:** Missing outcomes can make probability totals misleading.
- **Double-counting overlaps:** Union probability requires subtraction of intersections.
- **Confusing disjointness with independence:** They describe different relationships.
- **Incorrect event horizon:** “Default this month” differs from “default this year.”
- **Inconsistent units:** A loss threshold of 10% is 0.10 in decimal-return calculations.
- **Assuming joint independence:** Shared economic shocks often induce dependence.
- **Invalid scenario weights:** Probabilities must be finite, nonnegative, and sum to one.
- **Missing provenance:** Financial models should record the source, date, and version of scenario definitions.

## 16. Completion and next section

Sample spaces describe what can happen; events specify which outcomes matter to a financial decision. Set operations allow complex financial risks to be expressed clearly, while probability assignments quantify those events under an explicit model.
