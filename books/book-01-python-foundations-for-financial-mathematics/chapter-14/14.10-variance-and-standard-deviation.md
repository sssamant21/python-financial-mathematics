## Learning objectives

Explain variance and standard deviation as measures of dispersion; calculate both for discrete and continuous random variables; apply transformation identities; distinguish population and sample variance; interpret volatility in finance; and build a reproducible Python risk-dispersion analyzer.

## 1. Why dispersion matters

Expected value summarizes the center of a financial distribution, but not the uncertainty around that center. Two investments can have identical expected returns while differing dramatically in downside exposure.

**Variance** measures expected squared deviation from the mean. **Standard deviation** is its square root and has the same units as the underlying variable.

These measures are useful but incomplete: they treat upside and downside deviations symmetrically and may not adequately describe skewness, heavy tails, liquidity, or rare catastrophic losses.

## 2. Population variance

For a random variable X with finite second moment and μ=E[X]:

**Var(X)=E[(X−μ)²].**

For discrete X:

**Var(X)=Σ_x (x−μ)²P(X=x).**

For a variable with density f_X:

**Var(X)=∫₋∞^∞(x−μ)²f_X(x)dx.**

Variance is nonnegative. It is zero exactly when X is almost surely constant.

## 3. Worked investment example

Consider three possible net dollar payoffs:

| State | Probability | Payoff |
|---|---:|---:|
| Bear | 25% | −$20 |
| Base | 50% | +$5 |
| Bull | 25% | +$30 |

First compute the expected payoff:

**μ=.25(−20)+.50(5)+.25(30)=$5.**

Then compute squared deviations:

| Payoff | Deviation from μ | Squared deviation | Weighted contribution |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| −20 | −25 | 625 | 156.25 |
| 5 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 30 | 25 | 625 | 156.25 |

Therefore:

**Var(X)=156.25+0+156.25=312.5 dollars².**

**SD(X)=√312.5≈$17.6777.**

The standard deviation describes dispersion, not a guaranteed error bound or a loss threshold.

## 4. Computational shortcut

When E[X²] is finite:

**Var(X)=E[X²]−(E[X])².**

For the payoff example:

**E[X²]=.25(400)+.50(25)+.25(900)=337.5.**

Thus:

**Var(X)=337.5−25=312.5.**

The shortcut is algebraically correct, but directly accumulating centered squared deviations is often more numerically stable when observations are large relative to their spread.

## 5. Properties under transformations

For constants a and b:

**Var(aX+b)=a²Var(X).**

Consequently:

**SD(aX+b)=|a|SD(X).**

Adding a constant shifts the mean but does not change variance.

Doubling the payoff scale multiplies variance by four and standard deviation by two.

### Worked example A

Let Y=2X+10.

**E[Y]=2(5)+10=20.**

**Var(Y)=4(312.5)=1250.**

**SD(Y)=2√312.5≈35.3553.**

## 6. Variance of sums and covariance

For random variables X and Y with finite variances:

**Var(X+Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y)+2Cov(X,Y).**

For a difference:

**Var(X−Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y)−2Cov(X,Y).**

If X and Y are independent, their covariance is zero and:

**Var(X+Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y).**

The converse is not generally true: zero covariance does not prove independence.

### Worked example B — Shared risk

Suppose Var(X)=4, Var(Y)=9, and Cov(X,Y)=2.

**Var(X+Y)=4+9+2(2)=17.**

Ignoring covariance would incorrectly report 13.

This matters for portfolios, correlated credit losses, and operational failures sharing a common cause.

## 7. Portfolio volatility

For two asset returns R₁ and R₂ with weights w₁,w₂:

**Var(R_p)=w₁²σ₁²+w₂²σ₂²+2w₁w₂Cov(R₁,R₂).**

Equivalently, if standard deviations are positive and correlation ρ is defined:

**Var(R_p)=w₁²σ₁²+w₂²σ₂²+2w₁w₂ρσ₁σ₂.**

Portfolio volatility is the square root of portfolio variance. Diversification benefits depend on covariance, not merely the number of holdings.

## 8. Bernoulli and binomial variance

For a Bernoulli event indicator D with event probability p:

**Var(D)=p(1−p).**

For X~Binomial(n,p) under independent, identical Bernoulli trials:

**Var(X)=np(1−p).**

For n=10 and p=.1:

**Var(X)=10(.1)(.9)=.9 defaults².**

If defaults are correlated, the binomial variance formula is generally inappropriate.

## 9. Population versus sample variance

For a finite observed population x₁,...,x_N, the population variance is:

**σ²=(1/N)Σᵢ(xᵢ−μ)²**, where μ is the population mean.

For a sample x₁,...,x_n, the conventional unbiased estimator under independent, identically distributed sampling with finite variance is:

**s²=(1/(n−1))Σᵢ(xᵢ−x̄)²**, n≥2.

### Worked example C

Observations: 1,2,3.

Mean=2. Sum of squared deviations=2.

Population variance=2/3.

Sample variance=2/(3−1)=1.

The choice of denominator depends on whether the observations constitute the full population or a sample from a broader distribution.

## 10. Units and annualization

If daily returns are expressed as decimals, daily standard deviation is also expressed in decimal-return units. A daily SD of .02 corresponds to 2 percentage points.

A common approximate annualization formula is:

**σ_annual≈σ_daily√252**,

assuming an appropriate trading-day convention and sufficiently stable, uncorrelated daily returns. Autocorrelation, volatility clustering, irregular observations, and regime shifts can invalidate this approximation.

Do not annualize a one-day variance by multiplying the standard deviation by 252; the square-root scaling is the conventional independent-increment approximation.

## 11. Risk interpretation and limitations

Standard deviation measures dispersion on both sides of the mean. A high positive surprise increases standard deviation just as a large negative surprise does.

Risk decisions should often supplement volatility with:

- Downside deviation and semivariance.
- Quantile-based Value at Risk and Expected Shortfall.
- Maximum drawdown, liquidity constraints, and stress scenarios.
- Tail dependence and concentration analysis.
- Backtesting and model calibration.

These concepts are introduced here as context; later chapters develop their calculations.

## 12. Numerical reliability

Validate probabilities and values before computing moments. Reject nonfinite inputs. Use stable summation, such as **math.fsum**, when possible.

For empirical data, a two-pass variance algorithm is often preferable to subtracting two large, nearly equal moments. For streaming datasets, numerically stable online algorithms such as Welford's method are useful.

Store whether the reported number is a theoretical distribution variance, population descriptive variance, or sample variance estimate.

```python
from math import fsum, isfinite, sqrt
import unittest

def validate(pmf):
    if not pmf: raise ValueError("empty distribution")
    if any(isinstance(x,bool) or not isinstance(x,(int,float)) or not isfinite(x) for x in pmf):
        raise ValueError("nonfinite outcome")
    if any(isinstance(p,bool) or not isinstance(p,(int,float)) or not isfinite(p) or p<0 or p>1 for p in pmf.values()):
        raise ValueError("invalid probability")
    if abs(fsum(pmf.values())-1)>1e-12: raise ValueError("probabilities must sum to one")
    return dict(pmf)

def mean(pmf):
    d=validate(pmf)
    return fsum(x*p for x,p in d.items())

def variance(pmf):
    d=validate(pmf)
    mu=mean(d)
    return fsum(p*(x-mu)**2 for x,p in d.items())

def standard_deviation(pmf):
    return sqrt(variance(pmf))

def transformed(pmf,a,b):
    d=validate(pmf)
    if any(isinstance(v,bool) or not isinstance(v,(int,float)) or not isfinite(v) for v in (a,b)):
        raise ValueError("invalid transform")
    out={}
    for x,p in d.items():
        y=a*x+b
        if not isfinite(y): raise ValueError("nonfinite transformed outcome")
        out[y]=out.get(y,0.0)+p
    return out

def sample_variance(values):
    v=list(values)
    if len(v)<2 or any(isinstance(x,bool) or not isinstance(x,(int,float)) or not isfinite(x) for x in v):
        raise ValueError("at least two finite observations required")
    mu=fsum(v)/len(v)
    return fsum((x-mu)**2 for x in v)/(len(v)-1)

def population_variance(values):
    v=list(values)
    if not v or any(isinstance(x,bool) or not isinstance(x,(int,float)) or not isfinite(x) for x in v):
        raise ValueError("finite observations required")
    mu=fsum(v)/len(v)
    return fsum((x-mu)**2 for x in v)/len(v)

class VarianceTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def setUp(self): self.d={-20:.25,5:.5,30:.25}
    def test_01_mean(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(mean(self.d),5)
    def test_02_variance(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(variance(self.d),312.5)
    def test_03_standard_deviation(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(standard_deviation(self.d),sqrt(312.5))
    def test_04_constant(self): self.assertEqual(variance({7:1}),0)
    def test_05_shift(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(variance(transformed(self.d,1,10)),312.5)
    def test_06_scale(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(variance(transformed(self.d,2,0)),1250)
    def test_07_negative_scale(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(variance(transformed(self.d,-1,0)),312.5)
    def test_08_sample_variance(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(sample_variance([1,2,3]),1)
    def test_09_population_variance(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(population_variance([1,2,3]),2/3)
    def test_10_bernoulli(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(variance({0:.8,1:.2}),.16)
    def test_11_single_population(self): self.assertEqual(population_variance([5]),0)
    def test_12_invalid(self):
        for f in (lambda:variance({0:.7,1:.4}),
                  lambda:sample_variance([1]),
                  lambda:population_variance([]),
                  lambda:transformed(self.d,float("nan"),0)):
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError): f()

if __name__=="__main__":
    result=unittest.TextTestRunner(verbosity=2).run(
        unittest.defaultTestLoader.loadTestsFromTestCase(VarianceTests))
    if not result.wasSuccessful(): raise SystemExit(1)
```

## 14. Twenty-four worked exercises

1. **Three-state mean:** Payoffs −20,5,30 with weights .25,.5,.25. **Solution:** Mean=5.

2. **Three-state variance:** Same PMF. **Solution:** Variance=312.5.

3. **Standard deviation:** Variance=312.5. **Solution:** SD≈17.6777.

4. **Squared payoff moment:** Same PMF. **Solution:** E[X²]=337.5.

5. **Shortcut formula:** E[X²]=337.5, E[X]=5. **Solution:** Var(X)=337.5−25=312.5.

6. **Constant variable:** X=8 surely. **Solution:** Variance=0.

7. **Add constant:** Y=X+10. **Solution:** Var(Y)=Var(X)=312.5.

8. **Double payoff:** Y=2X. **Solution:** Var(Y)=4(312.5)=1250.

9. **Negative scaling:** Y=−X. **Solution:** Var(Y)=312.5.

10. **Standard deviation scaling:** Y=2X. **Solution:** SD(Y)=2SD(X)≈35.3553.

11. **Bernoulli default:** p=.2. **Solution:** Var(D)=.2(.8)=.16.

12. **Binomial defaults:** n=10,p=.1 independent trials. **Solution:** Var(N)=.9.

13. **Population variance:** Data 1,2,3 treated as population. **Solution:** 2/3.

14. **Sample variance:** Data 1,2,3 treated as sample. **Solution:** 1.

15. **Degrees of freedom:** Sample size n=5. **Solution:** Unbiased sample variance divides by n−1=4 under iid finite-variance sampling.

16. **Risk units:** Returns expressed as decimals. **Solution:** Variance in squared decimal-return units.

17. **Percentage conversion:** SD=.02 in decimal returns. **Solution:** SD=2 percentage points.

18. **Two-point distribution:** X=0 or 10 equally likely. **Solution:** E[X]=5; Var(X)=25.

19. **Risk comparison:** Two assets each have E[R]=5%, with SDs 2% and 15%. **Solution:** Second has greater return dispersion under these summaries.

20. **Portfolio covariance:** Var(X)=4,Var(Y)=9,Cov(X,Y)=2. **Solution:** Var(X+Y)=4+9+4=17.

21. **Independent sum:** Var(X)=4,Var(Y)=9, independent. **Solution:** Var(X+Y)=13.

22. **Perfect negative relation:** Y=−X. **Solution:** Var(X+Y)=0.

23. **Volatility caveat:** Returns have fat tails. **Solution:** Standard deviation alone may understate extreme-loss risk.

24. **Validation:** Probabilities sum to 1.2. **Solution:** Reject distribution before computing variance.

## 16. Troubleshooting and operational controls

| Symptom | Likely cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Negative variance from a shortcut | Cancellation or calculation error | Use centered two-pass calculation |
| SD reported in squared units | Confusion between variance and SD | Take square root and correct units |
| Wrong sample estimate | Used n instead of n−1 | Identify population versus sample |
| Portfolio variance too low | Covariance ignored | Include cross terms |
| Annualized volatility too high | Multiplied SD by 252 | Review square-root-of-time assumptions |
| Historical volatility shifts | Regime change or data-quality issue | Inspect windows and backtest |
| Tail losses exceed expectations | SD alone misses tail behavior | Add stress and downside metrics |
