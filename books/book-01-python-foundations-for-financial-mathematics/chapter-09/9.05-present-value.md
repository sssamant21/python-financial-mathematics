## Reverse the accumulation calculation

Section 9.4 began with a principal P and calculated its amount F after n periods at a constant effective rate i:

```math
F=P(1+i)^n.
```

When F is known and the question asks for its equivalent at time 0, divide by the same positive accumulation factor:

```math
\boxed{PV=\frac{F}{(1+i)^n}}.
```

Present value is an amount at a specified valuation date, not a separate payment. The California Board of Equalization describes the present-worth factor as the reciprocal of the future-worth factor [1]. The derivation above follows directly by solving the accumulation equation for P.

Use one currency, one deterministic payment, a constant effective periodic rate, and a whole number of matching periods. Assume no fees, taxes, default, or intervening payments. The rate is supplied by the model author; calculating PV does not establish which rate is appropriate for a real asset.

## An original worked example

A synthetic payment of 5,000 illustrative USD will occur at the end of year 3. At an assumed effective annual rate of 4%,

```math
PV=\frac{5000}{1.04^3}
=\frac{5000}{1.124864}
\approx4444.981793354574.
```

Display the answer as **4,444.98 USD today**. Keep the unrounded value for subsequent calculations. Growing that value forward three years at the same 4% recovers 5,000 within floating-point tolerance.

| Item | Amount or assumption | Date or unit |
|---|---|---|
| Valuation origin | Time 0 | Today |
| Future receipt F | +5,000 USD | End of year 3 |
| Effective rate i | .04 | Per year |
| Term n | 3 | Whole years |
| Equivalent PV | Approximately +4,444.981793 USD | Time 0 |

No cash is received today merely because the model reports a positive PV. If an investor actually bought the claim at that model-equivalent amount, the purchase would be a negative time-0 payment and the future receipt positive. The present-value calculation alone does not execute or establish that purchase.

## Why subtracting interest is wrong

Multiplying F by 1−ni does not invert compound accumulation. In the example, 5,000(1−.04×3)=4,400, which grows to 4,949.4016 at 4% for three years, not 5,000.

Even under a simple-interest contract, reversing F=P(1+rt) gives P=F/(1+rt), rather than F(1−rt). A quoted simple discount rate applied to a future face amount would be another convention. Do not substitute it for an effective compound rate just because both calculations reduce an amount.

## Compare alternatives at the same date

Consider receipt A of 4,500 today and receipt B of 5,000 at year 3. Apply the same assumptions and 4% effective annual rate:

| Alternative | Equivalent today, USD | Equivalent at year 3, USD |
|---|---:|---:|
| A: 4,500 today | 4,500.00 | 5,061.888 |
| B: 5,000 at year 3 | 4,444.981793… | 5,000.000 |

A has the larger equivalent amount at either date. Its time-0 advantage is approximately 55.018207 USD. The year-3 advantage is 61.888 USD; these differences have different valuation dates and should be labeled accordingly.

This ranking is conditional on the supplied rate and comparable payment assumptions. A larger model PV does not resolve differences in payment uncertainty, access to funds, tax treatment, currency, or contractual restrictions. Such differences require explicit modeling. Do not select a rate solely to obtain a desired ranking.

## Rate, time, and sign effects

For a fixed positive future payment and n>0, increasing i within i>−1 decreases PV because it increases the accumulation factor. For a fixed positive i and positive payment, increasing n decreases PV. These statements hold the future amount constant; they do not compare balances that themselves change with the horizon.

At n=0, PV=F. At i=0, PV=F for any allowed n. For −1<i<0 and n>0, PV exceeds F when F is positive. For instance, discounting 1,000 for one period at −2% gives 1,000/.98≈1,020.408163. Thus a discount factor below one requires a positive rate and positive term; it is not a universal property of all allowed inputs. The positive-rate interpretation in reference [1] is extended here with these explicit mathematical boundaries.

A future obligation has a negative signed payment from the payer's viewpoint. Its PV remains negative because the accumulation factor is positive. Reversing the viewpoint reverses the sign. Increasing a positive discount rate makes the PV of a fixed negative payment less negative: the monotonic statement for positive receipts must not be copied without its sign qualification.

## Match the period and the rate

A 4% effective annual rate with n=3 means three years. An effective monthly rate requires a term in months. A nominal annual quote compounded monthly needs conversion to its periodic rate; it cannot be inserted unchanged as an effective annual or monthly input. Sections 9.10 and 9.11 develop those distinctions.

The lab accepts whole periods only. Fractional periods, irregular dates, day-count rules, changing rates, and term structures require explicit extensions. This section discounts one payment; Sections 9.8 and 9.9 will combine multiple payments and net present value. It does not infer a market yield curve from the constant input rate.

Financial discounting also differs from deflating a nominal amount for inflation. The former establishes financial equivalence under a selected valuation rate; the latter expresses purchasing power in another date's price units. Use consistent nominal or real assumptions rather than applying the two operations interchangeably.

The function preserves a signed payment, accepts a finite effective periodic rate greater than −1, and requires nonnegative integer periods. Booleans and nonfinite inputs are rejected. Its return value is a signed equivalent amount at time 0, rather than an automatically negated purchase price.

This introductory function calculates the accumulation factor directly. It rejects unsupported intermediate overflow, complete underflow, and nonfinite or zero output for a nonzero payment. It can reject cases where another numerical arrangement could return a representable result. Very small rates may be lost in 1+i, and nonzero subnormal outputs may have reduced relative precision. The checks do not guarantee arbitrary-range accuracy or exact monetary settlement. Use explicit precision and contractual rounding policies for broader requirements. The library reference documents the finite-value checks used here [2].

```python
"""Signed single-payment PV under a constant effective periodic rate."""
import math
from numbers import Real
import unittest


def finite_real(value, name):
    if isinstance(value, bool) or not isinstance(value, Real):
        raise TypeError(f'{name} must be real, not bool')
    try:
        result = float(value)
    except OverflowError as exc:
        raise ValueError(f'{name} outside float range') from exc
    if not math.isfinite(result):
        raise ValueError(f'{name} must be finite')
    return result


def present_value(payment, rate, periods):
    """Discount one signed payment; periods count matched whole intervals."""
    f=finite_real(payment,'payment'); r=finite_real(rate,'rate')
    if r <= -1:
        raise ValueError('rate must exceed -1')
    if isinstance(periods,bool) or not isinstance(periods,int):
        raise TypeError('periods must be an integer, not bool')
    if periods < 0:
        raise ValueError('periods must be nonnegative')
    if f == 0 or r == 0 or periods == 0:
        return f
    try:
        accumulation=(1+r)**periods
    except OverflowError as exc:
        raise ValueError('accumulation outside supported float range') from exc
    if not math.isfinite(accumulation) or accumulation <= 0:
        raise ValueError('accumulation outside supported float range')
    value=f/accumulation
    if not math.isfinite(value) or value == 0:
        raise ValueError('present value outside supported float range')
    return value


class PresentValueTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_01_worked_value(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(present_value(5000,.04,3),4444.981793354574)

    def test_02_forward_reverse(self):
        for rate in [.04,0,-.02]:
            value=present_value(5000,rate,3)
            self.assertAlmostEqual(value*(1+rate)**3,5000)

    def test_03_zero_boundaries(self):
        self.assertEqual(present_value(5000,.04,0),5000)
        self.assertEqual(present_value(5000,0,50),5000)
        self.assertEqual(present_value(0,.04,3),0)

    def test_04_rate_and_time_ordering(self):
        self.assertGreater(present_value(5000,.02,3),present_value(5000,.06,3))
        self.assertGreater(present_value(5000,.04,1),present_value(5000,.04,3))
        self.assertGreater(present_value(5000,-.02,3),5000)

    def test_05_signed_payments(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(present_value(-5000,.04,3),-present_value(5000,.04,3))
        self.assertAlmostEqual(present_value(10000,.04,3),2*present_value(5000,.04,3))

    def test_06_common_date_comparison(self):
        today=4500; later=present_value(5000,.04,3)
        self.assertGreater(today,later)
        self.assertGreater(today*1.04**3,5000)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(today-later,55.018206645426)

    def test_07_invalid_inputs(self):
        for args in [(True,.04,3),(5000,'0.04',3),(5000,.04,True),(5000,.04,3.5)]:
            with self.assertRaises(TypeError): present_value(*args)
        for args in [(float('nan'),.04,3),(5000,float('inf'),3),(5000,-1,3),
                     (5000,-1.1,3),(5000,.04,-1),(10**1000,.04,3)]:
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError): present_value(*args)

    def test_08_numerical_limits_and_staged_discount(self):
        for args in [(1,1,2000),(1,-.5,2000),(5e-324,1,1),(1e308,-.5,2)]:
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError): present_value(*args)
        staged=present_value(present_value(5000,.04,2),.04,1)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(staged,present_value(5000,.04,3))


if __name__=='__main__':
    print(f'5000 in three years at 4% effective annually: PV = {present_value(5000,.04,3):.2f}')
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
```

Expected demonstration output:

```text

```

## Practice with answers

1. A payment of 1,210 is due in two years. Discount it at 10% effective annually. **Answer:** PV=1,000; compounding this amount recovers 1,210.
