## Money has a date

An offer of 1,000 today and an offer of 1,080 in two years are different dated claims. Comparing 1,080 with 1,000 alone leaves out the opportunity to use the earlier money during those two years. Time value calculations establish a common valuation date before comparing the amounts. The California Board of Equalization introduces this common-date principle in its time value of money training [1].

The calculation requires assumptions. In this section, use one currency, deterministic payments, a constant effective rate per period, and a nonnegative whole number of periods. Assume no fees, taxes, interim deposits, withdrawals, or payment default. The same accumulation rule applies throughout the comparison. These assumptions define an illustrative equivalence model, not a recommendation or a promise that an investment will earn the supplied rate.

A positive rate makes earlier availability valuable within this model. At zero rate, equal amounts at different dates are mathematically equivalent. At a negative rate greater than −100% per period, a positive balance shrinks as time advances. Therefore, “money today is always worth more” is too broad without specifying the rate and comparison assumptions.

## A minimal dated-payment model

Let P be an amount at time 0, i the effective rate for one period, and n the number of those periods. For this introductory compound model, define the accumulation factor

```math
a(i,n)=(1+i)^n.
```

The factor has no currency unit. Multiplying P by it preserves the currency unit while moving the valuation date forward:

```math
F=P\,a(i,n).
```

Conversely, a payment F at time n has the time-0 equivalent

```math
P=\frac{F}{a(i,n)}.
```

These equations preview the dedicated present-value, future-value, and compounding sections that follow. Here their purpose is to make dated equivalence concrete. Multiplication and division use the same factor; discounting does not mean subtracting n times the interest rate.

If a period is a year, i must be an effective annual rate. If a period is a month, i must be an effective monthly rate. A nominal annual quote cannot be inserted as a monthly rate. Rate conversion is developed later in the chapter. Calendar dates, partial periods, and contractual day-count rules are outside this opening model.

## Compare the two offers at one date

Suppose the illustrative opportunity rate is 5% effective annually. The following are original comparison alternatives; they are not market quotations.

| Alternative | Amount received | Receipt time | Equivalent at time 0 | Equivalent at year 2 |
|---|---:|---|---:|---:|
| A | 1,000.00 | Today | 1,000.00 | 1,102.50 |
| B | 1,080.00 | End of year 2 | 979.59 | 1,080.00 |

For A, the year-2 equivalent is 1,000 × 1.05² = 1,102.50. For B, the time-0 equivalent is 1,080 / 1.05² ≈ 979.5918367. Under the stated assumptions, A has the larger equivalent amount at either common date. The comparison is internally consistent because the positive common accumulation factor preserves ordering.

Receiving B later is not inherently better just because its printed amount is larger. Nor does this calculation decide a real choice when credit quality, liquidity restrictions, currency, tax treatment, or payment uncertainty differ. Those differences require additional modeling rather than silently changing the meaning of i.

Keep full precision during the calculation and round only for display. The table’s 979.59 is a displayed approximation, not the input used to reverse the calculation.

## Equivalence is not an extra cash flow

“1,000 today is equivalent to 1,102.50 in two years at 5%” describes two representations of value. It does not say that an account receives both amounts. A lender’s initial cash outflow would carry a negative sign and a later receipt a positive sign. Reversing the viewpoint reverses both signs. The Python helper below preserves the sign of the amount it translates; it does not infer a full loan contract or create an offsetting payment.

The model has useful consistency checks:

- At n = 0, the accumulation factor is one.
- At i = 0, the accumulation factor is one for every allowed n.
- Moving a value forward and then backward with the same rate and term recovers it within numerical tolerance.
- Splitting a horizon into m and n periods gives a(i,m+n) = a(i,m)a(i,n) when the rate remains constant.

These checks reveal more than comparing only one displayed answer with a calculator.

## Discounting and inflation answer different questions

An assumed opportunity rate translates dated monetary value. An inflation adjustment translates purchasing-power units. They need not use the same percentage.

For an explicitly separate synthetic assumption of 2% price inflation over one year, a nominal year-end balance of 1,050 has purchasing power of 1,050/1.02 ≈ 1,029.41 in today’s currency purchasing-power units. This is not its time-0 financial value discounted at 5%, which is 1,000. Nominal cash flows and real cash flows must be paired with consistent valuation assumptions; the two adjustments should not be substituted casually.

The helper accepts finite real numeric amounts and rates, excludes booleans, requires a built-in integer term, and restricts i > −1. Signed amounts are supported. Numerical overflow, zero accumulation caused by underflow, nonfinite output, and total loss of a nonzero translated amount are rejected. This is an introductory float implementation: it does not implement decimal settlement rounding, arbitrary precision, fractional periods, or a production rate convention. Subnormal results can still have reduced relative precision even when nonzero.

```python
"""Single-payment equivalence under an explicitly supplied periodic rate."""
import math
from numbers import Real
import unittest


def finite_real(value, name):
    if isinstance(value, bool) or not isinstance(value, Real):
        raise TypeError(f'{name} must be a real number, not bool')
    result = float(value)
    if not math.isfinite(result):
        raise ValueError(f'{name} must be finite')
    return result


def accumulation(rate, periods):
    rate = finite_real(rate, 'rate')
    if rate <= -1:
        raise ValueError('rate must exceed -1')
    if isinstance(periods, bool) or not isinstance(periods, int):
        raise TypeError('periods must be an integer, not bool')
    if periods < 0:
        raise ValueError('periods must be nonnegative')
    try:
        factor = (1 + rate) ** periods
    except OverflowError as exc:
        raise ValueError('accumulation overflow') from exc
    if not math.isfinite(factor) or factor <= 0:
        raise ValueError('accumulation outside supported float range')
    return factor


def equivalent(amount, rate, periods, *, to_future):
    amount = finite_real(amount, 'amount')
    if not isinstance(to_future, bool):
        raise TypeError('to_future must be bool')
    factor = accumulation(rate, periods)
    result = amount * factor if to_future else amount / factor
    if not math.isfinite(result) or (amount != 0 and result == 0):
        raise ValueError('equivalent amount outside supported float range')
    return result


class TVMIntroductionTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_01_one_period_equivalence(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(equivalent(1000, .05, 1, to_future=True), 1050)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(equivalent(1050, .05, 1, to_future=False), 1000)

    def test_02_common_date_comparison(self):
        later_today = equivalent(1080, .05, 2, to_future=False)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(later_today, 979.5918367346939)
        self.assertGreater(1000, later_today)
        self.assertGreater(equivalent(1000, .05, 2, to_future=True), 1080)

    def test_03_round_trip_and_composition(self):
        future = equivalent(1234.56, .03, 7, to_future=True)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(equivalent(future, .03, 7, to_future=False), 1234.56)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(accumulation(.03, 2)*accumulation(.03, 5), accumulation(.03, 7))

    def test_04_zero_time_and_zero_rate(self):
        self.assertEqual(equivalent(1000, .05, 0, to_future=True), 1000)
        self.assertEqual(equivalent(1000, 0, 20, to_future=False), 1000)

    def test_05_negative_rate_and_signed_amount(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(equivalent(1000, -.02, 1, to_future=True), 980)
        self.assertGreater(equivalent(1000, -.02, 1, to_future=False), 1000)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(equivalent(-1000, .05, 1, to_future=True), -1050)

    def test_06_invalid_inputs(self):
        for bad in [True, '0.05', None]:
            with self.assertRaises(TypeError): accumulation(bad, 2)
        for bad in [float('nan'), float('inf'), -1, -1.2]:
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError): accumulation(bad, 2)
        for bad in [True, 2.5, '2']:
            with self.assertRaises(TypeError): accumulation(.05, bad)
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): accumulation(.05, -1)
        with self.assertRaises(TypeError): equivalent(1, .05, 1, to_future=1)
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): equivalent(float('nan'), .05, 1, to_future=True)

    def test_07_float_range_failures(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): accumulation(1, 2000)
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): accumulation(-.5, 2000)
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): equivalent(1e308, 1, 2, to_future=True)
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): equivalent(5e-324, 1, 1, to_future=False)

    def test_08_purchasing_power_is_separate(self):
        nominal_future = equivalent(1000, .05, 1, to_future=True)
        real_in_today_units = nominal_future / 1.02
        self.assertAlmostEqual(real_in_today_units, 1029.4117647058824)
        self.assertNotAlmostEqual(real_in_today_units, 1000)


if __name__ == '__main__':
    print(f'1000 today -> {equivalent(1000, .05, 2, to_future=True):.2f} in two years')
    print(f'1080 in two years -> {equivalent(1080, .05, 2, to_future=False):.2f} today')
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
```

Expected demonstration output:

```text

```
