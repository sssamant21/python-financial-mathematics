## Learning objectives

Solve for the number of periods or elapsed years needed to reach a target balance; use logarithms to invert periodic, effective annual, nominal, and continuous growth models; identify impossible and non-unique solutions; distinguish mathematical fractional periods from contractual schedules; and verify solutions with Python.

## Solving the compound-interest equation

For a nonzero initial balance \(PV\), final balance \(FV\), constant periodic rate \(i\), and \(n\) periods,

\[
FV=PV(1+i)^n.
\]

If \(1+i>0\) and \(FV/PV>0\), divide by \(PV\) and take natural logarithms:

\[
\ln(FV/PV)=n\ln(1+i).
\]

Provided \(i\neq0\), the unique real solution is

\[
\boxed{n=\frac{\ln(FV/PV)}{\ln(1+i)}}.
\]

This \(n\) is measured in **compounding periods**, not automatically years. If \(i>0\), a higher positive target requires positive time. If \(-1<i<0\), a lower positive target requires positive time. A rate and target moving in opposite directions imply negative mathematical time and are rejected when the task requires a future horizon.

## Worked example: reaching a growth target

Suppose an illustrative 1,000 USD balance grows at 10% effective annually and the target is 1,210 USD:

\[
t=\frac{\ln(1210/1000)}{\ln(1.10)}
=\frac{\ln(1.21)}{\ln(1.10)}=2\text{ years}.
\]

The inverse check is \(1000(1.10)^2=1210\). The result depends on the assumed fixed 10% annual effective rate and excludes fees, contributions, and withdrawals.

## Doubling time

For a target \(FV=2PV\) and a positive effective annual rate \(e\),

\[
\boxed{t_{\text{double}}=\frac{\ln 2}{\ln(1+e)}}.
\]

At 7% effective annually, doubling takes approximately 10.245 years under the constant-growth model. This is a mathematical interpolation. If interest is credited only at complete annual dates, the balance first meets or exceeds the target after **11 complete credits**, not at a fractional posting date. Always distinguish the continuous-time equivalent from the first contractual payment or posting date.

The Rule of 72 (\(72\) divided by a percentage rate) is a rough mnemonic, not an identity and not a substitute for the logarithmic calculation.

## Time under different rate conventions

**Effective annual rate** \(e>-1\), \(e\neq0\):

\[
\boxed{t=\frac{\ln(FV/PV)}{\ln(1+e)}}.
\]

**Nominal annual rate** \(j\), convertible \(m\) times a year:

\[
\boxed{t=\frac{\ln(FV/PV)}{m\ln(1+j/m)}}.
\]

Here \(m\) is a positive integer and \(1+j/m>0\). The answer is in years, but \(mt\) may be fractional. A schedule that credits only complete periods requires an explicit rounding or first-crossing policy.

**Continuously compounded annual rate** \(r_c\neq0\):

\[
\boxed{t=\frac{\ln(FV/PV)}{r_c}}.
\]

For example, 100 USD becoming \(100e^{0.18}\) USD at 6% continuously compounded annually takes \(0.18/0.06=3\) years.

## Negative rates and declining balances

A negative rate between \(-100\%\) and 0% per period can produce a declining positive balance. At \(-10\%\) effective annually, 100 becomes 81 in two years:

\[
t=\frac{\ln(0.81)}{\ln(0.90)}=2.
\]

The same formulas work for negative signed balances when the initial and final balances share a sign, although a negative balance may represent a liability rather than an investment.

## No-solution and non-unique cases

When the growth rate is **zero**, the accumulation factor is one at every time. If \(FV=PV\), every nonnegative time satisfies the equation, so elapsed time is **not uniquely identifiable**. If \(FV\neq PV\), the target is impossible under zero growth.

If the rate is positive and the target is smaller than the initial balance, or the rate is negative and the target is larger, no nonnegative time solves the constant-rate model. A zero balance or opposite-sign balances cannot be modeled by a strictly positive accumulation factor. These conditions should raise explicit errors rather than produce misleading output.

## Numerical precision and model assumptions

For small periodic rates, compute \(\ln(1+i)\) with `math.log1p(i)` instead of `math.log(1+i)` to avoid losing significant digits. Compute the log of the absolute balances separately to avoid intermediate overflow in \(FV/PV\); this can still lose precision when balances are nearly equal, so demanding high-precision finance requires a defined numeric policy.

Calendar dates, business days, leap years, actual/360 versus actual/365, compounding cutoffs, and posting dates require a separate scheduling layer. A fractional real-valued solution is not automatically an executable contract term.

```python
"""Solve time from constant-rate growth, with explicit feasibility checks."""
import math
from numbers import Real
import unittest

def finite(x, label):
    if isinstance(x, bool) or not isinstance(x, Real):
        raise TypeError(f"{label} must be a real number")
    try:
        x = float(x)
    except OverflowError as exc:
        raise ValueError(f"{label} exceeds float range") from exc
    if not math.isfinite(x):
        raise ValueError(f"{label} must be finite")
    return x

def log_ratio(present, future):
    p = finite(present, "present")
    f = finite(future, "future")
    if p == 0 or f == 0 or (p > 0) != (f > 0):
        raise ValueError("balances must be nonzero and have the same sign")
    # Difference of logarithms avoids overflow in f / p.
    return math.log(abs(f)) - math.log(abs(p))

def time_from_log_growth(log_growth, log_factor):
    if log_factor == 0:
        if log_growth == 0:
            raise ValueError("any time fits; time is not uniquely identifiable")
        raise ValueError("target cannot be reached at zero growth")
    time = log_growth / log_factor
    if not math.isfinite(time):
        raise ValueError("time exceeds float range")
    if time < 0:
        raise ValueError("target is unreachable at nonnegative time")
    return time

def continuous_years(present, future, continuous_rate):
    r = finite(continuous_rate, "continuous annual rate")
    return time_from_log_growth(log_ratio(present, future), r)

def effective_years(present, future, effective_annual_rate):
    e = finite(effective_annual_rate, "effective annual rate")
    if e <= -1:
        raise ValueError("annual growth factor must be positive")
    return time_from_log_growth(log_ratio(present, future), math.log1p(e))

def periodic_periods(present, future, periodic_rate):
    i = finite(periodic_rate, "periodic rate")
    if i <= -1:
        raise ValueError("periodic growth factor must be positive")
    return time_from_log_growth(log_ratio(present, future), math.log1p(i))

def nominal_years(present, future, nominal_rate, frequency):
    j = finite(nominal_rate, "nominal annual rate")
    if isinstance(frequency, bool) or not isinstance(frequency, int):
        raise TypeError("frequency must be an integer")
    if frequency <= 0:
        raise ValueError("frequency must be positive")
    p = j / frequency
    if j != 0 and p == 0:
        raise ValueError("periodic rate underflow")
    if p <= -1:
        raise ValueError("periodic growth factor must be positive")
    return time_from_log_growth(log_ratio(present, future),
                                frequency * math.log1p(p))

class TimeTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_01_periodic_doubling(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(periodic_periods(100, 121, .1), 2)
    def test_02_effective_years(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(effective_years(1000, 1210, .1), 2)
    def test_03_continuous(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(continuous_years(100, 100*math.exp(.06*3), .06), 3)
    def test_04_nominal_monthly(self):
        f = 1000*(1+.06/12)**24
        self.assertAlmostEqual(nominal_years(1000, f, .06, 12), 2)
    def test_05_decline(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(effective_years(100, 81, -.1), 2)
    def test_06_same_balance(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(effective_years(100, 100, .04), 0)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(continuous_years(-100, -100, -.04), 0)
    def test_07_unreachable_or_indeterminate(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError):
            effective_years(100, 110, -.03)
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError):
            effective_years(100, 100, 0)
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError):
            effective_years(100, 110, 0)
    def test_08_bad_inputs(self):
        for args in ((0, 110, .1), (100, -110, .1), (100, 110, -1),
                     (100, float("nan"), .1), (True, 110, .1)):
            with self.assertRaises((TypeError, ValueError)):
                effective_years(*args)
        for bad in (0, -2, 12.0, True):
            with self.assertRaises((TypeError, ValueError)):
                nominal_years(100, 110, .06, bad)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"Time to grow 1000 to 1210 at 10% EAR: "
          f"{effective_years(1000, 1210, .10):.6f} years")
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
```

## Exercises with answers

1. **Two annual periods:** 500 becomes 605 at 10% effective annually. **Answer:** \(\ln(605/500)/\ln(1.10)=2\) years.
2. **Doubling:** How long does a balance take to double at 8% effective annually? **Answer:** \(\ln 2/\ln 1.08\approx9.0065\) years.
3. **Continuous:** A balance grows by a factor \(e^{0.15}\) at 5% continuously compounded. **Answer:** \(0.15/0.05=3\) years.
4. **Monthly:** At 1% per month, how many modeled periods to grow by 10%? **Answer:** \(\ln(1.10)/\ln(1.01)\approx9.579\) periods; first full monthly posting meeting the target is period 10.
5. **Decline:** How long does 100 take to become 81 at \(-10\%\) effective annually? **Answer:** 2 years.
6. **Zero rate:** At 0%, when does 100 become 120? **Answer:** Never under the model. When does 100 remain 100? **Answer:** Every nonnegative time; no unique solution.
