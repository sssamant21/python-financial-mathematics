## 1.1.1 Financial Mathematics: The Core Idea

Financial mathematics applies mathematical reasoning to problems involving money, time, cash flows, interest rates, uncertainty, valuation, and financial decisions.

Typical questions include what an investment will be worth in the future, what a future payment is worth today, how cash flows should be valued, how loans and bonds are analyzed, and how financial returns and risks can be measured.

The basic workflow is:

**Financial Problem → Mathematical Model → Calculation → Financial Interpretation**

## 1.1.2 Money Has a Time Dimension

One of the foundational ideas in finance is the time value of money. A dollar available today and a dollar available later are generally not economically equivalent when money can earn a return over time.

Suppose `PV = 1000` and money earns an effective annual rate `r = 0.05` for one year. Then:

`FV = PV(1 + r) = 1000(1.05) = 1050`

This introduces three essential dimensions: **money + time + rate**.

## 1.1.3 Present Value and Future Value

For a single amount compounded at a constant effective rate per period:

`FV = PV(1 + r)^n`

where `PV` is present value, `FV` is future value, `r` is the effective rate per compounding period, and `n` is the number of those periods.

Solving for present value:

`PV = FV / (1 + r)^n`

Compounding moves value forward through time; discounting moves future value back toward a valuation date.

## 1.1.4 Compounding

With `PV = 1000`, `r = 0.05`, and `n = 3`:

`FV = 1000(1.05)^3 = 1157.625`

Period by period:

`1000 → 1050 → 1102.50 → 1157.625`

The accumulated value becomes the base for subsequent growth.

## 1.1.5 Discounting

If `FV = 10000` five years from now and the effective annual discount rate is `0.06`:

`PV = 10000 / (1.06)^5 ≈ 7472.58`

Discounting allows amounts occurring at different times to be compared on a common valuation date.

## 1.1.6 Cash Flows

A cash flow is an amount associated with a point or interval in time. A simplified timeline might contain an initial investment of `-1000` followed by inflows of `300`, `400`, and `500`.

Under a simplified constant-rate discrete-compounding model, a general present-value structure is:

`PV = Σ CF_t / (1 + r)^t`

This idea leads naturally to net present value, bond valuation, annuities, project valuation, and discounted cash-flow analysis.

## 1.1.7 Interest Rates Are Part of the Model

A number such as `0.05` is incomplete without context. It could represent an annual effective rate, monthly effective rate, nominal quoted rate, continuously compounded rate, discount rate, yield, or another convention.

**Rate = numerical value + time basis + convention + financial meaning.**

## 1.1.8 Simple and Compound Interest

Under a simplified simple-interest model:

`FV = PV(1 + rn)`

Under periodic compound interest:

`FV = PV(1 + r)^n`

For `PV = 1000`, `r = 0.05`, and `n = 10`, simple interest gives `1500`, while annual compounding gives approximately `1628.89`.

## 1.1.9 Annuities

Many financial contracts contain repeated cash flows, including loans, mortgages, pensions, savings contributions, and coupon-like payment streams.

For an ordinary annuity with payment `C`, effective rate `r` per period, and `n` payments, a common present-value formula is:

`PV = C(1 - (1 + r)^(-n)) / r`, for `r != 0`.

Later sections derive this from discounted cash flows rather than treating it as a formula to memorize.

## 1.1.10 Loans and Amortization

A loan can be viewed as an initial cash flow plus future repayment cash flows. Mathematical models can determine periodic payments, outstanding balances, interest portions, principal portions, and amortization schedules.

## 1.1.11 Bonds

A basic fixed-coupon bond can be modeled as coupons plus principal repayment. A simplified valuation framework is:

`P = Σ C_t/(1 + y)^t + F/(1 + y)^n`

where `P` is bond value, `C_t` is coupon cash flow, `F` is face/redemption value, `y` is the appropriate yield or discount rate per modeled period, and `n` is the number of periods.

The underlying idea is that value is the present value of future cash flows under the chosen model.

## 1.1.12 Investment Returns

A simple holding-period return with no intermediate cash flows is:

`R = (V1 - V0) / V0`

If `V0 = 100` and `V1 = 108`, then `R = 0.08 = 8%`.

Real return calculations become more involved when dividends, deposits, withdrawals, timing differences, and multiple periods are introduced.

## 1.1.13 Risk and Uncertainty

Future prices, returns, defaults, and rates may be uncertain. Financial mathematics therefore extends into probability and statistics.

If a payoff `X` can take values `x_i` with probabilities `p_i`, then:

`E[X] = Σ p_i x_i`, with `p_i >= 0` and `Σ p_i = 1`.

Financial mathematics therefore combines **time + value + uncertainty**.

## 1.1.14 Probability and Statistics

Later sections use random variables, distributions, expected returns, variance, standard deviation, covariance, correlation, estimation, simulation, and risk measures.

For a random return `R`, variance is:

`Var(R) = E[(R - E[R])^2]`.

## 1.1.15 Portfolio Mathematics

For portfolio weights `w_i` and asset returns `R_i`:

`R_p = Σ w_i R_i`.

Under a simple fully invested formulation, `Σ w_i = 1`. Portfolio risk also depends on how asset returns move together, leading to covariance matrices, diversification, portfolio variance, and optimization.

## 1.1.16 Derivatives and Contingent Claims

Options, forwards, futures, and swaps derive value from other assets or financial variables. For example, a European call option payoff at maturity is:

`max(S_T - K, 0)`.

This introduces state-dependent payoffs, uncertainty, valuation, probability, and discounting.

## 1.1.17 Financial Mathematics Is Not Formula Memorization

A disciplined calculation asks what the financial question is, which quantities are known and unknown, what units and rate conventions apply, when cash flows occur, what assumptions the model makes, whether uncertainty is involved, which numerical method is appropriate, and how results will be validated.

**Financial mathematics is not formula substitution alone.**

## 1.1.18 Mathematical Models Are Simplifications

A future-value model may assume a constant rate, known compounding periods, no fees, no taxes, no defaults, and no intermediate withdrawals. Mathematical correctness must therefore be combined with appropriate assumptions.

## 1.1.19 Why Computation Becomes Necessary

Manual calculation works for small examples. Realistic problems may contain many assets, thousands of cash flows, or millions of simulation paths.

**Financial mathematics + programming = scalable quantitative analysis.**

## 1.1.20 Where Python Fits

Python provides built-in numeric types and standard-library numerical facilities. A simple implementation might be:

```python
principal = 1_000.0
rate_per_period = 0.05
periods = 3

future_value = principal * (1 + rate_per_period) ** periods
print(future_value)
```

The key translation is **financial concept → mathematical model → computational representation**.

## 1.1.21 Financial Mathematics and Numerical Precision

Computer representations do not encode every mathematical number exactly in every numeric format. Financial applications may require careful choices involving binary floating point, decimal arithmetic, rounding, tolerances, currency precision, and numerical stability.

We therefore distinguish mathematical value, computer representation, displayed value, and financial rounding policy.

## 1.1.22 Workflow Used Throughout This Series

The standard workflow is:

1. Define the financial problem.
2. Define variables and units.
3. Select the mathematical model.
4. State assumptions.
5. Implement the model in Python.
6. Validate the implementation.
7. Interpret the result financially.

**Problem → Variables → Model → Assumptions → Python → Validation → Interpretation**

## 1.1.23 Example — From Financial Question to Model

Suppose an investor places `$5,000` into an account earning an effective annual rate of `4%` for five years with annual compounding and no intermediate cash flows.

`PV = 5000`, `r = 0.04`, `n = 5`.

`FV = 5000(1.04)^5 ≈ 6083.26`.

Under the stated assumptions, the investment grows to approximately `$6,083.26` after five years. The assumptions are part of the answer; the number alone is incomplete.

## 1.1.24 Key Takeaways

Financial mathematics applies mathematical reasoning to financial quantities, cash flows, time, rates, uncertainty, valuation, and decisions. Time value of money, present/future value, compounding, discounting, cash-flow timing, rate conventions, probability, portfolio mathematics, and model assumptions form the foundation for later material. Programming becomes valuable as calculations scale, and numerical representation must be handled deliberately.

The central workflow is:

**Financial Problem → Mathematical Model → Computational Model → Validation → Financial Interpretation**
