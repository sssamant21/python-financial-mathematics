## 1. Purpose

A return time-series chart preserves observation order. A histogram groups observed returns into numerical intervals and shows how often values fall into those intervals. It helps inspect concentration, spread, and the supplied sample's extremes, but it loses the original sequence.

This section constructs histograms from an explicitly defined synthetic return sample. It distinguishes counts, sample shares, and density; makes bin boundaries reviewable; and accounts for unknown observations without turning them into zeros. The complete lab separates numerical binning from plotting so that counts and normalization can be checked independently of the exported picture.

The example describes a sample. It does not fit a probability model, forecast future returns, or establish a tail-risk estimate.

## 3. Specify a Comparable Return Sample

A histogram requires a numerical sample, but financial comparability requires more context. State the return definition, observation horizon, instrument or portfolio, reporting window, adjustment basis, and exclusion policy before interpreting its shape.

Do not silently pool one-day, one-month, and irregular-gap returns into one distribution. Their numerical magnitudes refer to different exposure intervals. Likewise, simple price returns, log returns, and total returns are different measurements even when stored in arrays of the same shape.

The lab receives already-derived synthetic **simple price returns of one common horizon**. It does not infer or verify that horizon from timestamps because none are supplied. A real pipeline must establish the metadata and calculation lineage upstream. These returns are decimal fractions: `0.01` means 1%.

The sample slots are:

 0.02,  0.03,  0.04,  0.10, unknown, unknown

There are 13 declared slots, 11 observed returns, and 2 unknowns. The two zero values are observed unchanged outcomes, not missing values. The list is arranged for inspection; it does not claim a particular historical time order.

## 4. Bin Edges Define the Question

A histogram partitions the numerical return axis. NumPy accepts explicit increasing bin edges [1]. With edges:

```python
edges = np.array([-0.10, -0.02, 0.00, 0.02, 0.10])
```

the bins use this convention: each bin includes its left edge and excludes its right edge, except the last bin, which also includes the final right edge [1]. For the fixture:

| Return interval | Included observed values | Count |
|---|---|---:|
| [-10%, -2%) | -8%, -4% | 2 |
| [-2%, 0%) | -2%, -1% | 2 |
| [0%, 2%) | 0%, 0%, 1% | 3 |
| [2%, 10%] | 2%, 3%, 4%, 10% | 4 |

The exact -2% observation belongs to the second bin. The exact zero observations belong to the third. The final +10% observation is included in the final bin. A boundary rule should be tested, especially when observations lie exactly on selected edges.

Increasing edges alone do not guarantee that all data are counted. NumPy can exclude observations outside a supplied histogram range [1]. This lab deliberately rejects edges that fail to cover any observed return. It does not normalize a truncated subset silently.

For a production report that intentionally focuses on a restricted range, disclose the excluded tail counts and the normalization denominator. Zooming the display, clipping data, and selecting histogram support are different operations.

## 5. Counts, Sample Shares, and Density

Let $N$ be the number of observed included returns, $n_k$ the count in bin $k$, and $w_k$ its width in decimal-return units.

The three common height conventions are:

$$\text{count}_k=n_k,\qquad \text{share}_k=\frac{n_k}{N},\qquad
\text{density}_k=\frac{n_k}{Nw_k}.$$

They obey different normalization checks:

$$\sum_k n_k=N,\qquad \sum_k\text{share}_k=1,\qquad
\sum_k\text{density}_k\,w_k=1.$$

Density height is not a probability or a percentage of observations. Its **area over a bin** equals that bin's observed-sample share. Heights can exceed one without violating normalization.

For the unequal-width fixture bins:

| Bin | Width, decimal return | Count | Observed-sample share | Density per unit decimal return |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| [-10%, -2%) | 0.08 | 2 | 2/11 | 2.272727… |
| [-2%, 0%) | 0.02 | 2 | 2/11 | 9.090909… |
| [0%, 2%) | 0.02 | 3 | 3/11 | 13.636364… |
| [2%, 10%] | 0.08 | 4 | 4/11 | 4.545455… |

The first two bins have equal counts and shares. The narrower second bin has four times the density height because the same sample share occupies one quarter of the numerical width.

For the third bin, `13.636364 * 0.02` is approximately `0.272727`, or 27.27% of the observed sample. Adding the four density heights is not the normalization check. Multiply by the corresponding widths and then sum.

## 6. Percentage Labels Do Not Change Density Units

The implementation stores returns and edges as decimal fractions. A percentage tick formatter labels `0.02` as 2%, but it does not rescale the underlying x coordinates or the density values.

The density axis is therefore labeled **per unit decimal return**. If values and edges were instead multiplied by 100 and represented in percentage-point units, the numerical bin widths would be 100 times larger and the corresponding density heights 100 times smaller. The sample shares and areas would be unchanged.

Do not label the density y-axis “probability (%)” merely because the horizontal ticks show percentages. A count chart has counts on its y-axis; a density chart has density units; a bin-share chart would need its own explicit share convention.

## 7. Missingness and Denominators

The helper excludes NaNs from the observed sample and records how many were excluded. It retains genuine zeros and rejects infinity as an invalid value. Infinity is not reclassified as missing just to permit plotting.

The empirical negative fraction among observed returns is:

$$\frac{4}{11}\approx36.36\%.$$

That is an observed-sample proportion. It is not `4/13`, because the two unknown slots have no known signs. It is not a forecast probability of a loss in the next interval. Missing observations may be related to market conditions or pipeline behavior, so exclusion can alter the visible sample in a systematic way.

If every slot is missing, there is no observed distribution to normalize. The helper requires at least two observed values. That is a minimal teaching-data contract, not a claim that two values provide a statistically reliable distribution. An all-zero observed sample is accepted when explicit covering bin edges are supplied; it is concentrated in the zero-containing bin rather than treated as missing.

The input slots here already represent intended return observations. A leading undefined return created from a price baseline should be distinguished upstream from missing expected intervals, as discussed in Section 8.5. The histogram helper cannot reconstruct that distinction from an unlabeled numeric array.

## 8. Bin Choice Changes the Visible Shape

The figure uses the same observed sample twice:

- The left panel uses equal-width edges `[-10%, -5%, 0%, 5%, 10%]` and count heights `[1, 3, 6, 1]`.
- The right panel uses unequal-width edges `[-10%, -2%, 0%, 2%, 10%]` and density heights derived from counts `[2, 2, 3, 4]`.

These panels illustrate different binning and normalization choices. Their y-values are not directly comparable. The titles and units identify the difference.

Equal-width count bins make height comparisons straightforward. With unequal widths, density is useful because area, rather than raw height, represents sample share. Wider bins can contain more values simply because they span more possible outcomes.

Automatic rules can provide candidate edges. NumPy's `histogram_bin_edges` supports strategies such as `auto` and `fd` [3]. For example:

```python
candidate_edges = np.histogram_bin_edges(observed, bins="auto")
```

Here `observed` means an already validated finite sample; this illustrative call is not the lab's edge policy. An automatic rule does not establish the true number of modes or the correct financial support. Inspect sensitivity to reasonable alternative edges and retain the final edges in reproducibility records.

When comparing two assets or reporting windows, use common bin edges and compatible return definitions. Count heights also reflect sample size, so comparing counts from different-sized samples can be misleading. Relative shares or densities address size normalization, while coverage and selection differences still require disclosure.

## 9. What a Histogram Cannot Recover

Permuting a sample leaves its histogram unchanged. It can nevertheless change the sequence of gains and losses, interim peaks, drawdowns, and the experience of holding an investment. The tests demonstrate histogram permutation invariance, not equivalence of financial paths.

A histogram also summarizes values within bins. It does not identify every individual return or resolve the exact location of an observation inside a bin. The source sample remains necessary for exact calculations, quantiles, threshold counts, and data investigation.

A roughly bell-shaped display does not establish a normal probability model. Bin choices and small samples can alter apparent symmetry, gaps, and modes. A fitted curve would introduce a model and an estimation method; the lab fits none. An empty extreme bin means that this sample contains no observed value there, not that such an outcome is impossible.

The fixture is too small and deliberately constructed to support empirical market conclusions. There is no Value at Risk, expected shortfall, confidence interval, dependence estimate, or annualization calculation here. Those topics need additional definitions, assumptions, and validation.

### `return_histograms.py`

```python
"""Explicit histogram edges, missingness accounting, and density normalization."""
from pathlib import Path
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use('Agg')
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.ticker import PercentFormatter, MaxNLocator
import numpy as np


def fixture():
    # Already-derived, same-horizon synthetic simple price returns; two unknowns.
    return np.array([-.08, -.04, -.02, -.01, 0., 0., .01,
                     .02, .03, .04, .10, np.nan, np.nan])


def summarize(returns, edges):
    if (not isinstance(returns, np.ndarray) or returns.ndim != 1
            or returns.dtype.kind not in 'iuf' or not 2 <= len(returns) <= 10000):
        raise ValueError('returns must be a 1-D real numeric array with 2..10000 slots')
    values = returns.astype(float, copy=True)
    if np.isinf(values).any():
        raise ValueError('infinite returns are invalid, not missing')
    observed = values[~np.isnan(values)]
    if len(observed) < 2 or np.any(observed < -1) or np.any(observed > 10):
        raise ValueError('need at least two observed simple returns in [-1, 10]')
    if (not isinstance(edges, np.ndarray) or edges.ndim != 1
            or edges.dtype.kind not in 'iuf' or not 2 <= len(edges) <= 101):
        raise ValueError('edges must be a 1-D real numeric array with 2..101 entries')
    bins = edges.astype(float, copy=True)
    if (not np.isfinite(bins).all() or np.any(np.diff(bins) < 1e-6)
            or bins[0] < -1 or bins[-1] > 10):
        raise ValueError('edges must be finite, within [-1,10], with widths >= 1e-6')
    if observed.min() < bins[0] or observed.max() > bins[-1]:
        raise ValueError('edges must cover every observed return; no silent tail exclusion')
    counts, _ = np.histogram(observed, bins=bins)
    mass = counts / observed.size
    density = mass / np.diff(bins)
    return {'observed': observed, 'edges': bins, 'counts': counts,
            'mass': mass, 'density': density, 'total': len(values),
            'missing': int(np.isnan(values).sum())}


def build_figure(returns):
    count_edges = np.array([-.10, -.05, 0., .05, .10])
    density_edges = np.array([-.10, -.02, 0., .02, .10])
    a = summarize(returns, count_edges)
    b = summarize(returns, density_edges)
    fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 5), layout='constrained')
    axes[0].stairs(a['counts'], a['edges'], fill=True,
                   color='#0072B2', edgecolor='black', alpha=0.75)
    axes[0].set(title='Equal-width bins | height = count', ylabel='Observed intervals')
    axes[0].yaxis.set_major_locator(MaxNLocator(integer=True))
    axes[1].stairs(b['density'], b['edges'], fill=True,
                   color='#D55E00', edgecolor='black', alpha=0.75)
    axes[1].set(title='Unequal-width bins | area = sample share',
                ylabel='Density per unit decimal return')
    for ax in axes:
        ax.set(xlabel='Simple price return', ylim=(0, None), xlim=(-.10, .10))
        ax.xaxis.set_major_formatter(PercentFormatter(xmax=1.0, decimals=0))
        ax.set_xticks([-.10, -.05, 0, .05, .10])
        ax.grid(axis='y', alpha=0.2)
    fig.suptitle(f'Synthetic same-horizon returns | observed {len(a["observed"])}/{a["total"]}; '
                 f'unknown {a["missing"]}', fontsize=12)
    fig.supxlabel('Unknowns excluded and counted; zero returns retained. No fitted distribution or forecast.',
                  fontsize=9)
    return fig, axes


def main():
    returns = fixture()
    result = summarize(returns, np.array([-.10, -.02, 0., .02, .10]))
    print(f'Slots: {result["total"]}; observed: {len(result["observed"])}; missing: {result["missing"]}')
    print(f'Unequal-bin counts: {result["counts"].tolist()}')
    print(f'Sample shares sum: {result["mass"].sum():.6f}')
    area = np.sum(result['density'] * np.diff(result['edges']))
    print(f'Density area: {area:.6f}')
    print(f'Observed negative fraction: {np.mean(result["observed"] < 0):.2%}')
    fig, _ = build_figure(returns)
    try:
        Path('output').mkdir(exist_ok=True)
        fig.savefig('output/return_histograms.png', dpi=150)
    finally:
        plt.close(fig)
    print('Saved output/return_histograms.png')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
```

The histogram is computed once for each declared edge array. Matplotlib's `stairs` draws the precomputed heights across those edges [2]. This separates the count/density calculation from the presentation and gives tests direct access to both.

Each panel has a zero-based y-axis. Integer tick selection is used on the count axis. The density panel retains its numerical density scale, while percentage formatting applies only to the return x-axis. The caller owns the returned Figure and saves and closes it explicitly. Re-running the demonstration replaces the PNG.

## 11. Reference Tests

### `test_return_histograms.py`

```python
import tempfile
import unittest
from pathlib import Path
import numpy as np
from numpy.testing import assert_allclose, assert_array_equal
from return_histograms import fixture, summarize, build_figure, plt


class HistogramTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def setUp(self):
        self.edges = np.array([-.10, -.02, 0., .02, .10])

    def test_known_counts_and_coverage(self):
        result = summarize(fixture(), self.edges)
        assert_array_equal(result['counts'], [2, 2, 3, 4])
        self.assertEqual(result['total'], 13); self.assertEqual(result['missing'], 2)
        self.assertEqual(result['counts'].sum(), 11)
        self.assertEqual((result['observed'] == 0).sum(), 2)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(np.mean(result['observed'] < 0), 4/11)

    def test_edge_assignment_including_final_edge(self):
        # Interior edges belong to the bin on their right; the last edge is included.
        result = summarize(self.edges.copy(), self.edges)
        assert_array_equal(result['counts'], [1, 1, 1, 2])

    def test_density_area_and_independent_numpy_route(self):
        result = summarize(fixture(), self.edges)
        assert_allclose(result['mass'], np.array([2, 2, 3, 4])/11)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(result['mass'].sum(), 1)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(np.sum(result['density'] * np.diff(self.edges)), 1)
        expected, _ = np.histogram(result['observed'], bins=self.edges, density=True)
        assert_allclose(result['density'], expected)
        self.assertGreater(result['density'].max(), 1)

    def test_permutation_invariance(self):
        a = summarize(fixture(), self.edges)
        b = summarize(fixture()[::-1], self.edges)
        assert_array_equal(a['counts'], b['counts'])
        assert_allclose(a['density'], b['density'])

    def test_invalid_returns(self):
        for bad in [np.array([np.nan, np.nan]), np.array([0., np.nan]),
                    np.array([0., np.inf]), np.array([-1.01, 0.]),
                    np.array([0., 11.]), np.array(['0', '1']),
                    np.array([[0., .01]])]:
            with self.subTest(values=bad), self.assertRaises(ValueError):
                summarize(bad, self.edges)

    def test_invalid_edges_and_uncovered_tails(self):
        for bad in [np.array([0., 0.]), self.edges[::-1], np.array([-.1, np.inf]),
                    np.array([-.05, .05]), np.array([-.1, -.1 + 1e-8, .1])]:
            with self.subTest(edges=bad), self.assertRaises(ValueError):
                summarize(fixture(), bad)

    def test_inputs_preserved_and_zero_sample_supported(self):
        values = fixture(); before = values.copy(); edges = self.edges.copy()
        result = summarize(values, edges)
        result['observed'][0] = 0; result['edges'][0] = -0.5
        assert_allclose(values, before, equal_nan=True)
        assert_array_equal(edges, self.edges)
        zero = summarize(np.zeros(3), self.edges)
        assert_array_equal(zero['counts'], [0, 0, 3, 0])

    def test_artists_png_and_cleanup(self):
        before = set(plt.get_fignums()); fig, axes = build_figure(fixture())
        try:
            left = axes[0].patches[0].get_data()
            right = axes[1].patches[0].get_data()
            assert_array_equal(left.values, [1, 3, 6, 1])
            assert_allclose(right.values, np.array([2, 2, 3, 4])/11/np.diff(self.edges))
            self.assertIn('10', axes[0].xaxis.get_major_formatter()(.1))
            with tempfile.TemporaryDirectory() as folder:
                path = Path(folder)/'hist.png'; fig.savefig(path)
                self.assertEqual(path.read_bytes()[:8], b'\x89PNG\r\n\x1a\n')
        finally:
            plt.close(fig)
        self.assertEqual(set(plt.get_fignums()), before)


if __name__ == '__main__':
    unittest.main()
```

The eight methods cover known counts and coverage, edge inclusion, density normalization and NumPy's independent `density=True` route, permutation invariance, invalid samples, invalid or tail-excluding edges, source preservation and an all-zero sample, and plotted stair heights plus PNG export and figure cleanup.

The manual fixture counts make the first check independent of automatic edge selection. The edge test includes every supplied edge as an observation, including the final right edge. Density is reconciled through area rather than a sum of heights.

Automated checks establish data assignment and file generation. They do not establish readability or whether the chart is appropriate for a real investment question. The exported PNG therefore also receives a separate visual inspection.

## 14. Common Errors and Corrections

| Error | Consequence | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Replacing NaNs with zero | Adds invented mass at zero | Exclude unknowns explicitly and disclose their count |
| Filtering out zero returns with a truthiness check | Removes valid unchanged outcomes | Select by missingness and validity, not nonzero value |
| Selecting narrow support without accounting for tails | Hides observations and changes normalization | Cover all observations or disclose truncation and denominator |
| Treating density height as bin probability | Can produce impossible-looking percentages | Multiply density by the bin width |
| Summing density heights to check normalization | Fails for general widths | Sum height times width |
| Comparing unequal-width count heights as concentration | Wider bins gain a mechanical advantage | Use density or explain the width effect |
| Comparing count histograms with different sample sizes | Height reflects sample size as well as composition | Use compatible normalization and disclose sizes |
| Inferring a normal model from a bell-like chart | Confuses appearance with model validation | Treat it as a descriptive picture; test a separately specified model |
| Mixing different return horizons | Combines incompatible measurements | Establish a consistent sample definition upstream |
| Treating an empty tail bin as zero future risk | Overstates what a finite sample establishes | Report the sample limit and avoid unsupported extrapolation |

## 15. Exercises and Answer Guidance

1. **Boundary:** Which unequal-width bin contains exactly -2%?  
   **Guidance:** `[-2%, 0%)`, because internal right edges are excluded from the preceding bin.
2. **Final edge:** Is exactly +10% included?  
   **Guidance:** Yes; the last bin includes its final right edge.
3. **Denominator:** Why is the observed negative fraction 4/11 rather than 4/13?  
   **Guidance:** Two slots have unknown signs. The stated fraction describes observed returns only.
4. **Density:** A bin contains 5 of 20 observations and has decimal width 0.05. Find its share and density.  
   **Guidance:** Share 0.25; density `0.25 / 0.05 = 5` per unit decimal return. Area is 0.25.
5. **Units:** If the same width is written as five percentage points instead of decimal 0.05, what is the corresponding density?  
   **Guidance:** `0.25 / 5 = 0.05` per percentage point; the area still represents the same share.
6. **Permutation:** Reverse the sample order. What remains unchanged and what information is unavailable?  
   **Guidance:** Counts and densities remain unchanged; the histogram cannot identify the original temporal sequence or its drawdown path.
7. **Missing versus zero:** What would filling the two NaNs with zero change?  
   **Guidance:** Observed size would be falsely increased to 13 and the zero-containing bin count increased by two, changing shares and densities.
8. **Tail policy:** Why does the figure reject a supplied -15% return?  
   **Guidance:** Its fixed support is [-10%, +10%]. Expand the declared edges and display range together instead of silently excluding it.
