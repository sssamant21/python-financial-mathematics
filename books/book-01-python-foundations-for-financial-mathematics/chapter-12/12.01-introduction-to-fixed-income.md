## 1. What is fixed income?

**Fixed income** is a broad category of investments built around contractual or expected debt-related cash flows. A conventional bond is a debt security issued by a government, company, or other borrower. The issuer promises payments according to the security's terms, subject to its ability and willingness to pay.

The term does **not** imply that every fixed-income security has a fixed market price, a fixed interest payment, or zero risk. Floating-rate bonds, inflation-linked bonds, zero-coupon bonds, callable securities, and defaultable obligations behave differently.

The central question in fixed-income mathematics is:

**What is the present value of a specified stream of future cash flows under a given discounting convention?**

## 2. Bond terminology

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Issuer | Entity that borrows through the security |
| Face value / par | Principal amount contractually due at maturity, subject to credit risk |
| Coupon rate | Contractual rate used to determine coupon payments |
| Coupon payment | Periodic interest cash flow under the bond terms |
| Maturity | Date at which contractual principal is due |
| Market price | Amount investors pay for the security, excluding or including accrued interest depending on quotation convention |
| Yield | Return or discount-rate measure inferred from price and cash flows under a stated convention |
| Credit risk | Possibility the issuer fails to make promised payments |
| Interest-rate risk | Exposure of bond value to changes in market discount rates |

A coupon rate is **not** the same as yield to maturity. Coupon rate determines promised payments; yield is a price-linked valuation or return measure.

## 3. A basic fixed-coupon bond

Consider a bond with:

- Face value **F = $1,000**.
- Annual coupon rate **c = 5%**.
- Coupon frequency **m = 2** payments per year.
- Time to maturity **T = 3 years**.
- Number of coupon periods **n = mT = 6**.

The coupon per period is:

**C = F × c / m = 1000 × 0.05 / 2 = $25.**

The issuer's promised payments are $25 at each of the first five half-year dates, followed by **$1,025** at maturity: the final $25 coupon plus the $1,000 principal repayment.

| Period | Time in years | Coupon | Principal | Total promised cash flow |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 0.5 | $25 | $0 | $25 |
| 2 | 1.0 | $25 | $0 | $25 |
| 3 | 1.5 | $25 | $0 | $25 |
| 4 | 2.0 | $25 | $0 | $25 |
| 5 | 2.5 | $25 | $0 | $25 |
| 6 | 3.0 | $25 | $1,000 | $1,025 |

The undiscounted promised total is **$1,150**, but that is not the present market value. Future payments must be discounted, and actual realization depends on the issuer and contract.

## 4. Present-value principle

For promised cash flows CF(k) at the end of equal periods, and a constant effective periodic discount rate i, the simple model is:

**P = sum from k=1 to n of CF(k)/(1+i)^k.**

For a plain fixed-coupon bond:

**P = sum from k=1 to n of C/(1+i)^k + F/(1+i)^n.**

When the quoted annual yield y is **nominal with coupon-frequency compounding**, set **i=y/m**. This convention is explicitly assumed here; real market quotation conventions can differ.

For positive i, the equivalent annuity form is:

**P = C[1-(1+i)^(-n)]/i + F/(1+i)^n.**

For i=0, **P=nC+F**.

This is a simplified promised-cash-flow model. It does not explicitly incorporate default probabilities, taxes, liquidity premiums, option features, settlement-date accrual, or a nonflat term structure.

## 5. Premium, par, and discount

For the example bond with 5% coupon rate and six semiannual periods:

- If the market yield is **5%** under the same nominal semiannual convention, the model price is **$1,000 (par)**.
- If the market yield is **3%**, the model price is **above $1,000 (premium)**.
- If the market yield is **7%**, the model price is **below $1,000 (discount)**.

The intuition: a lower discount rate increases the present value of fixed promised cash flows, while a higher discount rate decreases it.

The relationship is conditional on identical frequency conventions and a conventional positive-cash-flow bond. Later sections explore bond pricing and yield sensitivity more deeply.

## 6. Major categories of fixed-income instruments

**Government bonds:** Issued by sovereign or other government entities. Credit and currency risks depend on the issuer and denomination.

**Corporate bonds:** Issued by companies. Their valuation reflects contractual terms and issuer creditworthiness.

**Municipal bonds:** Issued by state and local governments or related entities; tax treatment can affect investor comparisons.

**Zero-coupon bonds:** Make no periodic coupon payments; their promised maturity payment is discounted to the valuation date.

**Floating-rate notes:** Coupon rates reset according to contractual reference-rate rules, often with a spread.

**Inflation-linked securities:** Principal and/or payments adjust according to an inflation index under the issue's rules.

**Callable and putable bonds:** Embedded options alter the issuer's or holder's ability to change the expected cash-flow path.

**Asset-backed securities:** Payments depend on pools of underlying assets and structural rules; prepayment and credit risks can be significant.

## 7. The principal risks

- **Interest-rate risk:** Bond prices can change when market yields change.
- **Credit/default risk:** Promised payments may not be received in full or on time.
- **Reinvestment risk:** Coupons may be reinvested at rates different from the original yield assumption.
- **Liquidity risk:** Selling quickly may require accepting a less favorable price.
- **Inflation risk:** Fixed nominal cash flows may lose purchasing power.
- **Call/prepayment risk:** Contractual options or underlying borrower behavior can change expected cash flows.
- **Currency risk:** Payments denominated in another currency may fluctuate in home-currency value.

Do not confuse a predictable contractual payment schedule with a guaranteed investment return.

## 8. Relationship to loans and mortgages

Chapter 11 modeled loans primarily from the **borrower's** perspective: the borrower receives principal and makes payments.

Bond analytics commonly takes the **investor's** perspective: the investor pays a purchase price and receives coupons and principal, subject to the security's terms and credit performance.

Both use discounting, amortization, and cash-flow timing. The difference is the instrument's cash-flow structure and the direction of cash flows.

The standard-library lab constructs coupon cash flows, calculates their discounted price using `math.log1p`, classifies premium/par/discount pricing, and includes **12 unit tests**. Independent execution evidence remains to be recorded.

```python
"""Chapter 12.1 — fixed-income foundations, standard-library reference lab."""
import math
import unittest

def validate(face, coupon_rate, years, frequency, yield_rate):
    for name, value in (("face",face),("coupon rate",coupon_rate),
                        ("years",years),("yield",yield_rate)):
        if isinstance(value,bool) or not isinstance(value,(int,float)) or not math.isfinite(value):
            raise ValueError(name+" must be finite")
    if face<=0 or years<=0 or coupon_rate<0 or yield_rate<0:
        raise ValueError("face/years must be positive; rates nonnegative")
    if isinstance(frequency,bool) or not isinstance(frequency,int) or frequency<1:
        raise ValueError("frequency must be positive integer")
    n=years*frequency
    if not math.isclose(n,round(n),abs_tol=1e-10):
        raise ValueError("years * frequency must be an integer")
    return int(round(n))

def bond_cash_flows(face,coupon_rate,years,frequency=2):
    n=validate(face,coupon_rate,years,frequency,0)
    coupon=face*coupon_rate/frequency
    return [(k, coupon+(face if k==n else 0.0)) for k in range(1,n+1)]

def price(face,coupon_rate,years,yield_rate,frequency=2):
    n=validate(face,coupon_rate,years,frequency,yield_rate)
    periodic_yield=yield_rate/frequency
    coupon=face*coupon_rate/frequency
    if periodic_yield==0:
        return coupon*n+face
    discount=math.log1p(periodic_yield)
    coupons=math.fsum(coupon*math.exp(-k*discount) for k in range(1,n+1))
    principal=face*math.exp(-n*discount)
    return coupons+principal

def classify(face,coupon_rate,years,yield_rate,frequency=2):
    p=price(face,coupon_rate,years,yield_rate,frequency)
    if math.isclose(p,face,rel_tol=1e-11,abs_tol=1e-8):return "par"
    return "premium" if p>face else "discount"

class Tests(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_01_cash_flow_count(self):
        self.assertEqual(len(bond_cash_flows(1000,.05,3,2)),6)
    def test_02_coupon(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(bond_cash_flows(1000,.05,3,2)[0][1],25)
    def test_03_maturity_cash_flow(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(bond_cash_flows(1000,.05,3,2)[-1][1],1025)
    def test_04_total_undiscounted(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(sum(v for _,v in bond_cash_flows(1000,.05,3,2)),1150)
    def test_05_at_par(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(price(1000,.05,3,.05,2),1000,places=8)
    def test_06_premium(self):
        self.assertGreater(price(1000,.05,3,.03,2),1000)
    def test_07_discount(self):
        self.assertLess(price(1000,.05,3,.07,2),1000)
    def test_08_zero_coupon(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(price(1000,0,3,.06,2),1000/(1+.06/2)**6)
    def test_09_zero_yield(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(price(1000,.05,3,0,2),1150)
    def test_10_price_decreases_with_yield(self):
        self.assertGreater(price(1000,.05,3,.04,2),price(1000,.05,3,.06,2))
    def test_11_classifications(self):
        self.assertEqual(classify(1000,.05,3,.05),"par")
        self.assertEqual(classify(1000,.05,3,.03),"premium")
        self.assertEqual(classify(1000,.05,3,.07),"discount")
    def test_12_invalid_inputs(self):
        for args in ((0,.05,3,.05,2),(1000,-.01,3,.05,2),
                     (1000,.05,3,-.01,2),(1000,.05,3,.05,0),
                     (1000,.05,3.1,.05,2)):
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError):price(*args)

if __name__=="__main__":
    for y in (.03,.05,.07):
        print("Yield:",f"{100*y:.1f}%","Price:",f"{price(1000,.05,3,y,2):.2f}",
              "Classification:",classify(1000,.05,3,y,2))
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
```

## 10. Exercises and worked answers

1. **Issuer:** What is an issuer? **Answer:** The entity borrowing money by issuing a debt security.
2. **Face value:** What is the example bond's principal due at maturity? **Answer:** $1,000, subject to payment performance.
3. **Coupon:** Calculate a semiannual coupon on $1,000 at 5% annual coupon rate. **Answer:** $25.
4. **Periods:** How many coupons are scheduled over three years with semiannual payments? **Answer:** Six.
5. **Final payment:** What is due at maturity for the example bond? **Answer:** $1,025.
6. **Undiscounted total:** Sum all six promised payments. **Answer:** 5×25+1025=$1,150.
7. **Present value:** Why is $1,150 not the bond's present price? **Answer:** Future cash flows must be discounted and are subject to payment risk.
8. **Periodic yield:** Convert nominal annual 6% with semiannual compounding. **Answer:** 3% per half-year.
9. **At par:** What yield matches a 5% coupon under the same frequency convention for a plain fixed-coupon bond? **Answer:** 5%.
10. **Premium:** What happens to price when yield falls below coupon rate? **Answer:** The modeled price rises above par.
11. **Discount:** What happens when yield rises above coupon rate? **Answer:** The modeled price falls below par.
12. **Zero coupon:** How is a zero-coupon bond priced? **Answer:** Discount the single promised maturity payment.
13. **Rate risk:** Why can a fixed-coupon bond's market price change? **Answer:** Its cash flows are fixed, but market discount rates change.
14. **Credit risk:** Does a contractual payment schedule guarantee receipt? **Answer:** No; the issuer may default.
15. **Reinvestment risk:** Why does a realized return differ from an initial yield measure? **Answer:** Coupons may be reinvested at different rates, and sale price may change.
16. **Borrower versus investor:** What is the key cash-flow perspective difference? **Answer:** A borrower receives proceeds and repays; a bond investor pays the price and receives contractual payments.
