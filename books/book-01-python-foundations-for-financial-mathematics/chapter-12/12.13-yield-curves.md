## 1. What is a yield curve?

A yield curve relates an interest-rate measure to the **time to maturity or tenor** at a specified valuation date. It is a snapshot of the term structure, not a prediction of realized future rates.

A curve is only meaningful when its underlying rates are comparable. Government yields, swap rates, overnight-indexed rates, credit yields, and zero-coupon spot rates can differ because of instruments, credit exposure, liquidity, compounding, and quotation conventions.

Always identify the **valuation date, instrument universe, currency, rate type, day count, compounding, and tenor**.

## 2. Common yield-curve types

| Curve | Meaning | Typical application |
|---|---|---|
| Par yield curve | Coupon rates that would price standardized coupon instruments at par | Benchmark coupon-bond comparison |
| Spot/zero curve | Discount rates for individual maturities, under a specified convention | Discounting dated cash flows |
| Forward curve | Rates implied today for future borrowing/lending intervals | Forward valuation and risk analysis |
| Market yield curve | Observed yields of selected traded securities | Market overview and relative-value analysis |

A par yield at five years is generally **not** the five-year zero-coupon spot rate. A coupon bond contains multiple dated payments, which must be discounted consistently.

## 3. Yield-curve shapes

- **Upward sloping:** Longer-tenor rates exceed shorter-tenor rates over the specified range.
- **Flat:** Rates are similar across the compared maturities.
- **Inverted:** Shorter-tenor rates exceed longer-tenor rates.
- **Humped:** Intermediate maturities have higher rates than both shorter and longer tenors.

Curve shapes reflect multiple forces, including monetary-policy expectations, expected inflation, term premiums, credit, and market supply and demand. No single shape mechanically determines future economic outcomes.

## 4. Level, slope, and curvature

A curve can move through:

1. **Level change:** Many tenors rise or fall together.
2. **Slope change:** Short and long maturities move by different amounts, steepening or flattening the curve.
3. **Curvature change:** Intermediate maturities move relative to the ends.

A parallel shift is a convenient stress scenario but is not the only relevant interest-rate risk.

## 5. Worked example — An illustrative spot curve

Assume annual-effective spot rates:

| Maturity | Spot rate |
|---:|---:|
| 1 year | 3.00% |
| 2 years | 3.50% |
| 3 years | 4.00% |
| 5 years | 4.50% |

The endpoint slope is **4.50% − 3.00% = 1.50 percentage points**, or **150 basis points**, over the 1-to-5-year span.

The curve is upward sloping between its endpoints. This does not imply that every intervening segment of an arbitrary curve is upward sloping.

## 6. Interpolating missing maturities

For an illustrative piecewise-linear interpolation of spot rates between known nodes (t0,r0) and (t1,r1):

**r(t) = r0 + [r1−r0]×(t−t0)/(t1−t0).**

For t=1.5 years between the 1-year 3.00% and 2-year 3.50% nodes:

**r(1.5) = 3.00% + (3.50%−3.00%)×0.5 = 3.25%.**

For t=4 years between the 3-year 4.00% and 5-year 4.50% nodes:

**r(4) = 4.25%.**

Interpolation is a modeling choice. Linear interpolation of zero rates, log discount factors, or instantaneous forward rates can produce different values and smoothness properties. Production curve construction also requires market calibration, conventions, and extrapolation policies.

## 7. Discount factors from spot rates

Under **annual-effective compounding**, a spot rate r(t) implies:

**DF(t) = 1/[1+r(t)]^t.**

The present value of a single cash flow CF at time t is:

**PV = CF×DF(t).**

For the illustrative two-year spot rate of 3.50%:

**DF(2) = 1/(1.035)^2 ≈ 0.93352.**

A $1,000 payment due in two years has present value:

**PV ≈ $933.52.**

These formulas require **1+r(t)>0** and consistent units. The chapter's Python lab uses annual-effective compounding for clarity.

## 8. Pricing a coupon bond with a spot curve

Suppose a bond pays CF(k) at times t(k), and a discount factor is available for each payment:

**P = Σ CF(k)×DF(t(k)).**

Unlike discounting every cash flow at a single YTM, spot-curve pricing uses a maturity-appropriate discount factor for each payment.

A bond's quoted YTM is generally a summary rate that reproduces its market price under a specific cash-flow and compounding convention. It is not itself a complete discount curve.

## 9. Forward rates implied by spot rates

For annual-effective compounding, the forward rate f(t1,t2) implied between t1 and t2 satisfies:

**[1+f(t1,t2)]^(t2−t1) = DF(t1)/DF(t2).**

Therefore:

**f(t1,t2) = [DF(t1)/DF(t2)]^[1/(t2−t1)] − 1.**

For the 1-year 3.00% and 2-year 3.50% spot rates:

**1+f(1,2) = (1.035)^2/(1.03).**

The resulting one-year forward rate is approximately **4.00%**. This is an **implied forward rate**, not a guaranteed realized future rate.

Section 12.15 develops forward rates more fully.

## 10. Curve construction versus curve illustration

A production yield curve is typically constructed from actual instruments such as deposits, Treasury securities, futures, swaps, or overnight-indexed swaps, subject to a specified market framework.

**Bootstrapping** solves for discount factors sequentially or jointly so that modeled instrument values match observed market quotes, after appropriate conventions and adjustments.

The lab below deliberately **does not bootstrap** market instruments. It starts with supplied illustrative spot-rate nodes and interpolates within their tenor range. This distinction prevents confusing a demonstration curve with a calibrated market curve.

## 11. Operational checks and limitations

- Ensure tenors are strictly increasing and no duplicate maturity nodes exist.
- Keep rates in decimal units: 3.5% is 0.035.
- Use consistent compounding when converting rates to discount factors.
- Do not silently extrapolate beyond the observed curve.
- Verify discount-factor positivity and investigate arbitrage inconsistencies.
- Distinguish yield-curve shifts from issuer credit-spread changes.
- Treat missing, stale, or outlier quotes as data-quality issues.

For positive spot rates, discount factors generally decrease with maturity in the illustrative example, but this is not an unconditional property of all possible market rate environments.

## 12. Python hands-on lab

Save as **yield_curves.py**. Run `python yield_curves.py` and `python -m compileall -q yield_curves.py`.

The lab implements piecewise-linear spot-rate interpolation, discount factors, zero-coupon valuation, implied forwards, endpoint shape classification, and **12 reference unit tests**. Independent runtime verification remains pending.

```python
import math, unittest
def validate(nodes):
    if len(nodes)<2:raise ValueError('at least two nodes required')
    for t,r in nodes:
        if any(isinstance(v,bool) or not isinstance(v,(int,float)) or not math.isfinite(v) for v in (t,r)) or t<=0:raise ValueError('invalid node')
    if any(nodes[i][0]>=nodes[i+1][0] for i in range(len(nodes)-1)):raise ValueError('tenors must strictly increase')
def interpolate(nodes,t):
    validate(nodes)
    if isinstance(t,bool) or not isinstance(t,(int,float)) or not math.isfinite(t) or not nodes[0][0]<=t<=nodes[-1][0]:raise ValueError('tenor outside curve range')
    for k,(tk,rk) in enumerate(nodes):
        if t==tk:return rk
        if t<tk:
            t0,r0=nodes[k-1]
            return r0+(rk-r0)*(t-t0)/(tk-t0)
    return nodes[-1][1]
def discount_factor(nodes,t):
    r=interpolate(nodes,t)
    if r<=-1:raise ValueError('invalid annually compounded spot rate')
    return math.exp(-t*math.log1p(r))
def zero_price(face,nodes,t):
    if isinstance(face,bool) or not isinstance(face,(int,float)) or not math.isfinite(face) or face<=0:raise ValueError('invalid face')
    return face*discount_factor(nodes,t)
def forward(nodes,t1,t2):
    if not 0<t1<t2:raise ValueError('invalid forward interval')
    return (discount_factor(nodes,t1)/discount_factor(nodes,t2))**(1/(t2-t1))-1
def shape(nodes):
    validate(nodes);first,last=nodes[0][1],nodes[-1][1]
    return 'upward' if last>first else 'downward' if last<first else 'flat-endpoints'
class Tests(unittest.TestCase):
    def setUp(self):self.c=[(1,.03),(2,.035),(3,.04),(5,.045)]
    def test_01_node(self):self.assertEqual(interpolate(self.c,2),.035)
    def test_02_midpoint(self):self.assertAlmostEqual(interpolate(self.c,1.5),.0325)
    def test_03_long_interval(self):self.assertAlmostEqual(interpolate(self.c,4),.0425)
    def test_04_discount(self):self.assertAlmostEqual(discount_factor(self.c,2),1/1.035**2)
    def test_05_price(self):self.assertAlmostEqual(zero_price(1000,self.c,2),1000/1.035**2)
    def test_06_forward(self):self.assertAlmostEqual((1+forward(self.c,1,2)),1.035**2/1.03)
    def test_07_shape(self):self.assertEqual(shape(self.c),'upward')
    def test_08_inverted(self):self.assertEqual(shape([(1,.05),(2,.04)]),'downward')
    def test_09_flat(self):self.assertEqual(shape([(1,.04),(2,.04)]),'flat-endpoints')
    def test_10_discount_monotonic(self):self.assertLess(discount_factor(self.c,3),discount_factor(self.c,2))
    def test_11_positive_price(self):self.assertGreater(zero_price(1000,self.c,3),0)
    def test_12_invalid(self):
        for fn in (lambda:interpolate(self.c,.5),lambda:interpolate([(2,.03),(1,.04)],1.5),lambda:zero_price(0,self.c,2),lambda:forward(self.c,2,2)):
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError):fn()
if __name__=='__main__':
    curve=[(1,.03),(2,.035),(3,.04),(5,.045)]
    print('Curve shape:',shape(curve))
    for t in (1,1.5,2,3,4,5):print(f'{t:.1f}y: spot={interpolate(curve,t):.4%}, DF={discount_factor(curve,t):.6f}')
    print('1y-2y forward:',forward(curve,1,2))
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
```

## 13. Exercises with worked answers

1. **Definition:** What does a yield curve plot? **Answer:** A rate measure against tenor or maturity at a valuation date.
2. **Spot curve:** What does it provide? **Answer:** Maturity-specific zero-coupon discount rates.
3. **Par curve:** What does it represent? **Answer:** Coupon rates for standardized instruments priced at par.
4. **Forward curve:** What does it show? **Answer:** Rates implied today for future intervals.
5. **Upward shape:** What is the endpoint relationship? **Answer:** Longer-tenor rate exceeds shorter-tenor rate.
6. **Inversion:** What is the endpoint relationship? **Answer:** Shorter-tenor rate exceeds longer-tenor rate.
7. **Slope:** 5-year 4.5% minus 1-year 3.0%? **Answer:** 150 bp.
8. **Interpolation:** 1.5-year rate between 3.0% and 3.5%? **Answer:** 3.25%.
9. **Interpolation:** 4-year rate between 3-year 4.0% and 5-year 4.5%? **Answer:** 4.25%.
10. **Discount factor:** Formula under annual-effective compounding? **Answer:** (1+r)^(-t).
11. **Two-year DF:** At 3.5%? **Answer:** Approximately 0.93352.
12. **Zero price:** PV of $1,000 due in two years at 3.5%? **Answer:** Approximately $933.52.
13. **Spot pricing:** How do you value multiple cash flows? **Answer:** Sum each CF multiplied by its matching discount factor.
