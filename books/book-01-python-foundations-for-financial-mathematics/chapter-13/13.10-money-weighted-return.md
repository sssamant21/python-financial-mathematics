## Learning objectives

Understand money-weighted return (MWR) and internal rate of return (IRR); construct investor-perspective cash flows; calculate periodic IRR and date-based XIRR; compare money-weighted and time-weighted returns; identify multiple-root and no-root cases; implement bracketed numerical solvers; and design audit-ready performance reports.

## 1. What money-weighted return measures

Money-weighted return accounts for **how much capital was invested and when it was invested**. It is commonly calculated as an internal rate of return: the discount rate that makes the present value of all signed investment cash flows equal to zero.

Money-weighted return differs from time-weighted return (Section 13.9). TWR geometrically links investment subperiod returns to largely neutralize investor cash-flow timing. MWR deliberately retains that timing, making it relevant to the investor's realized capital deployment.

**Sign convention used throughout this section:**

- Investor contribution or purchase: **negative cash flow** (money paid out).
- Investor withdrawal, distribution, or final liquidation value: **positive cash flow** (money received).
- Final portfolio value is treated as a **positive terminal cash flow**, even if not actually liquidated, and must be clearly labeled as a valuation assumption.

## 2. Periodic IRR equation

For equally spaced cash flows CF0,...,CFn, the periodic IRR r solves:

**0 = CF0 + CF1/(1+r) + CF2/(1+r)² + ... + CFn/(1+r)^n.**

The discounting convention assumes the first cash flow occurs at time zero and each subsequent cash flow occurs at the end of a regular period.

IRR is a root-finding problem; most cash-flow sequences do not have a convenient closed-form solution.

### Worked example A — One-year investment

Invest $100 today and receive $110 one year later.

**0 = −100 + 110/(1+r).**

Therefore **r=10%**.

The IRR coincides with the simple holding-period return in this special single-investment, single-distribution case.

## 3. Multi-year investment

Invest $100 now, receive nothing at the end of year 1, and receive $121 at the end of year 2.

**0 = −100 + 121/(1+r)²**.

Thus **(1+r)²=1.21** and **r=10% annually**.

A cumulative 21% gain over two years corresponds to a 10% effective annual compound return.

## 4. Contributions change money-weighted performance

Suppose an investor contributes $100 at time 0 and another $50 at the end of year 1. At the end of year 2, the portfolio is worth $180.

The cash-flow sequence is:

| Time | Investor cash flow |
|---|---:|
| 0 | −$100 |
| 1 | −$50 |
| 2 | +$180 |

The IRR solves:

**−100−50/(1+r)+180/(1+r)²=0.**

Multiplying by (1+r)² and writing x=1+r:

**100x²+50x−180=0**.

The positive x root is:

**x=[−50+sqrt(50²+4×100×180)]/200**.

Then **r=x−1≈11.45%**.

The cash-flow timing matters: $100 was invested for two years, while $50 was invested for only one year.

## 5. Money-weighted versus time-weighted return

Consider two investment periods:

- Portfolio starts with $100 and earns +10%, reaching $110.
- The investor contributes $50, bringing capital to $160.
- The portfolio then earns −10%, ending at $144.

**TWR = (1.10)(0.90)−1 = −1% over the two periods.**

Investor cash flows are −$100 at time 0, −$50 at time 1, and +$144 at time 2.

MWR solves:

**−100−50/(1+r)+144/(1+r)²=0**.

This gives a periodic IRR of approximately **−2.25% per period**. The investor committed additional capital immediately before the losing period, so the money-weighted outcome is worse than a constant-capital interpretation.

Do not compare a two-period cumulative TWR directly to a one-period IRR without aligning the time horizons: if each period is one year, the two-year cumulative TWR is −1%, while the IRR is an annualized rate.

## 6. Date-based XIRR for irregular cash flows

Actual investment cash flows rarely occur at perfectly equal intervals. A date-based money-weighted return uses elapsed time fractions:

**0 = Σ CF_i / (1+r)^((date_i−date_0)/365).**

This example uses an explicit **Actual/365 Fixed** year fraction. The computed r is an annual-effective rate under that convention.

### Worked example B — One calendar year

Invest $100 on January 1, 2025, and receive $110 on January 1, 2026.

Elapsed days = 365.

**0 = −100 + 110/(1+r)^(365/365)**.

Therefore **XIRR=10% annually**.

For leap years or other irregular intervals, the day-count convention must be explicitly recorded. Some spreadsheets use 365 in XIRR calculations; financial contracts and performance standards may prescribe other rules.

## 7. Solving IRR numerically

Define the net present value function:

**NPV(r)=Σ CF_t/(1+r)^t**.

Find r such that **NPV(r)=0**.

A bracketed bisection solver:

1. Chooses lower and upper candidate rates greater than −100%.
2. Evaluates NPV at each endpoint.
3. Requires opposite signs at the endpoints.
4. Repeatedly halves the interval and retains the half with a sign change.
5. Stops when the residual or interval width meets the tolerance.

Bisection is robust **when a sign-changing bracket exists**, but it does not guarantee that a root exists or that the root is unique.

For numerical production use, carefully handle overflow, very long horizons, unusual cash-flow scales, and ill-conditioned roots.

## 8. Multiple IRRs and ambiguous cash flows

Cash flows that switch sign more than once can have **multiple IRR roots**.

For example:

**CF = [−100, +230, −132]**.

The NPV equation is:

**−100+230/(1+r)−132/(1+r)²=0**.

Multiplying by (1+r)²:

**−100(1+r)²+230(1+r)−132=0**.

The two valid roots are **r=10%** and **r=20%**.

A root finder using a single bracket can return one root or fail to find either, depending on the endpoints. There is no uniquely defined single IRR without an additional decision rule.

Other possible cases include no admissible real IRR or an economically unhelpful IRR. Alternative evaluation methods include NPV at an explicitly chosen discount rate and modified IRR, where assumptions are stated.

## 9. Why IRR can mislead

IRR is a useful summary, but interpretation requires care:

- A high IRR on a small amount of invested capital may produce fewer dollars than a lower IRR on a much larger amount.
- Early distributions can raise IRR even if absolute gains are modest.
- Interim portfolio valuations are estimates rather than guaranteed proceeds.
- Multiple roots or no roots can make IRR ambiguous.
- Comparing IRRs across different horizons and risk profiles without context is misleading.
- IRR does not directly describe the investment's volatility, liquidity, or downside risk.

Report invested capital, distributions, terminal value, measurement dates, and an absolute-value metric alongside IRR.

## 10. Terminal value and residual value

For an ongoing investment, include the **estimated terminal market value** as a positive cash flow at the measurement date.

Example:

- Initial investment: −$100.
- Intermediate distribution: +$10.
- Terminal value: +$105.

For one year with both positive flows at the end:

**0=−100+(10+105)/(1+r)**.

**r=15%**.

If the terminal value is omitted, the calculation would ignore an asset the investor still owns. Conversely, counting a terminal value and also counting an actual sale of the same asset at the same instant would double-count proceeds.

## 11. Reconciliation with absolute wealth

A money-weighted return must reconcile with the signed cash-flow ledger. A simple investment multiple can be useful:

**Multiple of invested capital = (total realized distributions + terminal value) / total invested capital**,

provided categories are clearly defined and transfers are not double-counted.

For a $100 investment, $20 distribution, and $100 terminal value:

**Investment multiple = (20+100)/100=1.20×**.

The investment multiple ignores timing; IRR does not. They are complementary, not interchangeable.

The lab implements periodic NPV/IRR, dated XNPV/XIRR, sign validation, bisection root finding, and **12 reference unit tests**. The solvers deliberately reject brackets without a sign change; they do not enumerate every possible root. Independent runtime verification remains pending.

```python
import math
import unittest
from datetime import date

def validate_cashflows(flows):
    if len(flows) < 2:
        raise ValueError("at least two cash flows required")
    values = []
    for flow in flows:
        if isinstance(flow, bool) or not isinstance(flow, (int, float)) or not math.isfinite(flow):
            raise ValueError("cash flows must be finite numeric values")
        values.append(float(flow))
    if not any(x < 0 for x in values) or not any(x > 0 for x in values):
        raise ValueError("both negative and positive flows required")
    return values

def npv(rate, flows):
    values = validate_cashflows(flows)
    if not math.isfinite(rate) or rate <= -1:
        raise ValueError("rate must exceed -100%")
    return math.fsum(cf / (1 + rate) ** i for i, cf in enumerate(values))

def irr_bisection(flows, low=-0.999, high=1.0, tolerance=1e-12, max_iterations=300):
    values = validate_cashflows(flows)
    f_low, f_high = npv(low, values), npv(high, values)
    if f_low == 0: return low
    if f_high == 0: return high
    if f_low * f_high > 0:
        raise ValueError("no sign-changing root in bracket")
    for _ in range(max_iterations):
        mid = (low + high) / 2
        f_mid = npv(mid, values)
        if abs(f_mid) <= tolerance or (high - low) <= tolerance:
            return mid
        if f_low * f_mid <= 0:
            high, f_high = mid, f_mid
        else:
            low, f_low = mid, f_mid
    raise RuntimeError("IRR solver did not converge")

def xnpv(rate, dated_flows):
    if rate <= -1 or not math.isfinite(rate):
        raise ValueError("rate must exceed -100%")
    if len(dated_flows) < 2:
        raise ValueError("at least two dated flows required")
    if any(not isinstance(d, date) for d, _ in dated_flows):
        raise ValueError("date objects required")
    if any(d < dated_flows[0][0] for d, _ in dated_flows):
        raise ValueError("first date must be earliest")
    values = validate_cashflows([cf for _, cf in dated_flows])
    origin = dated_flows[0][0]
    return math.fsum(cf / (1 + rate) ** ((d - origin).days / 365.0)
                     for (d, _), cf in zip(dated_flows, values))

def xirr_bisection(dated_flows, low=-0.9, high=1.0, tolerance=1e-10):
    a, b = xnpv(low, dated_flows), xnpv(high, dated_flows)
    if a * b > 0:
        raise ValueError("no sign-changing root in bracket")
    for _ in range(300):
        m = (low + high) / 2
        fm = xnpv(m, dated_flows)
        if abs(fm) <= tolerance or high - low <= tolerance:
            return m
        if a * fm <= 0:
            high, b = m, fm
        else:
            low, a = m, fm
    raise RuntimeError("XIRR solver did not converge")

class MoneyWeightedTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_01_npv_zero(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(npv(.1,[-100,110]),0)
    def test_02_irr_gain(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(irr_bisection([-100,110]),.1)
    def test_03_irr_loss(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(irr_bisection([-100,90]),-.1)
    def test_04_irr_two_year(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(irr_bisection([-100,0,121]),.1)
    def test_05_irr_contribution(self):
        r=irr_bisection([-100,-50,180])
        self.assertAlmostEqual(npv(r,[-100,-50,180]),0,places=8)
    def test_06_xnpv(self):
        f=[(date(2025,1,1),-100),(date(2026,1,1),110)]
        self.assertAlmostEqual(xnpv(.1,f),0)
    def test_07_xirr(self):
        f=[(date(2025,1,1),-100),(date(2026,1,1),110)]
        self.assertAlmostEqual(xirr_bisection(f),.1)
    def test_08_zero_irr(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(irr_bisection([-100,100]),0)
    def test_09_multi_sign(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(npv(0,[-100,230,-132]),-2)
    def test_10_dated_contribution(self):
        f=[(date(2025,1,1),-100),(date(2025,7,1),-50),(date(2026,1,1),170)]
        r=xirr_bisection(f)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(xnpv(r,f),0,places=7)
    def test_11_no_bracket(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): irr_bisection([-100,1000],high=.1)
    def test_12_invalid(self):
        cases=[lambda:irr_bisection([-100]),lambda:npv(-1,[-100,110]),
               lambda:irr_bisection([10,20]),
               lambda:xnpv(.1,[(date(2026,1,1),-100),(date(2025,1,1),110)])]
        for fn in cases:
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError): fn()

if __name__ == "__main__":
    cashflows=[-100,-50,180]
    r=irr_bisection(cashflows)
    print("Periodic money-weighted return:", f"{r:.4%}")
    print("NPV residual:", npv(r,cashflows))
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
```

## 13. Exercises with worked solutions

1. **Single-period IRR:** Invest $100, receive $110. **Answer:** 10%.
2. **Single-period loss:** Invest $100, receive $90. **Answer:** −10%.
3. **Two-year IRR:** Invest $100, receive $121 after two years. **Answer:** 10% per year.
4. **Zero IRR:** Invest $100, receive $100 one period later. **Answer:** 0%.
5. **Cash-flow signs:** Invest $500. **Answer:** CF=−500 from the investor perspective.
6. **Cash-flow signs:** Withdraw $80. **Answer:** CF=+80.
7. **Terminal valuation:** Unsold portfolio worth $300. **Answer:** Include +300 terminal value at the measurement date.
8. **Two contributions:** Invest $100 now, $50 in one year, receive $180 after two years. **Answer:** Solve 100(1+r)²+50(1+r)−180=0; r≈11.45%.
9. **TWR:** +10% followed by −10%. **Answer:** −1% cumulative.
10. **MWR with contribution:** CF=[−100,−50,+144]. **Answer:** Approximately −2.25% per period.
11. **XIRR horizon:** January 1, 2025 to January 1, 2026. **Answer:** 365 days under Actual/365 Fixed.
12. **Date-based return:** −100 then +110 over 365 days. **Answer:** 10% annual XIRR.
13. **NPV check:** CF=[−100,+110] at r=10%. **Answer:** −100+110/1.1=0.
14. **Two IRR roots:** CF=[−100,+230,−132]. **Answer:** 10% and 20%.
15. **No bracket:** NPV has the same sign at both selected endpoints. **Answer:** Bisection cannot certify a root in that bracket.
16. **Investment multiple:** $100 invested, $20 distributed, $100 terminal value. **Answer:** 1.20×.
17. **Terminal-value double count:** Why not count both a sale and identical residual value? **Answer:** It would count the same proceeds twice.
18. **Method selection:** Evaluate manager skill independent of investor contribution timing. **Answer:** Use properly calculated TWR; MWR instead reflects investor capital timing.

## 15. Production and SRE considerations

- Record precise cash-flow dates, time zones, and cutoff policies.
- Distinguish external investor cash flows from internal portfolio transactions.
- Document sign conventions and terminal-value treatment.
- Reject duplicate or missing ledger events.
- Validate date ordering and year-fraction methodology.
- Detect multiple-root cases rather than assuming every IRR is unique.
- Use bracket searches and residual checks with explicit numerical tolerances.
- Track currency and FX conversion conventions.
- Preserve immutable input snapshots and solver-version metadata.
- Publish IRR alongside invested capital, distributions, terminal value, and TWR where relevant.
