# 13.8 — Arithmetic vs. Geometric Mean Return

**Book:** Book 1 — Python Foundations for Financial Mathematics  
**Chapter:** 13 — Returns and Investment Performance  
**Status:** Manuscript published as a draft; independent runtime and editorial QA pending

## Learning objectives

Distinguish the arithmetic and geometric mean of period returns; derive their formulas and economic interpretations; explain compounding drag and the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality; understand volatility-related approximations and their limitations; implement and validate calculations in Python.

## 1. Two averages answer different questions

Investment returns fluctuate over time. Two commonly reported averages are:

- **Arithmetic mean return:** the ordinary average of observed simple period returns.
- **Geometric mean return:** the constant compound rate that reproduces the observed terminal wealth over the same number of periods.

Neither statistic is universally better. The correct choice depends on whether the goal is describing the **average one-period outcome** or the **historical compound growth rate**.

For n period simple returns R1,...,Rn, define the arithmetic mean:

**A = (R1 + R2 + ... + Rn) / n**.

For gross factors Gt=1+Rt, define the geometric mean:

**G = [(1+R1)(1+R2)...(1+Rn)]^(1/n) − 1**.

The two means have the same units, provided the underlying returns have the same period length and methodology.

## 2. Worked example — +50%, then −50%

An investment begins with $1,000.

| Period | Simple return | Wealth |
|---|---:|---:|
| Start | — | $1,000 |
| 1 | +50% | $1,500 |
| 2 | −50% | $750 |

The arithmetic mean is:

**A = (0.50−0.50)/2 = 0%.**

The cumulative gross factor is:

**1.50 × 0.50 = 0.75**.

The cumulative simple return is **−25%**.

The geometric mean is:

**G = sqrt(0.75)−1 ≈ −13.39746% per period**.

Check:

**$1,000 × (1+G)² = $750.**

An arithmetic mean of zero does **not** imply unchanged wealth. The arithmetic mean describes the average of the two *reported period returns*, whereas the geometric mean describes the *compound rate* realized across them.

## 3. Deriving the geometric mean from wealth

For a self-financing investment with no unadjusted external cash flows:

**Wn = W0 ∏(1+Rt)**.

Suppose a constant period return g produced the same final wealth:

**Wn = W0(1+g)^n**.

Equating the two:

**(1+g)^n = ∏(1+Rt)**.

Thus:

**g = [∏(1+Rt)]^(1/n)−1**.

For strictly positive gross factors, logarithms give an equivalent expression:

**g = exp[(1/n)Σln(1+Rt)]−1**.

The logarithmic expression is useful for numerical stability. If any period has a −100% return, the terminal long-only wealth is zero and the geometric mean is −100%; the log-based calculation must treat this boundary separately.

## 4. Why the arithmetic mean is usually higher

For nonnegative gross factors, the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality states:

**(G1+...+Gn)/n ≥ (G1×...×Gn)^(1/n)**.

Subtracting one gives:

**Arithmetic mean simple return ≥ Geometric mean simple return**.

Equality occurs when all gross factors are equal, meaning every period's return is identical.

### Worked example — Constant returns

For +10%, +10%, and +10%:

**A=10%**.

**G=(1.1³)^(1/3)−1=10%**.

There is no arithmetic–geometric gap because there is no variability in the observed returns.

### Worked example — Unequal returns

For +20% and −10%:

**A=(20%−10%)/2=5%.**

**G=sqrt(1.20×0.90)−1=sqrt(1.08)−1≈3.92305%.**

The difference is approximately **1.07695 percentage points per period**.

## 5. Volatility drag: the mechanism

A percentage loss applies to wealth **after** earlier gains. For example:

- A +25% return turns $100 into $125.
- A subsequent −20% return turns $125 into $100.

The cumulative return is zero, even though the arithmetic mean is **+2.5%**.

The geometric mean is zero because **1.25×0.80=1**.

This gap is often called **volatility drag** or **variance drag**. It is a mathematical consequence of compounding. It does not mean that volatility alone uniquely determines compound performance; the complete return distribution and sequence matter for many real-world questions.

For a fixed set of returns and no external cash flows, reordering periods does not change the terminal product, although it can change interim wealth paths and drawdowns.

## 6. Approximation using return variance

For small simple returns, use the Taylor expansion:

**ln(1+R) ≈ R − R²/2**.

Taking the average and relating the second moment to variance suggests the familiar approximation:

**Geometric mean ≈ Arithmetic mean − variance/2**,

where variance is measured in **decimal return units squared** and the approximation is most accurate for small returns and moderate variability. More precisely, higher-order terms and the mean-return magnitude affect the difference.

### Worked example — +10% and −10%

Arithmetic mean:

**A = 0**.

Population variance:

**σ² = [(.10−0)²+(−.10−0)²]/2 = .01**.

Approximation:

**G ≈ 0−.01/2 = −0.005 = −0.5%**.

Exact geometric mean:

**sqrt(.99)−1 ≈ −0.501256%**.

The approximation is close here but should not be used as an exact identity, especially for large returns or heavy-tailed outcomes.

## 7. Sample variance versus population variance

When describing the observed n returns as the entire historical set under study, the population variance uses denominator n:

**σ²_pop = Σ(Rt−A)²/n**.

When estimating a population variance from a sample, the sample variance commonly uses n−1. These are different statistical estimators and must be labeled.

The illustrative variance-drag approximation in this section uses **population variance** over the observed return series. It is not a guarantee about expected future compound growth.

## 8. Annualization of average returns

For k equal-length periods per year, a historical geometric mean period return g has annual-effective equivalent:

**R_annual = (1+g)^k−1**.

For a historical geometric mean monthly return of 1%:

**R_annual = 1.01^12−1 ≈ 12.6825%.**

Multiplying an arithmetic mean monthly return by 12 may be used for some expected-return or approximation contexts, but it is **not** the same as realized annual compound growth.

For irregular intervals, use actual elapsed time rather than assuming every observation has equal duration.

## 9. Expected returns versus realized compound returns

An arithmetic mean is often useful as a sample estimate of a **one-period expected simple return**, under suitable assumptions. A geometric mean describes **realized compound growth** over a historical sequence.

Neither alone forecasts future performance. In particular:

- The sample arithmetic mean can be sensitive to outliers.
- The geometric mean can be dominated by severe losses, including total loss.
- Historical volatility and serial dependence can undermine simple forecasting assumptions.
- Contributions and withdrawals must be handled before interpreting a portfolio's growth rate.

The distinction between time-weighted and money-weighted performance is developed in Sections 13.9 and 13.10.

## 10. Portfolio interpretation

A one-period portfolio simple return is generally a weighted sum of constituent **simple** returns:

**Rp = Σwi Ri**, with beginning-value weights summing to one.

A portfolio's multi-period geometric return is computed by first determining the portfolio's actual return **each period**, reflecting weight drift or rebalancing, then linking those period returns.

Do not take a weighted average of individual assets' geometric returns and assume it equals the portfolio geometric return.

## 11. Numerical implementation

Python's `math.fsum` helps reduce floating-point summation error when averaging returns. For strictly positive gross factors, `math.log1p` and `math.expm1` provide a stable geometric-mean calculation:

```python
math.expm1(math.fsum(math.log1p(r) for r in returns) / len(returns))
```

This expression requires each r>−1. An observed −100% return should be handled explicitly as a zero-wealth boundary, rather than passed to `log1p`.

The lab computes arithmetic and geometric means, cumulative return, equivalent wealth, population variance, and a variance-drag approximation. It includes **12 reference unit tests**. Independent runtime verification remains pending.

```python
import math
import unittest

def checked_returns(returns):
    if not returns:
        raise ValueError("nonempty return sequence required")
    values = []
    for r in returns:
        if isinstance(r, bool) or not isinstance(r, (int, float)) or not math.isfinite(r) or r < -1:
            raise ValueError("finite simple returns >= -100% required")
        values.append(float(r))
    return values

def arithmetic_mean(returns):
    rs = checked_returns(returns)
    return math.fsum(rs) / len(rs)

def geometric_mean(returns):
    rs = checked_returns(returns)
    if -1.0 in rs:
        return -1.0
    return math.expm1(math.fsum(math.log1p(r) for r in rs) / len(rs))

def cumulative_return(returns):
    rs = checked_returns(returns)
    if -1.0 in rs:
        return -1.0
    return math.expm1(math.fsum(math.log1p(r) for r in rs))

def terminal_wealth(initial, returns):
    if isinstance(initial, bool) or not isinstance(initial, (int, float)) or not math.isfinite(initial) or initial <= 0:
        raise ValueError("positive finite initial wealth required")
    return initial * (1 + cumulative_return(returns))

def equivalent_wealth(initial, mean_return, periods):
    if isinstance(initial, bool) or not isinstance(initial, (int, float)) or not math.isfinite(initial) or initial <= 0:
        raise ValueError("positive finite initial wealth required")
    if isinstance(periods, bool) or not isinstance(periods, int) or periods <= 0:
        raise ValueError("positive integer periods required")
    if isinstance(mean_return, bool) or not isinstance(mean_return, (int, float)) or not math.isfinite(mean_return) or mean_return < -1:
        raise ValueError("invalid mean return")
    return initial * (1 + mean_return) ** periods

def population_variance(returns):
    rs = checked_returns(returns)
    mean = arithmetic_mean(rs)
    return math.fsum((r - mean) ** 2 for r in rs) / len(rs)

def approximate_geometric_mean(returns):
    rs = checked_returns(returns)
    return arithmetic_mean(rs) - population_variance(rs) / 2

class MeanReturnTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_01_arithmetic(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(arithmetic_mean([.5, -.5]), 0)
    def test_02_geometric(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(geometric_mean([.5, -.5]), math.sqrt(.75)-1)
    def test_03_cumulative(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(cumulative_return([.5, -.5]), -.25)
    def test_04_wealth(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(terminal_wealth(1000, [.5, -.5]), 750)
    def test_05_equivalent(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(equivalent_wealth(1000, geometric_mean([.5,-.5]), 2), 750)
    def test_06_constant(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(geometric_mean([.1,.1,.1]), .1)
    def test_07_inequality(self): self.assertLessEqual(geometric_mean([.2,-.1,.05]), arithmetic_mean([.2,-.1,.05]))
    def test_08_zero_loss(self): self.assertEqual(geometric_mean([.1,-1,.2]), -1)
    def test_09_variance(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(population_variance([.1,-.1]), .01)
    def test_10_approximation(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(approximate_geometric_mean([.1,-.1]), -.005)
    def test_11_roundtrip(self):
        rs=[.1,-.05,.02]
        self.assertAlmostEqual(equivalent_wealth(100,geometric_mean(rs),len(rs)),terminal_wealth(100,rs))
    def test_12_invalid(self):
        cases=[lambda:arithmetic_mean([]),lambda:geometric_mean([-1.01]),
               lambda:terminal_wealth(0,[.1]),lambda:equivalent_wealth(100,.1,0),
               lambda:population_variance([float("nan")])]
        for fn in cases:
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError): fn()

if __name__ == "__main__":
    returns=[.50,-.50]
    print("Arithmetic mean:", f"{arithmetic_mean(returns):.4%}")
    print("Geometric mean:", f"{geometric_mean(returns):.4%}")
    print("Cumulative return:", f"{cumulative_return(returns):.4%}")
    print("Terminal wealth:", f"${terminal_wealth(1000,returns):,.2f}")
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
```

## 15. Production and SRE considerations

- Do not mix daily, monthly, and annual returns in the same mean without a defined transformation.
- Preserve the original observation frequency and valuation convention.
- Distinguish gross-of-fees and net-of-fees returns.
- Handle corporate actions and distributions consistently before aggregation.
- Validate that geometric growth reconstructs cumulative return.
- Record whether variance uses n or n−1.
- Avoid interpreting a historical arithmetic mean as a guaranteed forecast.
- Flag −100% return events and reject non-finite or below-−100% long-only inputs.
- Report both arithmetic and geometric averages when their differing interpretations are material.

## 16. Completion and next section

Arithmetic mean returns summarize average single-period outcomes; geometric mean returns summarize historical compounded growth. The difference matters most when returns vary substantially.
