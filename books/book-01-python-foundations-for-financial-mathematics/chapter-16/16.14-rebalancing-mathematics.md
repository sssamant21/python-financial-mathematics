## Learning objectives

Calculate weight drift and portfolio returns; convert target allocations into trade sizes; distinguish one-way turnover from gross traded notional; model transaction costs and post-cost NAV; compare calendar and threshold rebalancing; and identify implementation risks involving cash flows, taxes, liquidity, and execution.

## 1. Why portfolios need rebalancing

Asset prices change at different rates. Even without trading, a portfolio's allocation drifts away from its initial weights. This may change expected return, volatility, sector concentration, and compliance with an investment mandate.

**Rebalancing** is the process of trading to restore or revise target exposures. Its purpose may be risk control, maintaining a policy allocation, or implementing a new investment decision.

Rebalancing is not guaranteed to improve realized returns. It can create trading costs, taxes, and market-impact risk.

## 2. Beginning weights and one-period returns

Let beginning-of-period asset weights be **wᵢ,0** with **Σᵢwᵢ,0=1**, and let asset i earn simple total return **Rᵢ**.

For a long-only, unleveraged portfolio with no external cash flows, the portfolio's simple total return is:

**Rₚ=Σᵢwᵢ,0Rᵢ**.

This assumes beginning weights are fixed during the period, with distributions treated consistently in total returns.

## 3. End-of-period position values

If initial portfolio NAV is **V₀**, the beginning position value of asset i is:

**Vᵢ,0=V₀wᵢ,0**.

After the asset return:

**Vᵢ,1=Vᵢ,0(1+Rᵢ)**.

Total ending NAV before trading and costs is:

**V₁=ΣᵢVᵢ,1=V₀(1+Rₚ)**.

This identity is the basis for drift and self-financing trade calculations.

## 4. Deriving weight drift

The new weight of asset i is its ending position value divided by total ending NAV:

**wᵢ,1=wᵢ,0(1+Rᵢ)/(1+Rₚ)**.

This formula requires positive ending NAV. It applies to simple total returns and a buy-and-hold, no-external-flow period.

The weight of an outperforming asset generally rises relative to the others.

## 5. Worked example — 50/50 portfolio

Suppose a portfolio begins with 50% in A and 50% in B.

Asset A earns +10% and B earns 0%.

**Rₚ=.5(.10)+.5(0)=.05**.

Starting from $100 NAV:

- A grows from $50 to $55.
- B remains at $50.
- Portfolio NAV becomes $105.

Ending weights are:

**w_A,1=55/105≈52.381%**.

**w_B,1=50/105≈47.619%**.

The portfolio has drifted from its 50/50 target.

## 6. From current weights to target weights

Let **w_current** be the weights immediately before rebalancing and **w_target** the desired allocation.

Ignoring costs and assuming a fully invested portfolio:

**Δwᵢ=w_target,ᵢ−w_current,ᵢ**.

The dollar trade is:

**Tradeᵢ=V×Δwᵢ**,

where V is pre-trade NAV.

A positive trade is a buy; a negative trade is a sale.

## 7. Worked example — restoring target weights

Current weights: [.4,.6]. Target weights: [.5,.5]. NAV: $100.

**Δw=[+.1,−.1]**.

Thus:

- Buy $10 of A.
- Sell $10 of B.

Total buys and sells both equal $10 in the frictionless, self-financing model.

Actual order sizes may differ due to commissions, spreads, taxes, lot sizes, and price movements during execution.

## 8. One-way turnover

A common one-way turnover definition for a self-financing, fully invested rebalance is:

**T=.5Σᵢ|w_target,ᵢ−w_current,ᵢ|**.

For [.4,.6] to [.5,.5]:

**T=.5(.1+.1)=.1=10% of NAV**.

The one-half factor avoids counting both the sale and the purchase as separate turnover in the summary statistic.

Some reports instead use total buys plus sells. Always state which convention is used.

## 9. Gross traded notional

The gross dollar amount traded is:

**GrossTraded=Σᵢ|Tradeᵢ|**.

Under a frictionless self-financing rebalance:

**GrossTraded=2VT**.

For NAV=$100 and one-way turnover=.1:

**GrossTraded=$20**.

A broker's fee schedule may charge on both buy and sell notional, so cost models must use the correct trading volume definition.

## 10. Transaction-cost approximation

A simple proportional transaction-cost model is:

**Cost=cΣᵢ|Tradeᵢ|**,

where c is a per-dollar traded rate.

For $20 gross traded notional and c=.001 (0.1%):

**Cost≈$0.02**.

This approximation uses trade amounts computed from pre-cost NAV. If target weights must be exact **after** costs, trade amounts and cost must be solved together.

## 11. Exact post-cost NAV accounting

Let **Hᵢ** be the current dollar holding, **V=ΣᵢHᵢ** pre-cost NAV, **tᵢ** target weights, and **C** total proportional transaction cost.

The target post-cost position value is:

**Hᵢ,new=tᵢ(V−C)**.

Required trade:

**qᵢ=tᵢ(V−C)−Hᵢ**.

With common proportional cost rate c on absolute traded notional:

**C=cΣᵢ|qᵢ|=cΣᵢ|tᵢ(V−C)−Hᵢ|**.

This is a fixed-point equation. The reference implementation solves it numerically by bisection under long-only, positive-NAV assumptions.

The resulting trades satisfy **Σᵢqᵢ=−C**, meaning sales finance both purchases and costs.

## 12. Why pre-cost targets can be misleading

Suppose a portfolio buys and sells to reach exact pre-cost target dollars, then deducts costs from cash or NAV. The post-cost allocation may no longer equal the desired target.

A cost-aware execution engine should specify whether targets are measured against pre-trade NAV, post-cost NAV, or a cash-reserved NAV.

The accounting convention is part of the strategy definition, not an implementation detail.

## 13. Calendar-based rebalancing

A calendar policy rebalances at predefined intervals, such as monthly, quarterly, or annually.

Advantages include predictable operational scheduling and clear governance.

Disadvantages include unnecessary trades when weights remain near targets and delayed correction when a major drift occurs between scheduled dates.

Calendar frequency should be evaluated against portfolio risk, transaction costs, taxes, and market liquidity.

## 14. Threshold-based rebalancing

A threshold policy trades when a weight deviates beyond a specified band.

For an **absolute weight band** b:

**Trigger if maxᵢ|w_current,ᵢ−w_target,ᵢ|>b**.

For a 50% target and a five-percentage-point band, weights outside 45%–55% trigger.

A **relative band** instead measures deviation as a percentage of target weight; it requires special treatment when the target weight is zero.

## 15. Calendar plus threshold policy

A hybrid policy may evaluate portfolio weights at scheduled dates but trade only if a threshold is exceeded.

This reduces some unnecessary trading while preserving a regular review process.

A separate emergency breach rule can trigger risk reduction outside the normal schedule.

The choice of band and review frequency should be validated with realistic execution assumptions.

## 16. Partial rebalancing

A portfolio does not always need to trade fully back to target.

A partial rebalance may move only part of the way:

**w_new=w_current+α(w_target−w_current)**,

where **0≤α≤1**.

For α=.5, the portfolio closes half of each weight gap before costs.

Partial rebalancing can reduce turnover but leaves residual allocation drift.

## 17. Cash flows, dividends, and distributions

External deposits and withdrawals must be separated from investment performance.

A deposit changes portfolio NAV and may provide cash to rebalance without selling existing assets. A withdrawal may force sales.

Dividends may be reinvested, held as cash, or distributed. The chosen convention affects weight drift and turnover.

A performance calculation should not count an investor deposit as portfolio return.

## 18. Taxes and tax-aware rebalancing

Selling appreciated holdings can realize taxable gains. Tax-aware rebalancing may consider holding periods, cost basis, loss harvesting, and account restrictions.

Tax treatment varies by jurisdiction and investor. The educational formulas here exclude taxes; they are not tax advice.

A portfolio can satisfy a target allocation before taxes yet be economically worse after tax costs.

## 19. Bid-ask spreads and market impact

Transaction costs are not always proportional.

Trading costs can include:

- Commissions and exchange fees.
- Bid-ask spreads.
- Market impact and slippage.
- Borrowing and financing costs.
- Taxes.
- Settlement and currency conversion costs.

Large trades relative to market liquidity may experience nonlinear costs. A production engine should estimate execution costs using asset-specific assumptions.

## 20. Rebalancing and portfolio risk

Because weights determine portfolio variance:

**σₚ²=wᵀΣw**,

weight drift can change risk even if covariance estimates stay constant.

After a strong equity rally, equity exposure may rise, increasing sensitivity to equity market declines.

Rebalancing can restore risk limits, but a fixed target is not automatically optimal under changing market conditions.

## 21. Rebalancing and portfolio constraints

Before generating trades, validate target weights against position limits, sector caps, liquidity requirements, leverage restrictions, and turnover budgets.

After execution, reconcile actual holdings and recheck the constraints. Rounding, partial fills, costs, and price movement can create differences from planned allocations.

Document approved exceptions and operational ownership.

## 22. Return attribution and backtesting

To compare buy-and-hold and rebalanced strategies fairly:

1. Use identical starting assets and initial NAV.
2. Apply the same historical return observations.
3. Rebalance only at permitted decision dates.
4. Use information available at each decision time.
5. Apply consistent trading costs, taxes, and cash-flow conventions.
6. Reconcile NAV and holdings at each period.
7. Compare risk and return out of sample.

Rebalancing does not create a guaranteed return premium. Outcomes depend on the return path, correlation, timing, and costs.

## 23. Python reference implementation

Save as `rebalancing_mathematics.py`. This standard-library reference calculates portfolio returns, drifted weights, target trade plans, one-way turnover, absolute-band triggers, and exact post-cost target allocations under a common proportional transaction-cost rate. It includes **12 reference unit tests**.

```python
"""Rebalancing mathematics: long-only, self-financing educational model."""
import math
import unittest

def vec(x,name):
    if not isinstance(x,(list,tuple)) or not x:raise ValueError(name+" must be nonempty")
    if any(type(v) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(v) for v in x):
        raise ValueError(name+" must contain finite values")
    return [float(v) for v in x]

def weights(x):
    w=vec(x,"weights")
    if any(v<0 for v in w) or not math.isclose(math.fsum(w),1,rel_tol=0,abs_tol=1e-10):
        raise ValueError("long-only weights must sum to one")
    return w

def drift(start,returns):
    w=weights(start);r=vec(returns,"returns")
    if len(w)!=len(r) or any(x < -1 for x in r):
        raise ValueError("aligned simple returns >= -100% required")
    holdings=[w[i]*(1+r[i]) for i in range(len(w))]
    gross=math.fsum(holdings)
    if gross<=0:raise ValueError("NAV exhausted")
    return {"portfolio_return":gross-1,"ending_weights":[x/gross for x in holdings],
            "ending_nav_per_unit":gross}

def trade_plan(current,target,nav):
    a=weights(current);b=weights(target)
    if len(a)!=len(b) or type(nav) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(nav) or nav<=0:
        raise ValueError("aligned weights and positive NAV required")
    delta=[b[i]-a[i] for i in range(len(a))]
    dollars=[nav*x for x in delta]
    buy=math.fsum(max(0,x) for x in dollars)
    sell=math.fsum(max(0,-x) for x in dollars)
    return {"trade_weights":delta,"trade_dollars":dollars,
            "buys":buy,"sells":sell,
            "one_way_turnover":.5*math.fsum(abs(x) for x in delta)}

def net_after_proportional_cost(current,target,nav,cost_rate):
    # Exact solution when proportional cost is charged on traded dollar notional.
    # Post-cost target holdings = target_i * (nav - cost).
    a=weights(current);b=weights(target)
    if len(a)!=len(b) or type(nav) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(nav) or nav<=0:
        raise ValueError("aligned weights and positive NAV required")
    if type(cost_rate) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(cost_rate) or not 0<=cost_rate<1:
        raise ValueError("cost rate must be in [0,1)")
    holdings=[nav*x for x in a]
    # Piecewise-linear fixed-point bisection: cost = c * sum |b_i*(nav-cost)-holdings_i|.
    def residual(cost):
        post_nav=nav-cost
        return cost-cost_rate*math.fsum(abs(b[i]*post_nav-holdings[i]) for i in range(len(a)))
    lo,hi=0.0,nav
    if residual(hi)<0:raise ValueError("cost equation has no feasible post-cost NAV")
    for _ in range(100):
        mid=(lo+hi)/2
        if residual(mid)>=0:hi=mid
        else:lo=mid
    cost=(lo+hi)/2
    post_nav=nav-cost
    trades=[b[i]*post_nav-holdings[i] for i in range(len(a))]
    return {"cost":cost,"post_cost_nav":post_nav,"trade_dollars":trades,
            "post_cost_weights":b,"total_traded":math.fsum(abs(x) for x in trades)}

def band_trigger(current,target,tolerance):
    a=weights(current);b=weights(target)
    if len(a)!=len(b) or type(tolerance) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(tolerance) or tolerance<0:
        raise ValueError("aligned weights and nonnegative tolerance required")
    return any(abs(x-y)>tolerance for x,y in zip(a,b))

class RebalancingTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_01_return(self):self.assertAlmostEqual(drift([.5,.5],[.1,0])["portfolio_return"],.05)
    def test_02_drift(self):self.assertAlmostEqual(drift([.5,.5],[.1,0])["ending_weights"][0],.55/1.05)
    def test_03_no_change(self):self.assertEqual(drift([.4,.6],[0,0])["ending_weights"],[.4,.6])
    def test_04_turnover(self):self.assertAlmostEqual(trade_plan([.4,.6],[.5,.5],100)["one_way_turnover"],.1)
    def test_05_buy(self):self.assertAlmostEqual(trade_plan([.4,.6],[.5,.5],100)["buys"],10)
    def test_06_sell(self):self.assertAlmostEqual(trade_plan([.4,.6],[.5,.5],100)["sells"],10)
    def test_07_cost_zero(self):self.assertAlmostEqual(net_after_proportional_cost([.4,.6],[.5,.5],100,0)["cost"],0,places=10)
    def test_08_cost_positive(self):
        r=net_after_proportional_cost([.4,.6],[.5,.5],100,.001)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(r["cost"],.001*r["total_traded"],places=10)
    def test_09_cost_accounting(self):
        r=net_after_proportional_cost([.4,.6],[.5,.5],100,.001)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(r["post_cost_nav"]+r["cost"],100)
        self.assertAlmostEqual(math.fsum(r["trade_dollars"]),-r["cost"],places=10)
    def test_10_trigger(self):self.assertTrue(band_trigger([.4,.6],[.5,.5],.05))
    def test_11_no_trigger(self):self.assertFalse(band_trigger([.49,.51],[.5,.5],.02))
    def test_12_invalid(self):
        for f in (lambda:drift([.5,.5],[-1,-1]),
                  lambda:drift([.5,.5],[0]),
                  lambda:trade_plan([.5,.5],[.6,.4],0),
                  lambda:net_after_proportional_cost([.5,.5],[.5,.5],100,1),
                  lambda:band_trigger([.5,.5],[.5,.5],-.1)):
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError):f()

if __name__=="__main__":
    print("Drift:",drift([.5,.5],[.1,0]))
    print("Rebalance:",trade_plan([.4,.6],[.5,.5],100))
    print("After costs:",net_after_proportional_cost([.4,.6],[.5,.5],100,.001))
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
```

## 26. Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Corrective action |
|---|---|---|
| Ending weights do not sum to one | Incorrect NAV normalization | Divide by total ending NAV |
| Portfolio return includes deposits | External flow counted as performance | Separate flows from investment return |
| Turnover differs across reports | One-way versus gross-traded convention | Document and reconcile definitions |
| Post-cost target weights are wrong | Pre-cost target sizing | Solve cost and trades jointly |
| Trades do not fund fees | Costs omitted from cash equation | Reconcile sum of trades to negative fees |
| Rebalance trigger differs | Absolute versus relative band | Specify threshold convention |
| Simulated performance too strong | Costs or slippage omitted | Add realistic execution assumptions |
| Rebalance breaches limits | Missing post-trade constraint check | Validate actual holdings after fills |
| Leveraged account gives strange drift | Long-only model applied to signed weights | Use leveraged NAV accounting |
