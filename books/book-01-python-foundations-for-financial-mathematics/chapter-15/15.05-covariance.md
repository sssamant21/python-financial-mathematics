## 1. Why covariance matters

Variance describes dispersion within one return series. **Covariance** describes whether deviations of two aligned return series from their respective means tend to have the same sign or opposite signs. It is fundamental to diversification, portfolio risk, factor models, hedging, and multivariate financial statistics.

Covariance is **not** a measure of causality, and its magnitude depends on the scale of the underlying variables. It also does not establish that the relationship will persist.

## 2. Data alignment is a prerequisite

Suppose we observe two hypothetical assets over the **same three dates**:

| Date | Asset A return | Asset B return |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1% | 2% |
| 2 | 2% | 4% |
| 3 | 3% | 6% |

These are paired observations. A covariance calculation is invalid if the two series have been independently sorted, joined to different dates, or mixed across incompatible frequencies. Align timestamps and calendars first; decide how missing and stale prices are handled; and record whether returns are simple or logarithmic.

## 3. Population covariance

For n aligned pairs (xᵢ,yᵢ), the empirical population-denominator covariance is:

**Covₙ(X,Y) = (1/n) Σ(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ).**

For the example, x̄=.02 and ȳ=.04. The deviations are:

| Date | A deviation | B deviation | Product |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | −.01 | −.02 | .0002 |
| 2 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 3 | .01 | .02 | .0002 |
| **Total** | 0 | 0 | **.0004** |

Thus **Covₙ(A,B)=.0004/3≈.0001333333** in squared decimal-return units.

## 4. Sample covariance

The conventional sample covariance uses the denominator n−1:

**sₓᵧ = [1/(n−1)] Σ(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ)**, for n≥2.

In our example, **sₓᵧ=.0004/2=.0002**.

The n−1 correction yields the usual unbiased covariance estimator under iid paired sampling with finite second moments. For dependent time series, the usual estimator and its uncertainty require additional care.

A single paired observation does not provide a defined sample covariance.

## 5. Interpreting the sign and magnitude

- **Positive covariance:** the two series tend to move above or below their means together.
- **Negative covariance:** their mean-centered movements tend to have opposite signs.
- **Zero covariance:** there is no linear covariance in the observed data; nonlinear dependence may remain.

Covariance is measured in the **product of the input units**. If returns are in decimal units, covariance is in decimal-return squared. Converting both return series to percentages multiplies covariance by **100²=10,000**.

For example, .0002 in decimal-return² equals **2 squared percentage points**.

## 6. Relation to correlation

For nonzero standard deviations:

**ρₓᵧ = Cov(X,Y)/(σₓσᵧ).**

The same formula applies to consistently defined sample covariance and sample standard deviations. Correlation is dimensionless and lies between −1 and +1.

Here, sample variance of A is .0001 and of B is .0004. Therefore:

**r=.0002/√(.0001×.0004)=1.**

The perfect positive sample correlation follows from B's returns being exactly twice A's returns in this constructed dataset. It does not imply a real-world forecast.

## 7. Covariance properties

For constants a,b,c,d:

**Cov(aX+b,cY+d)=ac Cov(X,Y).**

Also:

**Cov(X,Y)=Cov(Y,X)**, and **Cov(X,X)=Var(X)**.

If one series is constant, its covariance with any other finite paired series is zero; its correlation is undefined because its SD is zero.

The Cauchy–Schwarz bound requires:

**|Cov(X,Y)| ≤ SD(X)×SD(Y).**

This bound is an important numerical and modeling sanity check.

## 8. Covariance matrices

For two assets, the sample covariance matrix is:

[
\Sigma =
\begin{bmatrix}
s_A^2 & s_{AB} \\
s_{AB} & s_B^2
\end{bmatrix}.
]

For our example:

[
\Sigma =
\begin{bmatrix}
0.0001 & 0.0002 \\
0.0002 & 0.0004
\end{bmatrix}.
]

A valid covariance matrix is symmetric and positive semidefinite. The matrix above is singular because the two assets are perfectly linearly dependent in the sample.

For a portfolio weight vector w, portfolio variance is **wᵀΣw**.

## 9. Portfolio risk example

For two assets with weights w_A=w_B=.5:

**Var(Rₚ)=w_A²Var(A)+w_B²Var(B)+2w_Aw_BCov(A,B).**

Substituting the sample statistics:

**Var(Rₚ)=.25(.0001)+.25(.0004)+.5(.0002)=.000225.**

**SD(Rₚ)=√.000225=.015=1.5%.**

The example has perfect positive correlation, so it does not illustrate diversification benefits. Lower covariance, holding the marginal variances and weights fixed, would reduce the variance of a positive-weight two-asset portfolio.

## 10. Estimation pitfalls

Historical covariance can change with volatility regimes, market structure, sampling frequency, and stress conditions. Short histories produce unstable estimates, especially when the number of assets is large relative to the number of observations.

Common safeguards include aligned observation calendars, consistent return definitions, data-quality controls, sufficient history, stress testing, and explicit documentation of any shrinkage or regularization method. More advanced estimators appear in later portfolio and numerical chapters.

## 11. Python reference implementation

Save as `covariance.py`. The implementation validates aligned observations, exposes the population/sample denominator, computes covariance and correlation, constructs a covariance matrix, and includes **12 reference unit tests**.

```python
import math
import unittest

def paired_returns(a, b):
    if not isinstance(a, (list, tuple)) or not isinstance(b, (list, tuple)):
        raise ValueError("two sequences required")
    if len(a) != len(b) or not a:
        raise ValueError("nonempty aligned equal-length observations required")
    if any(type(x) not in (int, float) or not math.isfinite(x) for x in list(a)+list(b)):
        raise ValueError("finite numeric observations required")
    return [float(x) for x in a], [float(x) for x in b]

def covariance(a, b, ddof=1):
    x, y = paired_returns(a, b)
    if type(ddof) is not int or ddof not in (0, 1):
        raise ValueError("ddof must be 0 or 1")
    n = len(x)
    if n <= ddof:
        raise ValueError("insufficient observations")
    mx = math.fsum(x)/n
    my = math.fsum(y)/n
    return math.fsum((u-mx)*(v-my) for u, v in zip(x,y))/(n-ddof)

def variance(a, ddof=1):
    return covariance(a, a, ddof)

def correlation(a, b):
    vx = variance(a)
    vy = variance(b)
    if vx == 0 or vy == 0:
        raise ValueError("correlation undefined for constant series")
    result = covariance(a,b)/math.sqrt(vx*vy)
    return max(-1.0,min(1.0,result))

def two_asset_variance(a, b, weight_a, ddof=1):
    if type(weight_a) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(weight_a):
        raise ValueError("finite weight required")
    w = float(weight_a)
    return w*w*variance(a,ddof)+(1-w)**2*variance(b,ddof)+2*w*(1-w)*covariance(a,b,ddof)

def covariance_matrix(series, ddof=1):
    if not isinstance(series,(list,tuple)) or not series:
        raise ValueError("nonempty collection required")
    arrays = [paired_returns(s,s)[0] for s in series]
    if len({len(s) for s in arrays}) != 1:
        raise ValueError("all series must have equal length")
    return [[covariance(x,y,ddof) for y in arrays] for x in arrays]

class CovarianceTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def setUp(self):
        self.a=[.01,.02,.03]
        self.b=[.02,.04,.06]
    def test_01_sample_covariance(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(covariance(self.a,self.b),.0002)
    def test_02_population_covariance(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(covariance(self.a,self.b,0),.0004/3)
    def test_03_sample_variance(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(variance(self.a),.0001)
    def test_04_symmetry(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(covariance(self.a,self.b),covariance(self.b,self.a))
    def test_05_perfect_positive(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(correlation(self.a,self.b),1)
    def test_06_perfect_negative(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(correlation(self.a,[-v for v in self.b]),-1)
    def test_07_shift_invariance(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(covariance(self.a,self.b),covariance([v+1 for v in self.a],self.b))
    def test_08_scaling(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(covariance(self.a,[2*v for v in self.b]),2*covariance(self.a,self.b))
    def test_09_portfolio(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(two_asset_variance(self.a,self.b,.5),.000225)
    def test_10_matrix(self):
        m=covariance_matrix([self.a,self.b])
        self.assertAlmostEqual(m[0][1],m[1][0])
        self.assertAlmostEqual(m[1][1],.0004)
    def test_11_constant_covariance(self): self.assertEqual(covariance([1,1,1],self.a),0)
    def test_12_invalid(self):
        for fn in (lambda:covariance([],[]),lambda:covariance([1],[1]),
                   lambda:covariance([1,2],[1]),lambda:covariance([1,float("nan")],[1,2]),
                   lambda:correlation([1,1,1],[1,2,3]),
                   lambda:covariance_matrix([[1,2,3],[1,2]])):
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError): fn()

if __name__ == "__main__":
    a=[.01,.02,.03]
    b=[.02,.04,.06]
    print("Sample covariance:",covariance(a,b))
    print("Sample correlation:",correlation(a,b))
    print("Covariance matrix:",covariance_matrix([a,b]))
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
```

## 13. Twenty-four worked exercises

1. **Means:** A=1%,2%,3%; B=2%,4%,6%. **Worked solution:** Mean A=2%, mean B=4%.

2. **Deviations A:** A=1%,2%,3%. **Worked solution:** −1,0,1 percentage points.

3. **Deviations B:** B=2%,4%,6%. **Worked solution:** −2,0,2 percentage points.

4. **Cross products:** Aligned A and B deviations. **Worked solution:** 2,0,2 squared percentage points; total=4.

5. **Sample covariance:** n=3, cross-product sum=.0004. **Worked solution:** .0004/(3−1)=.0002.

6. **Population covariance:** Same sample. **Worked solution:** .0004/3≈.0001333333.

7. **Variance A:** A=1%,2%,3%. **Worked solution:** Sample variance=.0001.

8. **Variance B:** B=2%,4%,6%. **Worked solution:** Sample variance=.0004.

9. **Correlation:** Cov=.0002, variances=.0001,.0004. **Worked solution:** .0002/√(.0001×.0004)=1.

10. **Negative covariance:** B reflected as −2%,−4%,−6%. **Worked solution:** Sample covariance=−.0002.

11. **Symmetry:** Swap A and B. **Worked solution:** Cov(A,B)=Cov(B,A).

12. **Translation:** Add 5 percentage points to A. **Worked solution:** Covariance unchanged.

13. **Scaling:** Multiply B by 3. **Worked solution:** Covariance triples.

14. **Constant series:** A=2%,2%,2%. **Worked solution:** Covariance with any aligned finite series is zero.

15. **Correlation undefined:** A constant. **Worked solution:** SD(A)=0, so correlation undefined.

16. **Two-asset variance:** Weights .5/.5; var A=.0001; var B=.0004; cov=.0002. **Worked solution:** .25(.0001)+.25(.0004)+.5(.0002)=.000225.

17. **Portfolio SD:** Variance .000225. **Worked solution:** SD=.015=1.5%.

18. **Matrix diagonal:** Assets A and B. **Worked solution:** Diagonal entries are .0001 and .0004.

19. **Matrix symmetry:** Two-asset matrix. **Worked solution:** Off-diagonal entries both .0002.

20. **Cauchy–Schwarz:** σA=.01, σB=.02. **Worked solution:** |covariance|≤.0002.

21. **Diversification:** Positive covariance. **Worked solution:** May reduce diversification benefits compared with lower covariance, holding variances fixed.

22. **Misalignment:** Different trading calendars. **Worked solution:** Join on shared timestamps before covariance; do not zip unaligned returns.

23. **Sample size:** One paired observation. **Worked solution:** Sample covariance undefined; population-denominator empirical covariance=0.

24. **Unit conversion:** Covariance=.0002 decimal-return². **Worked solution:** 2 squared percentage points because .0002×10000=2.

## 14. Troubleshooting and operational checks

| Symptom | Likely cause | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|
| Covariance differs between tools | Population versus sample denominator | Specify ddof |
| Magnitude differs by 10,000 | Decimal versus percentage returns | Reconcile squared units |
| Correlation exceeds 1 in magnitude | Inconsistent moments or numeric error | Verify paired data and denominator |
| Covariance matrix is asymmetric | Calculation or alignment bug | Recompute from one aligned table |
| Singular covariance matrix | Redundant assets or insufficient observations | Inspect rank and regularization needs |
| Sudden covariance regime change | Market event or source defect | Segment periods and audit data |
| Positive covariance interpreted as causality | Statistical overreach | Avoid causal claims |
| Missing days silently filled with zero | Misaligned returns | Document join and missing-value policy |
