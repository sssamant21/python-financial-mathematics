## Learning objectives

Construct and interpret confidence intervals for financial statistics; distinguish standard errors from standard deviations; compare normal-approximation, Student's t, bootstrap percentile, and Wilson proportion intervals; explain confidence level and coverage; and identify dependence, heavy tails, and selection biases that invalidate naive intervals.

## 1. Why interval estimates matter

A point estimate such as a historical mean return conceals estimation uncertainty. A **confidence interval (CI)** is a data-dependent interval constructed by a procedure designed to cover a fixed population parameter at a specified long-run frequency under its assumptions.

For example, a 95% confidence procedure has 95% coverage across hypothetical repeated samples when its model assumptions hold. It does **not** mean a fixed unknown parameter has a 95% frequentist probability of being inside a particular realized interval.

In financial data, short histories, changing regimes, serial dependence, and non-normal returns can make textbook coverage inaccurate.

## 2. From sampling error to confidence interval

Let X₁,…,Xₙ be observations, x̄ their mean, and s their sample standard deviation.

For iid observations with finite variance, the conventional estimated standard error is:

**SE(x̄)=s/√n.**

The standard deviation s describes dispersion of individual observations. The standard error describes estimated uncertainty in the sample mean.

A generic symmetric interval has the form:

**estimate ± critical value × estimated standard error**.

The critical value and validity depend on the sampling distribution and assumptions.

## 3. Normal-approximation interval for a mean

Under a justified large-sample normal approximation, a nominal two-sided 100(1−α)% interval is:

**x̄ ± z₁₋α/₂ × s/√n.**

For a nominal 95% interval, z₀.₉₇₅≈1.9599639845.

### Worked example

Consider hypothetical simple returns **[.01,.02,.03]**.

- Mean: x̄=.02.
- Sample SD: s=.01.
- Estimated standard error: .01/√3≈.00577350.
- Normal 95% half-width: 1.959964×.00577350≈.011316.
- Normal-approximation interval: approximately **[.008684,.031316]**, or **[0.8684%,3.1316%]**.

**Warning:** n=3 is far too small to justify a generic normal approximation. This calculation illustrates the formula; it is not a recommended inference procedure for three arbitrary financial returns.

## 4. Student's t interval for a mean

If observations are independent draws from a normal population with unknown variance, the exact classical two-sided interval is:

**x̄ ± t₁₋α/₂,ₙ₋₁ × s/√n**.

For n=3, the degrees of freedom are 2, and the two-sided 95% t critical value is approximately **4.30265273**.

Using the same sample:

**.02 ± 4.30265273(.01/√3)**

gives approximately **[−.00484,.04484]**, or **[−0.484%,4.484%]**.

The t interval is wider than the z interval because the standard deviation is estimated from only three observations.

**Important:** Student's t does not magically correct non-normality, autocorrelation, or volatility clustering. The exact finite-sample t result requires its assumptions.

## 5. Choosing a confidence level

For a fixed standard error and symmetric normal procedure:

| Nominal confidence level | Approximate two-sided z critical |
|---|---:|
| 90% | 1.644854 |
| 95% | 1.959964 |
| 99% | 2.575829 |

Higher confidence generally produces wider intervals. The choice should reflect the decision context, not be selected after looking at results to obtain a desired conclusion.

## 6. Bootstrap percentile confidence interval

The ordinary nonparametric iid bootstrap approximates estimator variability by repeatedly resampling observations **with replacement**.

1. Begin with n observed returns.
2. Draw n returns with replacement to form one bootstrap sample.
3. Calculate the statistic, such as its mean.
4. Repeat B times.
5. Sort the B bootstrap statistics.
6. Use the empirical α/2 and 1−α/2 quantiles as interval endpoints.

For a 95% percentile interval, use the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. The reference implementation uses **linear interpolation at index (B−1)p**, commonly called type-7 quantiles.

Bootstrap percentile intervals are not universally accurate: bias, skewness, small samples, discrete statistics, dependence, and boundary parameters can cause poor coverage. More advanced alternatives include studentized and BCa intervals, but these require their own validity checks.

For financial time series, resampling isolated daily returns generally destroys serial dependence. A justified block bootstrap or dependence-aware analytical method may be preferable.

## 7. Confidence intervals for proportions

A financial operations example might estimate the fraction of trading sessions with a breach of a predefined risk limit.

For x successes in n independent Bernoulli trials, the naive Wald interval:

**p̂ ± z√[p̂(1−p̂)/n]**

can behave badly, especially near p̂=0 or 1 or with small n.

A more reliable common alternative is the **Wilson score interval**:

Let p̂=x/n, z=z₁₋α/₂, and D=1+z²/n.

**Center = [p̂+z²/(2n)]/D**.

**Half-width = z√[p̂(1−p̂)/n+z²/(4n²)]/D**.

The Wilson interval is center ± half-width.

For **0 breaches in 10 sessions**, the 95% Wilson upper bound is still positive; observing zero events does not establish a zero underlying breach probability. The iid Bernoulli assumptions remain important.

## 8. Confidence interval versus prediction interval

A **confidence interval for a mean** quantifies uncertainty in an estimated population mean.

A **prediction interval** targets a future observation (or a future aggregate), incorporating both estimation uncertainty and observation-level variability under a predictive model.

A 95% CI for mean return is not a range containing 95% of next-day returns. This distinction is especially important when market returns have fat tails or time-varying volatility.

## 9. Financial assumptions and limitations

| Issue | Why it matters | Better practice |
|---|---|---|
| Autocorrelation | iid standard error may misstate uncertainty | Consider HAC/Newey–West or justified block bootstrap |
| Volatility clustering | Return variance changes through time | Diagnose regimes and conditional volatility |
| Heavy tails | Normal approximations can be poor | Use robust diagnostics and stress analysis |
| Very small n | Estimated uncertainty is unstable | Report limitations and seek more data |
| Selection bias | Interval centers on wrong population | Correct data universe and inclusion rules |
| Look-ahead bias | Future information contaminates history | Enforce point-in-time datasets |
| Multiple comparisons | Repeated searches inflate false discoveries | Plan analyses and adjust inference |
| Missing values | Sample selection may be nonrandom | Document missingness and sensitivity |

A confidence interval cannot repair an invalid sampling frame, data leakage, or a misspecified estimand.

## 10. Python reference implementation

Save as `confidence_intervals.py`. The code uses only Python's standard library and includes **12 reference unit tests**. For t intervals, the critical value is passed explicitly to avoid implying that the standard library provides a Student-t quantile function.

```python
import math
import random
import statistics
import unittest

def finite_sample(values):
    if not isinstance(values, (list, tuple)) or not values:
        raise ValueError("nonempty sequence required")
    if any(type(x) not in (int, float) or not math.isfinite(x) for x in values):
        raise ValueError("finite numeric observations required")
    return [float(x) for x in values]

def sample_mean_se(values):
    xs = finite_sample(values)
    if len(xs) < 2:
        raise ValueError("at least two observations required")
    mean = math.fsum(xs) / len(xs)
    ss = math.fsum((x-mean)**2 for x in xs)
    sd = math.sqrt(ss / (len(xs)-1))
    return mean, sd / math.sqrt(len(xs))

def normal_approx_mean_ci(values, z=1.959963984540054):
    if type(z) not in (int, float) or not math.isfinite(z) or z <= 0:
        raise ValueError("positive finite critical value required")
    mean, se = sample_mean_se(values)
    return (mean-z*se, mean+z*se)

def t_mean_ci(values, t_critical):
    if type(t_critical) not in (int, float) or not math.isfinite(t_critical) or t_critical <= 0:
        raise ValueError("positive finite t critical value required")
    mean, se = sample_mean_se(values)
    return (mean-t_critical*se, mean+t_critical*se)

def percentile(sorted_values, probability):
    xs = finite_sample(sorted_values)
    if xs != sorted(xs) or type(probability) not in (int,float) or not 0 <= probability <= 1:
        raise ValueError("sorted data and probability in [0,1] required")
    h=(len(xs)-1)*probability
    lo=math.floor(h)
    hi=math.ceil(h)
    return xs[lo] + (h-lo)*(xs[hi]-xs[lo])

def bootstrap_percentile_ci(values, replicates=2000, alpha=.05, seed=42):
    xs=finite_sample(values)
    if len(xs)<2 or type(replicates) is not int or replicates<2:
        raise ValueError("at least two observations and replicates required")
    if type(alpha) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(alpha) or not 0<alpha<1:
        raise ValueError("alpha must be between zero and one")
    rng=random.Random(seed)
    n=len(xs)
    estimates=sorted(math.fsum(rng.choices(xs,k=n))/n for _ in range(replicates))
    return (percentile(estimates,alpha/2),percentile(estimates,1-alpha/2))

def wilson_proportion_ci(successes, trials, z=1.959963984540054):
    if type(successes) is not int or type(trials) is not int or trials<1 or not 0<=successes<=trials:
        raise ValueError("require integer 0 <= successes <= trials")
    if type(z) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(z) or z<=0:
        raise ValueError("positive finite z required")
    p=successes/trials
    z2=z*z
    denom=1+z2/trials
    center=(p+z2/(2*trials))/denom
    half=z*math.sqrt(p*(1-p)/trials+z2/(4*trials*trials))/denom
    return center-half,center+half

class ConfidenceIntervalTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def setUp(self): self.x=[.01,.02,.03]
    def test_01_mean(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(sample_mean_se(self.x)[0],.02)
    def test_02_se(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(sample_mean_se(self.x)[1],.01/math.sqrt(3))
    def test_03_normal_center(self):
        lo,hi=normal_approx_mean_ci(self.x)
        self.assertAlmostEqual((lo+hi)/2,.02)
    def test_04_normal_halfwidth(self):
        lo,hi=normal_approx_mean_ci(self.x)
        self.assertAlmostEqual((hi-lo)/2,1.959963984540054*.01/math.sqrt(3))
    def test_05_t_critical(self):
        lo,hi=t_mean_ci(self.x,4.302652729911275)
        self.assertAlmostEqual((hi-lo)/2,4.302652729911275*.01/math.sqrt(3))
    def test_06_percentile_endpoints(self):
        self.assertEqual(percentile([1,2,3],0),1)
        self.assertEqual(percentile([1,2,3],1),3)
    def test_07_percentile_mid(self): self.assertEqual(percentile([1,2,3],.5),2)
    def test_08_bootstrap_order(self):
        lo,hi=bootstrap_percentile_ci(self.x,200,seed=11)
        self.assertLessEqual(lo,hi)
    def test_09_bootstrap_reproducibility(self):
        self.assertEqual(bootstrap_percentile_ci(self.x,200,seed=11),
                         bootstrap_percentile_ci(self.x,200,seed=11))
    def test_10_wilson_zero(self):
        lo,hi=wilson_proportion_ci(0,10)
        self.assertGreaterEqual(lo,-1e-14)
        self.assertGreater(hi,0)
    def test_11_wilson_full(self):
        lo,hi=wilson_proportion_ci(10,10)
        self.assertLess(lo,1)
        self.assertLessEqual(hi,1+1e-14)
    def test_12_invalid(self):
        for f in (lambda:sample_mean_se([1]),
                  lambda:normal_approx_mean_ci(self.x,-1),
                  lambda:t_mean_ci(self.x,0),
                  lambda:bootstrap_percentile_ci(self.x,1),
                  lambda:bootstrap_percentile_ci(self.x,200,1),
                  lambda:wilson_proportion_ci(11,10),
                  lambda:sample_mean_se([1,float("nan")])):
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError): f()

if __name__=="__main__":
    x=[.01,.02,.03]
    print("Normal approximation:",normal_approx_mean_ci(x))
    print("Student t (df=2):",t_mean_ci(x,4.302652729911275))
    print("Bootstrap percentile:",bootstrap_percentile_ci(x))
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
```

## 12. Twenty-four worked exercises

1. **Point estimate:** Returns 1%,2%,3%. **Worked solution:** Mean=2%.

2. **Sample SD:** Same returns. **Worked solution:** s=1 percentage point.

3. **Standard error:** Same returns. **Worked solution:** SE=1%/√3≈0.57735 percentage points.

4. **Normal 95% half-width:** SE=.00577350. **Worked solution:** 1.959964×SE≈.011316.

5. **Normal 95% interval:** Mean=.02, SE=.00577350. **Worked solution:** Approximately [.008684,.031316].

6. **t degrees of freedom:** n=3. **Worked solution:** df=n−1=2.

7. **t critical:** 95% two-sided, df=2. **Worked solution:** t≈4.302653.

8. **t half-width:** SE=.00577350, t≈4.302653. **Worked solution:** Half-width≈.02484.

9. **t interval:** Mean=.02, n=3. **Worked solution:** Approximately [−.00484,.04484].

10. **Width comparison:** Same n=3 sample. **Worked solution:** The t interval is wider than the z interval.

11. **Coverage:** Nominal 95% confidence procedure. **Worked solution:** Under assumptions, approximately 95% of intervals across repetitions cover the fixed target.

12. **Interpretation:** Observed interval [a,b]. **Worked solution:** Do not assign 95% frequentist probability to a fixed parameter being inside this realized interval.

13. **Alpha:** 95% confidence. **Worked solution:** α=.05, each equal-tail probability=.025.

14. **99% critical:** Standard normal. **Worked solution:** z≈2.575829; wider than a 95% interval at fixed SE.

15. **Sample-size effect:** iid n increases by 4. **Worked solution:** Estimated SE approximately halves if s stays constant.

16. **Bootstrap resampling:** n=3 original observations. **Worked solution:** Each bootstrap replicate draws 3 values with replacement.

17. **Percentile interval:** Sorted estimates [1,2,3]. **Worked solution:** Type-7 50th percentile=2.

18. **Wilson zero success:** 0 successes out of 10. **Worked solution:** Wilson upper bound is positive; avoid zero-width Wald interval.

19. **Wilson full success:** 10 successes out of 10. **Worked solution:** Wilson lower bound is less than one.

20. **Dependence:** Autocorrelated daily returns. **Worked solution:** Naive iid CI may have incorrect coverage.

21. **Heavy tails:** Short sample with extreme returns. **Worked solution:** Normal/t assumptions may be inappropriate; inspect diagnostics.

22. **Selection bias:** Survivors-only stock universe. **Worked solution:** Confidence intervals cannot repair a biased sampling frame.

23. **Prediction versus confidence:** Mean-return CI. **Worked solution:** A confidence interval for a mean is not a prediction interval for the next return.

24. **Reproducibility:** Bootstrap random seed 42. **Worked solution:** Document seed, B, method, and data version.

## 13. Troubleshooting and operational checks

| Symptom | Likely cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Narrow interval with n=3 | Inappropriate z approximation | Evaluate t assumptions and sample limitations |
| Two tools return different bootstrap endpoints | Quantile interpolation or seed | Document quantile type, seed, B |
| Zero-width proportion interval at x=0 | Naive Wald method | Use Wilson or another suitable interval |
| Bootstrap interval changes across runs | Uncontrolled randomness | Fix seed and record implementation |
| Apparent 95% probability assigned to fixed parameter | Incorrect frequentist interpretation | Explain long-run coverage |
| CI mistaken for next-return range | Confusion with prediction intervals | State target quantity |
| Unexpectedly narrow financial CI | Serial dependence or regime changes | Diagnose time-series structure |
| Missing or infinite returns | Data-quality problem | Validate inputs and source data |
