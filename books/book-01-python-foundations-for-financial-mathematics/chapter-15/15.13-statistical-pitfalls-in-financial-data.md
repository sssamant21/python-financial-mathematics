## Learning objectives

Recognize and audit survivorship, selection, look-ahead, and data-snooping biases; explain how dependence, nonstationarity, heavy tails, missing data, and overlapping returns distort statistical inference; implement basic reproducible checks in Python; and design safer financial research workflows.

## 1. Why statistical pitfalls matter

A financial analysis can use mathematically correct formulas yet reach incorrect conclusions because the data, sampling process, or research design is flawed. A backtest can appear profitable because the researcher inadvertently used future information, ignored delisted companies, or selected the best result from hundreds of trials.

The correct workflow begins with a **well-defined estimand, point-in-time data, explicit assumptions, and a reproducible audit trail**, not merely a p-value or a visually appealing chart.

## 2. Survivorship and selection bias

**Survivorship bias** arises when the dataset includes only assets that remained active, listed, or observable at the end of the sample.

### Worked example

Suppose a fictional universe has two equally weighted stocks:

- Stock A survives with a +20% period return.
- Stock B is delisted with a −50% period return.

A survivor-only average is **+20%**. The full-universe arithmetic average is:

**(.20−.50)/2=−.15**, or **−15%**.

The difference is not a statistical estimation nuance; it is a **change in the population being measured**.

**Controls:** Maintain historical constituent membership, delisting returns, identifier mappings, and point-in-time eligibility. Track why observations were included or excluded.

Selection bias also appears when an analyst chooses funds with long histories, strategies with strong backtests, or periods that make a hypothesis look favorable.

## 3. Look-ahead bias and data leakage

**Look-ahead bias** occurs when a feature or decision uses information not yet available at the relevant time.

Examples:

- Using revised earnings in a backtest dated before the revision.
- Using the current day's closing price to trade at an earlier intraday time.
- Joining a daily macroeconomic series by observation date instead of actual publication timestamp.
- Scaling or imputing a time series using parameters estimated on the entire train-and-test dataset.

A robust record distinguishes:

1. **Event time:** when an underlying event occurred.
2. **Publication time:** when the source released the information.
3. **Availability time:** when the strategy's systems could access it.
4. **Decision time:** when the strategy formed an order.
5. **Execution time:** when an order could actually fill.

A feature is not valid merely because its calendar date is the same as the decision date. Publication delay, ingestion latency, and market-session boundaries matter.

The Python audit below flags any feature whose recorded availability time is later than its decision time. It cannot prove all timing is valid; equal timestamps still require latency checks.

## 4. Data snooping and multiple comparisons

Suppose a researcher tests 20 true null hypotheses at α=.05. If each test has a valid marginal 5% Type I error rate, the expected number of false rejections is:

**20×.05=1**.

This expectation does not require test independence; independence affects the distribution of false rejections and the probability of at least one.

A simple familywise safeguard is the **Bonferroni threshold**:

**α_per_test=α_family/m**.

For α_family=.05 and m=20, the threshold is **.0025**. Bonferroni controls familywise error under valid individual p-values without requiring independence, though it can be conservative.

Other approaches include false-discovery-rate control, preregistration, transparent experiment tracking, nested model selection, and truly independent out-of-sample evaluation.

**Warning:** Correcting p-values after hiding unreported experiments does not fix an undisclosed search process.

## 5. Serial dependence and volatility clustering

Financial returns may be serially correlated, and their squared returns may cluster even when raw-return autocorrelation is small.

For observations r₁,…,rₙ, one common sample lag-one autocorrelation estimator is:

**ρ̂₁=[Σₜ₌₂ⁿ(rₜ−r̄)(rₜ₋₁−r̄)]/[Σₜ₌₁ⁿ(rₜ−r̄)²]**.

This estimator is a descriptive diagnostic, not a full independence test.

With serial correlation, the variance of a sample mean generally includes autocovariance terms:

**Var(r̄)=(1/n²)ΣᵢΣⱼCov(rᵢ,rⱼ)**.

The iid simplification **σ²/n** need not hold. Inference may require HAC/Newey–West standard errors, block bootstrap, or a model suited to the dependence structure.

Volatility clustering motivates conditional-volatility models and stress tests; it does not automatically imply predictable return direction.

## 6. Overlapping returns

A rolling two-day return for days 1–2 and the next rolling two-day return for days 2–3 share the day-2 return.

Even when daily returns are independent, adjacent overlapping aggregated returns are typically dependent. Treating overlapping observations as independent can exaggerate the effective sample size and distort uncertainty estimates.

For simple returns Rₜ, a k-period compounded return is:

**Rₜ,k=∏ⱼ₌ₜ₋ₖ₊₁ᵗ(1+Rⱼ)−1**.

The reference implementation constructs overlapping returns for auditing but does not estimate their dependence-adjusted standard errors.

## 7. Nonstationarity and structural breaks

Financial relationships change as liquidity, monetary policy, market microstructure, participants, and risk regimes change.

Examples include:

- A volatility jump after a crisis.
- A factor premium that weakens after publication.
- A correlation that shifts during market stress.
- A strategy whose execution costs rise with scale.

A full-history estimate can obscure the current regime. Rolling windows, change-point analysis, regime comparisons, and time-aware validation can help, but **window selection itself can become a data-snooping problem**.

## 8. Heavy tails, skewness, and outliers

Financial returns may be asymmetric or exhibit more extreme outcomes than a normal model predicts.

An outlier can represent:

- A genuine crash or gap.
- A stock split not reflected in adjusted prices.
- A stale or erroneous quote.
- A futures roll discontinuity.
- A currency conversion error.

**Do not automatically delete extreme returns.** First distinguish data errors from economically real tail events. Removing real losses can severely understate VaR, expected shortfall, and drawdown risk.

For small samples, normal approximations and percentile bootstrap intervals may have poor tail coverage. Report sensitivity to distributional assumptions.

## 9. Missing data and calendar alignment

Missingness can be systematic. Delisted securities, suspended trading, thinly traded assets, and vendor coverage gaps are not necessarily random omissions.

When combining assets, align timestamps and define whether returns refer to close-to-close, open-to-close, or another horizon. Markets in different time zones can reflect different information sets even when dates match.

Forward-filling prices may manufacture zero returns and suppress measured volatility. Document any imputation and its consequences.

## 10. Spurious correlation and causation

A high correlation or regression R² does not establish causation.

Two trending nonstationary price series may appear strongly related even without a meaningful economic mechanism. Omitted market factors, common shocks, reverse causality, and contemporaneous feedback can also distort interpretation.

Use economic reasoning, time-aware controls, return or difference transformations when appropriate, and suitable time-series diagnostics. Do not assume that differencing always solves the underlying problem.

## 11. Backtest overfitting and economic relevance

A strategy selected because it performs best in the same sample used to design it is likely to have optimistic estimated performance.

A defensible evaluation separates:

- Research and model design.
- Chronological validation and tuning.
- Final untouched holdout evaluation.
- Forward monitoring after deployment.

All reported performance should reflect relevant fees, spread, slippage, financing, turnover, position limits, and capacity assumptions.

A statistically significant gross return is not necessarily an economically useful net return.

## 12. Audit matrix for financial statistics

| Pitfall | Typical symptom | Detection | Remediation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Survivorship | Unrealistically strong historical universe | Compare current and point-in-time constituents | Include delistings and historical membership |
| Look-ahead | Extraordinary predictive performance | Check feature availability timestamps | Enforce as-of joins and execution lag |
| Multiple testing | Many apparently significant signals | Count all attempted variants | Control multiplicity and use holdout |
| Autocorrelation | Overconfident confidence intervals | Inspect lag dependence | HAC or justified block methods |
| Volatility clustering | Underestimated tail risk | Inspect squared-return dynamics | Conditional risk models and stress tests |
| Heavy tails | Normal model misses crashes | Tail diagnostics | Robust tail analysis |
| Nonstationarity | Model fails after regime change | Rolling stability checks | Time-aware recalibration |
| Missingness | Suspiciously clean dataset | Missingness by asset and date | Explicit inclusion and imputation policy |
| Overlapping returns | Inflated effective sample size | Inspect window construction | Dependence-aware inference |
| Spurious regression | High R² without credible mechanism | Check trends and model specification | Appropriate time-series modeling |
| Cost omission | Gross profit, net loss | Execution-cost reconciliation | Net-of-cost backtest |
| Publication revisions | Backtest sees future restatements | Version audit | Point-in-time snapshots |

## 13. Python reference implementation

Save as `statistical_pitfalls.py`. The standard-library reference audits feature availability, survivor-only averages, overlapping compounded returns, lag-one autocorrelation, and Bonferroni thresholds. It includes **12 reference unit tests**.

```python
import math
import unittest

def finite(values):
    if not isinstance(values,(list,tuple)) or not values:
        raise ValueError("nonempty list or tuple required")
    if any(type(x) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(x) for x in values):
        raise ValueError("finite numeric observations required")
    return [float(x) for x in values]

def audit_asof(records):
    """Each record: (decision_time, feature_available_time)."""
    if not isinstance(records,(list,tuple)):
        raise ValueError("sequence of timestamp pairs required")
    flags=[]
    for row in records:
        if not isinstance(row,(tuple,list)) or len(row)!=2:
            raise ValueError("each row must be (decision_time, available_time)")
        decision,available=row
        try:
            flags.append(available>decision)
        except TypeError as exc:
            raise ValueError("comparable timestamp types required") from exc
    return flags

def survivor_mean(returns,retained):
    xs=finite(returns)
    if not isinstance(retained,(list,tuple)) or len(xs)!=len(retained) or any(type(x) is not bool for x in retained):
        raise ValueError("aligned boolean retention flags required")
    selected=[x for x,keep in zip(xs,retained) if keep]
    if not selected:
        raise ValueError("no retained observations")
    return math.fsum(selected)/len(selected)

def overlapping_returns(simple_returns,window):
    xs=finite(simple_returns)
    if type(window) is not int or not 1<=window<=len(xs):
        raise ValueError("valid integer window required")
    if any(x < -1 for x in xs):
        raise ValueError("simple returns below -100% invalid")
    out=[]
    for end in range(window,len(xs)+1):
        chunk=xs[end-window:end]
        out.append(-1.0 if -1 in chunk else math.expm1(math.fsum(math.log1p(x) for x in chunk)))
    return out

def lag_one_autocorrelation(values):
    xs=finite(values)
    if len(xs)<3:
        raise ValueError("at least three observations required")
    mean=math.fsum(xs)/len(xs)
    denominator=math.fsum((x-mean)**2 for x in xs)
    if denominator==0:
        raise ValueError("constant series")
    numerator=math.fsum((xs[i]-mean)*(xs[i-1]-mean) for i in range(1,len(xs)))
    return numerator/denominator

def bonferroni_threshold(alpha,number_of_tests):
    if type(alpha) not in (int,float) or not math.isfinite(alpha) or not 0<alpha<1:
        raise ValueError("alpha in (0,1) required")
    if type(number_of_tests) is not int or number_of_tests<1:
        raise ValueError("positive integer number_of_tests required")
    return alpha/number_of_tests

def sample_mean(values):
    xs=finite(values)
    return math.fsum(xs)/len(xs)

class PitfallAuditTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_01_asof_safe(self): self.assertEqual(audit_asof([(10,9)]),[False])
    def test_02_asof_leak(self): self.assertEqual(audit_asof([(10,11)]),[True])
    def test_03_asof_equal(self): self.assertEqual(audit_asof([(10,10)]),[False])
    def test_04_survivor(self): self.assertEqual(survivor_mean([.2,-.5],[True,False]),.2)
    def test_05_full_mean(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(sample_mean([.2,-.5]),-.15)
    def test_06_overlap_count(self): self.assertEqual(len(overlapping_returns([.01,.02,.03],2)),2)
    def test_07_overlap_value(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(overlapping_returns([.1,-.1],2)[0],-.01)
    def test_08_lag_autocorr(self):
        self.assertAlmostEqual(lag_one_autocorrelation([1,2,3]),0)
    def test_09_bonferroni(self): self.assertAlmostEqual(bonferroni_threshold(.05,20),.0025)
    def test_10_bonferroni_single(self): self.assertEqual(bonferroni_threshold(.05,1),.05)
    def test_11_total_loss(self): self.assertEqual(overlapping_returns([.2,-1],2),[-1])
    def test_12_invalid(self):
        for f in (lambda:survivor_mean([1,2],[False,False]),
                  lambda:audit_asof([(1,)]),
                  lambda:overlapping_returns([.1],2),
                  lambda:lag_one_autocorrelation([1,1,1]),
                  lambda:bonferroni_threshold(.05,0),
                  lambda:sample_mean([float("nan")])):
            with self.assertRaises(ValueError):f()

if __name__=="__main__":
    print("Survivor mean:",survivor_mean([.2,-.5],[True,False]))
    print("Full-universe mean:",sample_mean([.2,-.5]))
    print("Bonferroni threshold:",bonferroni_threshold(.05,20))
    unittest.main(verbosity=2)
```

## 14. Hands-on project — Bias and leakage audit of a fictional strategy

**Scenario:** An investment team reports unusually high returns and low drawdowns. Audit the evidence before accepting the result.

1. Define the strategy, investable universe, date range, and return conventions.
2. Reconstruct historical membership including delisted assets.
3. Recompute the average return with and without excluded names.
4. Audit each feature's availability time against decision time.
5. Identify revised or backfilled observations.
6. Count strategy variants, hyperparameter searches, and tested windows.
7. Apply a documented multiple-testing policy.
8. Compute lag-one return autocorrelation and inspect volatility clustering.
9. Identify overlapping returns and revise inference assumptions.
10. Review missing data, corporate actions, and cross-market alignment.
11. Recalculate performance net of realistic execution costs.
12. Produce an evidence log with unresolved risks, dataset hashes, and a final accept/reject recommendation for later QA.

**Acceptance references:** Survivor-only mean for [.20,−.50] with only the first asset retained is +.20; full-universe mean is −.15. Bonferroni threshold for α=.05 and 20 tests is .0025. Two-period compounded return for [+10%,−10%] is −1%.

## 15. Twenty-four worked exercises

1. **Survivorship bias:** Surviving return +20%, delisted return −50%. **Worked solution:** Survivor-only mean=+20%; full-universe mean=−15%.

2. **Look-ahead:** Feature available 16:01, decision at 16:00. **Worked solution:** Leakage: feature not available at decision.

3. **Timestamp boundary:** Feature and decision timestamp equal. **Worked solution:** May be valid only if publication and processing latency permit it.

4. **Restatement:** Revised earnings overwrite old record. **Worked solution:** Preserve point-in-time version as known then.

5. **Missingness:** Delisted names absent. **Worked solution:** Not missing at random; investigate selection mechanism.

6. **Outlier:** One extreme price jump. **Worked solution:** Check split/dividend and legitimate tail before exclusion.

7. **Volatility clustering:** Large moves follow large moves. **Worked solution:** Constant variance may be inappropriate.

8. **Serial correlation:** Positive lag-1 dependence. **Worked solution:** Naive iid mean SE may be too small.

9. **Overlapping returns:** Three daily returns, 2-day window. **Worked solution:** Two adjacent two-day returns share one observation.

10. **Nonstationarity:** Volatility doubles after regime shift. **Worked solution:** Full-history estimate may hide current risk.

11. **Small sample:** n=3 return observations. **Worked solution:** Asymptotic normality is not assured.

12. **Heavy tails:** Large rare losses. **Worked solution:** Normal VaR can understate extreme loss.

13. **Data snooping:** 20 null tests at α=.05. **Worked solution:** Expected false positives=1 if all nulls true.

14. **Bonferroni:** α=.05, 20 tests. **Worked solution:** Individual threshold=.0025.

15. **P-hacking:** Select best one-sided direction after results. **Worked solution:** Invalid nominal p-value.

16. **Multiple testing dependence:** Highly correlated strategies. **Worked solution:** Expected count still sums marginal probabilities; familywise probability changes.

17. **Confounding:** Market factor drives asset and signal. **Worked solution:** Raw correlation may not represent causal signal.

18. **Spurious regression:** Two trending nonstationary levels. **Worked solution:** High R² may arise without meaningful relation.

19. **Price versus return:** Regress trending prices. **Worked solution:** Investigate nonstationarity and appropriate return models.

20. **Calendar alignment:** US and Japan closes. **Worked solution:** Avoid treating different information sets as simultaneous.

21. **Annualization:** Autocorrelated returns. **Worked solution:** √252 volatility rule may fail.

22. **Selection on outcome:** Choose only winning backtests. **Worked solution:** Report all trials and independent holdout.

23. **Transaction costs:** Gross Sharpe looks positive. **Worked solution:** Include fees, slippage, financing, and capacity.

24. **Audit trail:** Reproducible research. **Worked solution:** Record source snapshot, timestamp rules, universe, code, seeds, and decisions.

## 16. Troubleshooting and operational checks

| Symptom | Likely cause | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|
| Backtest collapses after delisting data added | Survivorship bias | Rebuild historical universe |
| Feature appears before its publication | Look-ahead | Use point-in-time availability |
| Many small p-values across hundreds of trials | Selection effects | Track and adjust testing |
| Confidence intervals too narrow | Dependence or overlap | Reassess effective uncertainty |
| Extremely low volatility | Stale prices or forward filling | Audit data quality |
| Results shift across calendar conventions | Misaligned observations | Standardize timestamps |
| Tail risk disappears after cleaning | Real outliers removed | Preserve legitimate extremes |
| Model only works in sample | Overfitting or regime change | Validate on untouched future data |
