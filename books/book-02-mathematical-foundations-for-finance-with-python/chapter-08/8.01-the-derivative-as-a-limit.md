## Learning objectives

- Define a derivative using a limit of difference quotients.
- Distinguish average and instantaneous rates of change.
- Derive elementary derivatives directly from the definition.
- Explain why differentiability implies continuity but not conversely.
- Interpret financial derivatives with correct units and rate conventions.
- Compare analytical derivatives with numerical approximations and recognize cancellation.

## 1. From average to instantaneous change

For a function \(f\), the average rate of change between \(x\) and \(x+h\), where \(h\ne0\), is

\[
\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.
\]

The **derivative at \(x\)** is the limit, provided it exists as a finite two-sided limit:

\[
\boxed{f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.}
\]

The numerator measures the output change and the denominator measures the input change. The units of the derivative are therefore **output units per input unit**.

Geometrically, the quotient is a secant slope; its limit is the tangent slope. Economically, it is the instantaneous or marginal response to a small change in the input.

A derivative is a mathematical limit, not the result of setting \(h=0\) in a quotient that would then be undefined.

## 2. Deriving the derivative of a quadratic

Let \(f(x)=x^2\). Then

\[
\begin{aligned}
f'(x)
&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}\\
&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{2xh+h^2}{h}\\
&=\lim_{h\to0}(2x+h)=2x.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus the instantaneous slope of \(x^2\) at \(x=3\) is 6. This conclusion follows algebraically; a numerical table may illustrate it but does not replace the proof.

For \(f(x)=ax+b\), the difference quotient is exactly \(a\), so \(f'(x)=a\). For a constant \(f(x)=c\), the quotient is zero and \(f'(x)=0\).

## 3. Left and right derivatives

At an interior point, differentiability requires the left-hand and right-hand difference quotients to approach the same finite value.

Consider \(f(x)=|x|\) at zero:

\[
\frac{|h|-|0|}{h}=
\begin{cases}
-1,&h<0,\\
1,&h>0.
\end{cases}
\]

The one-sided limits disagree, so \(|x|\) is **not differentiable at zero**, even though it is continuous there.

Differentiability implies continuity: if \(f'(x)\) exists, then

\[
f(x+h)-f(x)=h\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}h\to0.
\]

The converse is false, as \(|x|\) demonstrates. At a domain endpoint, a one-sided derivative may be defined, but it is not the ordinary two-sided derivative at an interior point.

## 4. Financial example: continuously discounted present value

For a positive future cash flow \(C\), a continuously compounded annual rate \(r\), and horizon \(t\ge0\),

\[
PV(r)=Ce^{-rt}.
\]

Using the derivative definition,

\[
\begin{aligned}
PV'(r)
&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{Ce^{-(r+h)t}-Ce^{-rt}}h\\
&=Ce^{-rt}\lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^{-ht}-1}{h}\\
&=\boxed{-tCe^{-rt}}=-tPV(r).
\end{aligned}
\]

Here the final limit uses the small-change exponential limit from Section 7.6. The derivative is negative for \(C,t>0\): increasing the continuous discount rate decreases present value.

**Example:** If \(C=1000\), \(r=0.05\), and \(t=3\), then

\[
PV(0.05)=1000e^{-0.15}\approx860.708,\qquad
PV'(0.05)=-3PV(0.05)\approx-2582.124.
\]

The derivative is measured in currency units per **unit change in the decimal annual rate**. A 1-basis-point rate increase means \(h=0.0001\), so the first-order PV change is approximately \(PV'(r)h\approx-0.2582\), not \(-2582.124\).

The exact change is

\[
PV(r+h)-PV(r)=PV(r)\operatorname{expm1}(-th).
\]

This stable expression is useful for validating the local approximation.

## 5. Financial example: marginal profit

Suppose a stylized profit function is

\[
\Pi(q)=12q-0.02q^2-500.
\]

Its difference quotient simplifies to

\[
\frac{\Pi(q+h)-\Pi(q)}h=12-0.04q-0.02h.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{\Pi'(q)=12-0.04q.}
\]

At \(q=100\), marginal profit is 8 currency units per additional unit of sales, in the continuous approximation. The derivative is not necessarily the exact profit change from selling one additional *integer* unit; that finite difference is \(\Pi(q+1)-\Pi(q)\).

## 6. Numerical derivatives and limitations

A forward-difference approximation is

\[
D_h^+f(x)=\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}h.
\]

A centered-difference approximation is

\[
D_h^0f(x)=\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}.
\]

For sufficiently smooth functions, centered differences generally have smaller truncation error than forward differences for the same moderate step size. However, taking \(h\) arbitrarily close to zero is not always better in floating-point arithmetic: subtraction of nearly equal function values causes cancellation.

For the PV function, use a stable forward difference:

\[
\frac{PV(r+h)-PV(r)}h
=PV(r)\frac{\operatorname{expm1}(-th)}h.
\]

This is more stable than directly subtracting two nearly equal PV values.

Numerical differentiation provides a check, not a proof of differentiability or an unconditional error guarantee.

## 8. Exercises

**1.** State the derivative definition and explain why \(h\ne0\) in the difference quotient.

**2.** Derive the derivative of \(f(x)=4x+7\) from first principles.

**3.** Derive the derivative of \(f(x)=x^2\) from first principles.

**4.** Compute \(f'(3)\) for \(f(x)=x^2\).

**5.** Explain why \(f(x)=|x|\) is continuous but not differentiable at zero.

**6.** For \(PV(r)=500e^{-2r}\), derive \(PV'(r)\).

**7.** At \(r=0.04\), estimate the PV change in Exercise 6 for a 10-basis-point rate increase.

**8.** Derive the marginal profit for \(\Pi(q)=12q-0.02q^2-500\).

**9.** Why can a tiny numerical-difference step produce a poor approximation?

**10.** Distinguish a derivative from the finite change \(f(x+1)-f(x)\).

### Worked solutions

1. \(f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}[f(x+h)-f(x)]/h\), if the finite limit exists. The quotient itself requires \(h\ne0\).
2. \([4(x+h)+7-(4x+7)]/h=4\); the derivative is 4.
3. The quotient simplifies to \(2x+h\), which tends to \(2x\).
4. \(f'(3)=6\).
5. The left derivative is \(-1\) and the right derivative is \(1\); they disagree.
6. \(PV'(r)=-1000e^{-2r}=-2PV(r)\).
7. \(h=0.001\). Approximate change \(PV'(0.04)h=-e^{-0.08}\approx-0.923116\) currency units.
8. \(\Pi'(q)=12-0.04q\).
9. Floating-point cancellation and rounding can dominate truncation error.
10. A derivative is the limiting rate per infinitesimal input change; \(f(x+1)-f(x)\) is an exact finite change over a unit interval.
