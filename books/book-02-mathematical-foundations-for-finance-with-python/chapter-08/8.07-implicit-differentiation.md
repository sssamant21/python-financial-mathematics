## Learning objectives

Differentiate equations that define one variable implicitly in terms of another; apply the chain, product, and quotient rules to such equations; recognize conditions under which implicit sensitivity exists; and apply the method to break-even output, bond yield, and model constraints.

## 1. Explicit versus implicit functions

An **explicit** function specifies \(y=f(x)\). An **implicit** relationship specifies an equation

\[
F(x,y)=0
\]

whose solutions may define one or more branches \(y(x)\). For example, \(x^2+y^2=25\) defines the upper and lower semicircles wherever \(y\ne0\), not one global function of \(x\).

When \(y\) depends on \(x\), differentiate every occurrence of \(y\) using the chain rule:

\[
\frac{d}{dx}y^n=ny^{n-1}y'.
\]

**Worked example:** Differentiating \(x^2+y^2=25\) gives

\[
2x+2yy'=0,\qquad \boxed{y'=-x/y},\quad y\ne0.
\]

At \((3,4)\), the tangent slope is \(-3/4\). At \((5,0)\), this formula is undefined; a finite derivative \(dy/dx\) does not exist there for the circle branch.

## 2. General implicit derivative formula

If \(F(x,y(x))=0\) and \(F\) has continuous partial derivatives near a point, differentiation yields

\[
F_x(x,y)+F_y(x,y)y'=0.
\]

When \(F_y(x,y)\ne0\), the implicit function theorem guarantees a differentiable local branch under its usual neighborhood hypotheses, with

\[
\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}}.
\]

The condition \(F_y\ne0\) is sufficient for the local result, not necessary for every possible differentiable branch. If \(F_y=0\), this formula cannot be applied; examine the geometry or the original equation instead.

## 3. Product and chain rules inside an equation

Consider

\[
xy+y^2=10.
\]

Differentiating gives

\[
y+xy'+2yy'=0.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\boxed{y'=-\frac{y}{x+2y}},\qquad x+2y\ne0.
\]

At the point \((1,\,(-1+\sqrt{41})/2)\), the derivative is finite. The term \(d(xy)/dx=y+xy'\) is essential: treating \(y\) as a constant would be incorrect.

## 4. Financial application: break-even quantity and unit price

Suppose a firm has fixed cost \(K>0\), variable cost \(cq\), and sales revenue \(pq\). Break-even occurs when

\[
F(p,q)=pq-cq-K=(p-c)q-K=0.
\]

For \(p>c\), the break-even quantity is \(q=K/(p-c)\). Differentiate the implicit equation with respect to \(p\), holding \(c,K\) fixed:

\[
q+(p-c)\frac{dq}{dp}=0,
\qquad
\boxed{\frac{dq}{dp}=-\frac{q}{p-c}=-\frac{K}{(p-c)^2}}.
\]

**Worked example:** If \(K=1200\), \(c=20\), and \(p=50\), then \(q=40\) units and

\[
\frac{dq}{dp}=-\frac{40}{30}=-\frac43
\]

units per currency-unit increase in selling price. The sensitivity grows sharply in magnitude as \(p\) approaches \(c\) from above; this is a model warning about ill-conditioning near the break-even feasibility boundary.

## 5. Financial application: bond price and yield

For positive fixed cash flows \(C_i\) paid at positive integer periods \(i\), a bond price under a per-period yield \(y>-1\) is

\[
P(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{C_i}{(1+y)^i}.
\]

For a market price \(P_m\), the yield is defined implicitly by

\[
F(P_m,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_i(1+y)^{-i}-P_m=0.
\]

Differentiating with respect to market price,

\[
\left[-\sum_{i=1}^{n}iC_i(1+y)^{-i-1}\right]\frac{dy}{dP_m}-1=0.
\]

Thus,

\[
\boxed{\frac{dy}{dP_m}
=-\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}iC_i(1+y)^{-i-1}}}.
\]

For positive payments and \(y>-1\), the denominator is positive: a higher bond price implies a lower yield. Units are yield units per currency unit of market price.

**Worked example:** A one-period payment of 105 with price 100 has yield \(y=0.05\). Since \(P(y)=105/(1+y)\),

\[
\frac{dP}{dy}=-\frac{105}{(1+y)^2},\qquad
\frac{dy}{dP}=-\frac{(1+y)^2}{105}.
\]

At \(y=0.05\), \(dy/dP=-0.0105\) per currency unit. A 0.01-unit price increase produces a first-order yield change of approximately \(-0.000105\), or \(-1.05\) basis points, where one basis point is \(0.0001\).

## 6. Financial application: a constrained exposure relation

Suppose an exposure limit binds:

\[
a x^2+b y^2=L,\qquad a,b,L>0.
\]

If \(y>0\), implicit differentiation gives

\[
2ax+2byy'=0,\qquad
\boxed{y'=-\frac{ax}{by}}.
\]

The derivative describes the local tradeoff along the binding boundary. It is not, on its own, an optimal allocation rule; optimization also requires an objective and feasible-set assumptions.

## 7. Numerical checks and conditioning

For a smooth local solution branch \(y(x)\), a centered difference can check the derivative:

\[
D_hy(x)=\frac{y(x+h)-y(x-h)}{2h}.
\]

This requires solving the original equation consistently on the same branch at both perturbed points. A numerical root finder that jumps between branches can report misleading sensitivities.

If \(|F_y|\) is very small, \(-F_x/F_y\) may be very large: small input changes can produce large output changes. Near singular points, the derivative may fail to exist or the implicit relationship may not define a unique local function.

## 8. Exercises

**1.** Differentiate \(x^2+y^2=25\) and find the slope at \((3,4)\).

**2.** Explain why \(y'=-x/y\) is not defined at \((5,0)\).

**3.** Differentiate \(xy+y^2=10\).

**4.** State the general implicit derivative formula and its denominator condition.

**5.** For \(x^2+3y^2=12\), find \(dy/dx\) at \((0,2)\).

**6.** Derive the break-even sensitivity \(dq/dp\) from \((p-c)q=K\).

**7.** Evaluate break-even quantity and sensitivity for \(K=1200,c=20,p=50\).

**8.** Show that bond yield decreases with market price when all fixed cash flows are positive.

**9.** Find \(dy/dP\) for a one-period bond with payment 105 and price 100.

**10.** Explain two hazards when numerically checking an implicit derivative.

### Worked solutions

1. \(2x+2yy'=0\), hence \(y'=-x/y=-3/4\).
2. The denominator is zero and the circle has a vertical tangent there; \(y\) cannot be represented as a differentiable function of \(x\) through that point.
3. \(y+xy'+2yy'=0\), hence \(y'=-y/(x+2y)\), when \(x+2y\ne0\).
4. \(dy/dx=-F_x/F_y\), provided the relevant smooth local branch exists and \(F_y\ne0\).
5. \(2x+6yy'=0\), so \(y'=-x/(3y)=0\) at \((0,2)\).
6. \(q+(p-c)q'=0\), so \(q'=-q/(p-c)=-K/(p-c)^2\).
7. \(q=40\) and \(dq/dp=-4/3\).
8. \(dP/dy=-\sum_i iC_i(1+y)^{-i-1}<0\), so \(dy/dP=1/(dP/dy)<0\).
9. \(dy/dP=-(1.05)^2/105=-0.0105\).
10. Branch switching during root solving and amplification of numerical error when \(F_y\) is near zero; invalid perturbations and cancellation are additional hazards.
