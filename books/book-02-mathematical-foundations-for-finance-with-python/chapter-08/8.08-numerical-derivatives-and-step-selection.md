## Learning objectives

Approximate first and second derivatives with finite differences; derive their leading truncation errors; choose step sizes using scale, domain, and floating-point precision; identify nonsmooth functions; and validate duration- and convexity-like financial sensitivities.

## 1. Why numerical derivatives?

Analytic derivatives are preferable when tractable, but many pricing functions are defined by simulations, optimization procedures, or complex numerical models. Numerical differentiation approximates a local sensitivity using nearby function evaluations.

For differentiable \(f\), the derivative is

\[
f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}h.
\]

In floating-point arithmetic, however, **smaller \(h\) is not always better**. Decreasing \(h\) reduces truncation error but can increase roundoff error.

## 2. Forward, backward, and centered differences

The forward and backward approximations are

\[
D_h^+f(x)=\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}h,\qquad
D_h^-f(x)=\frac{f(x)-f(x-h)}h.
\]

Taylor expansion, assuming sufficient smoothness, gives

\[
D_h^+f(x)=f'(x)+\frac h2f''(x)+O(h^2),
\]

\[
D_h^-f(x)=f'(x)-\frac h2f''(x)+O(h^2).
\]

The centered difference is

\[
\boxed{D_h^0f(x)=\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}
=f'(x)+\frac{h^2}{6}f^{(3)}(x)+O(h^4).}
\]

Centered differences are generally second-order accurate in \(h\) for sufficiently smooth functions, compared with first-order accuracy for one-sided differences. A centered stencil requires **both** perturbations to be in the function's domain.

**Worked example.** For \(f(x)=x^3\) at \(x=2\), \(f'(2)=12\). At \(h=0.1\), the forward difference is \(12.61\), backward is \(11.41\), and centered is \(12.01\). The centered result follows exactly from \(D_h^0(x^3)=3x^2+h^2\).

## 3. Numerical second derivatives

The standard three-point second difference is

\[
\boxed{D_h^{(2)}f(x)=\frac{f(x+h)-2f(x)+f(x-h)}{h^2}.}
\]

For four-times continuously differentiable \(f\),

\[
D_h^{(2)}f(x)=f''(x)+\frac{h^2}{12}f^{(4)}(x)+O(h^4).
\]

Its truncation error is \(O(h^2)\), but roundoff error is amplified by \(1/h^2\). This makes the second derivative more sensitive to very small step sizes than the first derivative.

## 4. Balancing truncation and roundoff

A simplified error model for a centered first derivative is

\[
E(h)\approx A h^2+B\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}}{h},
\]

where \(A,B\) depend on the function, scale, and implementation. Balancing the terms suggests

\[
h_{\mathrm{center}}\propto\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}^{1/3}\times\text{input scale}.
\]

For a first-order forward difference, the analogous heuristic is \(h\propto\sqrt{\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}}\). For a three-point second difference, balancing \(h^2\) truncation against \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}/h^2\) suggests \(h\propto\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}^{1/4}\).

These are **heuristics, not universal optimal steps**. Near flat regions, discontinuities, boundaries, and noisy simulations, they can fail. Use a problem-specific input scale, inspect several step sizes, and compare against analytic or independent references whenever available.

## 5. Practical step selection

A robust finite-difference workflow:

1. Identify the function's domain and the units of the differentiated variable.
2. Choose a scale \(s=\max(1,|x|)\), or a domain-specific characteristic scale if the variable is naturally much smaller than one.
3. Start with \(h=s\,\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}^{1/3}\) for a centered first derivative or \(h=s\,\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}^{1/4}\) for a centered second derivative.
4. Confirm that \(x+h\ne x\), \(x-h\ne x\), and both perturbed inputs remain valid.
5. Compare results over a step ladder, such as \(h, h/2, 2h, 4h\).
6. If the model is stochastic or nonsmooth, consider variance reduction, common random numbers, one-sided slopes, or a derivative-aware method rather than trusting a small-step finite difference.

For rate sensitivities, \(h=0.0001\) represents one basis point of a decimal yield; \(h=0.01\) represents 100 basis points.

## 6. Financial example: price sensitivity and convexity

For a fixed positive cash flow \(C\) at horizon \(t\), discounted at continuously compounded rate \(r\),

\[
P(r)=Ce^{-rt}.
\]

Its analytic derivatives are

\[
P'(r)=-tP(r),\qquad P''(r)=t^2P(r).
\]

Define the normalized continuous-rate sensitivities

\[
D=-\frac{P'(r)}{P(r)}=t,\qquad
K=\frac{P''(r)}{P(r)}=t^2.
\]

For \(C=1000\), \(t=5\), and \(r=0.04\),

\[
P(r)=1000e^{-0.2}\approx818.730753,
\qquad D=5,\quad K=25.
\]

Estimate \(D\) from the centered first difference and \(K\) from the centered second difference, then compare with the analytic values. These definitions use a continuously compounded rate and should not be confused with every market convention for modified duration or bond convexity.

## 7. Richardson extrapolation

If the centered approximation satisfies

\[
D_h^0f(x)=f'(x)+c h^2+O(h^4),
\]

then the leading error cancels in

\[
\boxed{D_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{4D_{h/2}^0f(x)-D_h^0f(x)}3.}
\]

For a sufficiently smooth function and appropriate \(h\), Richardson extrapolation improves the truncation order to \(O(h^4)\). It can nevertheless amplify floating-point noise; verify that the step ladder is in a stable regime before relying on it.

## 8. Boundaries, kinks, and simulation noise

A centered difference at the boundary \(x=0\) of a function defined only for \(x\ge0\) may be invalid. A forward difference is often the correct available local approximation.

For \(f(x)=|x|\), the left derivative at zero is \(-1\), the right derivative is \(+1\), and the two-sided derivative does not exist. The centered difference equals zero for every \(h>0\), demonstrating that a stable numerical output **does not prove differentiability**.

Monte Carlo pricing adds sampling error. Independent random draws at perturbed inputs can swamp a finite difference; paired or common random numbers may reduce variance but require a carefully designed experiment.

## Finite-difference range follow-up

The forward, backward, centered, second-difference and Richardson helpers validate their returned estimates and raise `ValueError` for nonfinite calculations. Finite callback results alone do not guarantee a finite difference. Existing perturbation, doubled-step and squared-step guards remain; invalid full-step controls are rejected before callback evaluation.

Richardson uses the algebraically equivalent expression `fine + (fine - coarse)/3`, avoiding the unnecessary `4*fine` intermediate when the approximations are large and close. This does not guarantee arbitrary-range accuracy or eliminate cancellation/underflow. The half-step must also perturb the base point; a valid full step does not ensure a valid half-step.

## Normalized sensitivity range

Standalone `discounted_price` permits ordinary exponential underflow to zero, but rejects nonfinite discount exponents or prices. The normalized `rate_sensitivity` report requires a positive representable base price before dividing by it and validates duration, convexity and analytic convexity for finiteness. Exponential overflow continues to raise `OverflowError`; other nonfinite computed values and a zero normalization price raise `ValueError`.

For the continuous single-payment model, the centered-stencil duration equals `sinh(T*h)/h`; its convexity equals `4*(sinh(T*h/2)/h)^2` in exact arithmetic. These references check the computed finite-difference quantities, separately from the analytic limits T and T². Tiny positive/subnormal prices, cancellation and underflow can still impair accuracy; positivity and finiteness do not certify a useful step.

### Complete financial expression convention (V-134)

`discounted_price` reports the complete represented-input expression `cash_flow * exp(-rate * years)`. It uses the Chapter 8 scaled-price helper so an unreportable intermediate product or exponential does not by itself reject a finite final price. Cash must be positive and years nonnegative. Zero years returns cash; positive underflow reports zero; an unreportable final price raises `ValueError`.

`rate_sensitivity` keeps the positive reportable base-price gate and requires a positive finite step with finite distinct represented shifted rates. It reports normalized complete model-price differences: if `ru = rate + step`, `rd = rate - step`, `u = exp(-years*(ru-rate))`, and `d = exp(-years*(rd-rate))`, duration is `-(u-d)/(2*step)` and convexity is `(u-2+d)/step**2`, evaluated before the final binary report. Cash scale cancels first, so a shifted absolute price need not itself be reportable. This convention differs from taking ratios of individually rounded price callbacks. The generic finite-difference functions still operate on their returned binary callback values.

The shifts are the actual represented rates, while the denominators retain the requested step. An asymmetric grid can yield sampled curvature with a different sign from analytic curvature; sampled estimates are not replaced by analytic values. Analytic duration is years and analytic convexity is the final report of exact represented years squared. Final range failures raise `ValueError`; final underflow remains possible. The local normalized calculation uses a private 1600-digit Decimal context and finite-binary exponential cutoffs; it is not an arbitrary-precision derivative guarantee. See [V-134 evidence, independent complete-price references and scoped limits](../validation/chapter-08-section-08-financial-followup.md). Suggested-step, broader conditioning/noise/resource and independent review obligations remain separate.


### Suggested steps are heuristic seeds (V-135)

`suggested_step` uses the first-derivative cube-root or second-derivative fourth-root of binary epsilon, scaled by `max(1,abs(x))` or the caller's positive finite scale. It validates x, then derivative order, then scale and finally requires both represented x±h to be finite and distinct. An extremely small explicit scale can therefore be rejected, and a maximum finite x may have no acceptable suggested symmetric perturbation. The suggestion is not an optimal-step or derivative-accuracy guarantee: callback noise, quantization, conditioning and units can change a useful step. [Independent Newton-root references, validation priority and limits](../validation/chapter-08-suggested-step-followup.md) are scoped evidence; broader noise/conditioning review remains open.


### Noise and quantization can dominate a valid step (V-137)

The symmetric finite/distinct perturbation gate and a heuristic epsilon-root step do not guarantee an accurate derivative. With bounded sign noise of amplitude eta on a quadratic at zero,the centered first estimate has bias eta/h. Opposite center/shifted errors can add4eta/h² to its second estimate. A constant additive offset cancels instead. Quantizing an identity callback can yield zero slope when both samples round to the same level,including at the default suggested step. Increasing h can reduce this sample-noise contribution while changing truncation error; a useful choice needs callback precision/noise,scale and error-model information. [Exact selected noise/quantization references and separate accuracy diagnostics](../validation/chapter-08-noise-followup.md) document this limitation; no noise-aware selector is implemented.


### A bounded diagnostic sweep is not an optimal selector (V-138)

A preset grid can expose competing error terms when a synthetic model's noise is known. At zero,a cubic with sign noise has centered first error h²+eta/h; a quartic with opposite center/shift noise has second error2h²+4eta/h². The finite-grid minima can differ from continuous optimal steps and from a real provider's useful steps. [A reproducible34-step/86-callback diagnostic report](../validation/chapter-08-noisy-sweep-followup.md) retains estimates,error components and measured callback order/budgets. It is supplemental QA using the source helpers,not an adaptive production API or proof of finance-data accuracy.
