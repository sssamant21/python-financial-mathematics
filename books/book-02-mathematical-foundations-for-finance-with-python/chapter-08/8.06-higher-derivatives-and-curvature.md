## Learning objectives

Compute second and higher derivatives, interpret concavity and curvature, distinguish candidate inflection points from actual changes in concavity, analyze marginal cost and profit, and calculate the second-order rate sensitivity of discounted cash flows.

## 1. Higher derivatives

If \(f'\) is differentiable, its derivative is the **second derivative**, written \(f''(x)\) or \(d^2f/dx^2\). Repeated differentiation gives \(f^{(3)}(x)\), \(f^{(4)}(x)\), and so on.

For \(f(x)=3x^4-2x^3+5x-7\),

\[
f'(x)=12x^3-6x^2+5,\quad
f''(x)=36x^2-12x,\quad
f^{(3)}(x)=72x-12,\quad
f^{(4)}(x)=72.
\]

The first derivative describes a local slope; the second describes how that slope changes.

## 2. Concavity and inflection points

On an interval where \(f''\) exists:

- \(f''(x)>0\) throughout the interval implies the graph is **concave up** there.
- \(f''(x)<0\) throughout the interval implies **concave down**.
- A point is an **inflection point** when concavity changes across it.

A zero second derivative is only a candidate for an inflection point, not a guarantee. For \(f(x)=x^4\), \(f''(0)=0\) but \(f''(x)=12x^2\ge0\) on both sides, so zero is not an inflection point. For \(f(x)=x^3\), \(f''(x)=6x\) changes sign at zero, so zero is an inflection point.

A function may have an inflection point where its second derivative does not exist; the defining condition is the change in concavity.

## 3. Marginal cost and profit

Let total cost be

\[
C(q)=1000+20q+0.05q^2,\qquad q\ge0.
\]

Then

\[
C'(q)=20+0.1q,\qquad C''(q)=0.1>0.
\]

The marginal cost increases with quantity. If revenue is \(R(q)=100q-0.2q^2\), then profit is

\[
\Pi(q)=R(q)-C(q)=-1000+80q-0.25q^2.
\]

Hence

\[
\Pi'(q)=80-0.5q,\qquad \Pi''(q)=-0.5<0.
\]

The profit function is strictly concave. The stationary point \(\Pi'(q)=0\) occurs at \(q=160\), which is the unique global maximum on the model's feasible range \(0\le q\le500\). The result is conditional on the stylized revenue/cost model and constraints.

## 4. Second-order rate sensitivity of a cash flow

For \(C\ge0\), \(t\ge0\), and continuously compounded rate \(r\),

\[
PV(r)=Ce^{-rt}.
\]

Differentiate with respect to \(r\), holding \(C,t\) fixed:

\[
\boxed{PV'(r)=-tPV(r)},\qquad
\boxed{PV''(r)=t^2PV(r)}.
\]

For \(C>0,t>0\), \(PV''(r)>0\): the present-value curve is convex in the continuously compounded rate.

A second-order local approximation for a rate shock \(h\) is

\[
\boxed{PV(r+h)\approx PV(r)+PV'(r)h+\tfrac12PV''(r)h^2.}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\frac{\Delta PV}{PV}\approx-th+\frac{t^2h^2}{2}.
\]

The exact relative change is \(\operatorname{expm1}(-th)\). For a single cash flow, \(PV''/PV=t^2\) is the normalized second rate derivative. This is a continuous-rate sensitivity measure; standard market bond convexity conventions may use different yield and compounding definitions.

## 5. Numerical worked example

Take \(C=10{,}000\), \(r=0.04\), and \(t=5\). Then

\[
PV=10000e^{-0.2}\approx8187.307531,
\]
\[
PV'=-5PV\approx-40936.537655,\qquad
PV''=25PV\approx204682.688274.
\]

For a 100-basis-point increase, \(h=0.01\), the first-order approximation is

\[
\Delta PV_1=PV'h\approx-409.365377.
\]

The second-order approximation adds

\[
\tfrac12PV''h^2\approx10.234134,
\]

giving \(\Delta PV_2\approx-399.131242\). The exact change is

\[
\Delta PV_{\mathrm{exact}}=PV\operatorname{expm1}(-0.05)\approx-399.29924.
\]

The second-order estimate is much closer for this example, but this does not guarantee superiority for every model and shock size.

## 6. Multiple cash flows and convexity

For positive payments \(C_i\) at horizons \(t_i\ge0\),

\[
V(r)=\sum_{i=1}^n C_i e^{-rt_i}.
\]

Term-by-term differentiation of the finite sum gives

\[
V'(r)=-\sum_i t_i C_i e^{-rt_i},
\qquad
V''(r)=\sum_i t_i^2 C_i e^{-rt_i}\ge0.
\]

If at least one positive payment occurs at a strictly positive horizon, then \(V''(r)>0\). With negative or signed cash flows, the second derivative need not be positive.

## 7. Numerical second derivatives

The centered second-difference approximation is

\[
D_h^{(2)}f(x)=\frac{f(x+h)-2f(x)+f(x-h)}{h^2}.
\]

For smooth functions and moderate \(h\), its truncation error is typically \(O(h^2)\). Very small \(h\) can amplify floating-point cancellation because the numerator subtracts nearly equal values and divides by \(h^2\). Numerical agreement is evidence, not proof.

## 8. Exercises

**1.** Find \(f'\), \(f''\), and \(f^{(3)}\) for \(f(x)=2x^4-3x^3+x\).

**2.** Find the intervals of concavity for \(f(x)=x^3\).

**3.** Explain why \(f''(0)=0\) does not imply an inflection point.

**4.** For \(C(q)=1000+20q+0.05q^2\), compute \(C''(q)\) and interpret it.

**5.** For \(\Pi(q)=-1000+80q-0.25q^2\), find the profit-maximizing quantity on \([0,500]\).

**6.** Derive \(PV''(r)\) for \(PV(r)=Ce^{-rt}\).

**7.** Evaluate \(PV''\) for \(C=10000\), \(r=0.04\), and \(t=5\).

**8.** Write the second-order approximation to \(PV(r+h)\).

**9.** Prove \(V''(r)\ge0\) for a finite collection of nonnegative cash flows at nonnegative horizons.

**10.** Explain why a centered second difference can become less accurate as \(h\) becomes extremely small.

### Worked solutions

1. \(f'=8x^3-9x^2+1\); \(f''=24x^2-18x\); \(f^{(3)}=48x-18\).
2. \(f''(x)=6x\), so concave down on \((-\infty,0)\) and up on \((0,\infty)\), with an inflection at zero.
3. \(x^4\) has \(f''(0)=0\) but no concavity change.
4. \(C''(q)=0.1\); marginal cost rises by 0.1 currency units per unit of quantity in the continuous model.
5. \(\Pi'(q)=80-0.5q=0\) at \(q=160\); \(\Pi''=-0.5<0\), so it is the unique maximizer on the stated interval.
6. \(PV''(r)=t^2Ce^{-rt}=t^2PV(r)\).
7. \(25(10000e^{-0.2})\approx204682.688274\).
8. \(PV(r+h)\approx PV(r)+PV'(r)h+\tfrac12PV''(r)h^2\).
9. \(V''(r)=\sum_i t_i^2 C_i e^{-rt_i}\ge0\), since each summand is nonnegative.
10. The numerator involves cancellation and the division by \(h^2\) magnifies rounding errors.
