## Learning objectives

Represent linear systems as \(Ax=b\); construct augmented matrices; perform elementary row operations; explain Gaussian elimination, partial pivoting, and back-substitution; and interpret solutions and residuals in a financial exposure-matching application.

## 1. From equations to matrices

Consider

\[
2x+y=5,\qquad x-y=1.
\]

In matrix notation,

\[
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}}_A
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}}_x
=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}5\\1\end{bmatrix}}_b.
\]

The augmented matrix is

\[
[A\mid b]=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&\vline&5\\1&-1&\vline&1\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The solution is \(x=2,y=1\). Matrix rows encode equations; columns encode unknowns. In financial models, both axes must have documented economic meaning.

## 2. Elementary row operations

Three operations preserve the solution set of a linear system: swapping two rows, multiplying a row by a nonzero scalar, and adding a multiple of one row to another. Gaussian elimination uses these operations to create an upper-triangular system.

For the example, subtract half of row 1 from row 2:

\[
\begin{bmatrix}2&1&\vline&5\\0&-1.5&\vline&-1.5\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Back-substitution gives \(y=1\), followed by \(x=2\).

## 3. Partial pivoting

Naive elimination can divide by zero or amplify roundoff when a pivot is tiny. **Partial pivoting** chooses the row with the largest absolute entry in the current pivot column among the remaining rows and swaps it into the pivot position.

For

\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\2&3\end{bmatrix},\qquad b=\begin{bmatrix}1\\5\end{bmatrix},
\]

the first pivot is zero. Swapping the two rows permits elimination and yields \(x=1,y=1\).

Partial pivoting is an important baseline but is not a universal guarantee of numerical accuracy. Ill-conditioned systems, rank deficiency, and poorly scaled units require further diagnostics and possibly specialized solvers.

## 4. Back-substitution

After elimination, solve the last equation first. For upper-triangular \(U\),

\[
x_i=\frac{c_i-\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}u_{ij}x_j}{u_{ii}},
\]

provided each pivot is nonzero. A zero or near-zero pivot may indicate singularity or a numerically unstable system; do not silently replace it with a guessed value.

## 5. Financial application: matching factor exposures

Suppose two instruments have standardized sensitivities to RATE and CREDIT:

| Instrument | RATE | CREDIT |
|---|---:|---:|
| BOND_A | 2 | 1 |
| BOND_B | 1 | -1 |

To match target factor exposures RATE \(=5\), CREDIT \(=1\), solve

\[
\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}q_A\\q_B\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}5\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The unique solution is \(q_A=2,q_B=1\). Verification: RATE \(=2(2)+1(1)=5\); CREDIT \(=1(2)-1(1)=1\).

Here quantities and factor sensitivities are **illustrative dimensionless units**. A real implementation must use consistent per-instrument exposure units and distinguish instrument quantity, market value, DV01, and factor normalization.

## 6. Residuals and solution verification

For a computed solution \(\hat x\), the residual vector is

\[
r=b-A\hat x.
\]

A small residual means the equations are approximately satisfied, but it does **not** alone prove the solution is accurate when \(A\) is ill-conditioned. Use a scale-aware relative residual, for example

\[
\eta=\frac{\|b-A\hat x\|_\infty}
{\|A\|_\infty\|\hat x\|_\infty+\|b\|_\infty},
\]

with a documented convention for the zero denominator. Condition numbers and perturbation analysis follow in Section 12.7.

## 7. Labeled solver contract

The companion lab requires a square coefficient matrix, unique equation and unknown labels, a right-hand-side vector whose labels match the equation rows, finite values, and explicit units. It aligns \(b\) by equation label before elimination and reports the solution by unknown label.

It uses a scale-aware pivot threshold relative to the maximum absolute matrix entry. This is an introductory guard, not a complete numerical rank test. For production work, prefer validated LAPACK-backed solvers and retain condition diagnostics and independent checks.

## 9. Exercises with worked answers

**1.** Write \(2x+y=5\), \(x-y=1\) in matrix form.  
*Answer:* \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\), \(b=(5,1)^\mathsf T\).

**2.** Write its augmented matrix.  
*Answer:* \(\begin{bmatrix}2&1&|&5\\1&-1&|&1\end{bmatrix}\).

**3.** Eliminate the first coefficient in row 2.  
*Answer:* \(R_2\leftarrow R_2-\frac12R_1\).

**4.** What is the new row 2?  
*Answer:* \((0,-1.5\mid-1.5)\).

**5.** Solve for \(y\).  
*Answer:* \(y=1\).

**6.** Solve for \(x\).  
*Answer:* \(x=2\).

**7.** Verify the first equation.  
*Answer:* \(2(2)+1=5\).

**8.** Verify the second equation.  
*Answer:* \(2-1=1\).

**9.** Why pivot when the first pivot is zero?  
*Answer:* To avoid division by zero and permit elimination.

**10.** Solve \(y=1\), \(2x+3y=5\).  
*Answer:* \(x=1,y=1\).

**11.** What is back-substitution?  
*Answer:* Solving a triangular system from the last unknown upward.

**12.** What is the residual for an exact solution?  
*Answer:* The zero vector.

**13.** Does a tiny residual always imply a highly accurate solution?  
*Answer:* No; ill-conditioning can magnify solution error.

**14.** What is the shape of \(A\) for three equations in three unknowns?  
*Answer:* \((3,3)\).

**15.** What is the factor-matching solution in the worked finance example?  
*Answer:* \(q_A=2,q_B=1\).

**16.** Why align \(b\) by equation labels?  
*Answer:* Shuffled target rows otherwise produce an incorrect solution.
