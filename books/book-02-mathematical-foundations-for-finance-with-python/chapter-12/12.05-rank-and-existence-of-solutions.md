## Learning objectives

Define matrix rank, row and column independence, and augmented-matrix rank; apply the Rouché–Capelli existence criterion; distinguish unique, infinitely many, and inconsistent solutions; identify redundant financial instruments; and implement a transparent rank-classification lab with numerical safeguards.

## 1. Rank and independent information

The rank of a matrix \(A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\) is the dimension of its column space (equivalently, its row space). It counts the number of linearly independent rows or columns:

\[
0\le \operatorname{rank}(A)\le\min(m,n).
\]

A full-column-rank matrix has \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=n\); a full-row-rank matrix has \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=m\). A square \(n\times n\) matrix is invertible precisely when its rank is \(n\).

For example,

\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}
\]

has rank 1 because its second row is twice the first. Two equations do not necessarily provide two independent constraints.

## 2. Rank of the augmented system

For a linear system \(Ax=b\), let \([A\mid b]\) denote the coefficient matrix with \(b\) appended as a final column.

The **Rouché–Capelli criterion** states:

\[
Ax=b\text{ is consistent}\iff
\operatorname{rank}(A)=\operatorname{rank}([A\mid b]).
\]

When consistent, the number of free variables is \(n-\operatorname{rank}(A)\). Therefore:

| Condition | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=\operatorname{rank}([A\mid b])=n\) | Exactly one solution |
| \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=\operatorname{rank}([A\mid b])<n\) | Infinitely many solutions |
| \(\operatorname{rank}(A)<\operatorname{rank}([A\mid b])\) | No exact solution |

This classification holds for rectangular as well as square systems. The number \(n\) is the number of unknowns, not necessarily the number of equations.

## 3. Three worked systems

### Unique solution

\[
\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}5\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Both ranks are 2 and \(n=2\). The unique solution is \((x,y)=(2,1)\).

### Infinitely many solutions

\[
\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}3\\6\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Both ranks are 1, less than \(n=2\). The solutions satisfy \(x+2y=3\). Writing \(y=t\) gives \(x=3-2t\), with any real \(t\).

### No solution

With the same coefficient matrix but \(b=(3,7)^\mathsf T\), the first equation requires \(x+2y=3\) while the second requires \(x+2y=3.5\). Thus \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=1\), but \(\operatorname{rank}([A\mid b])=2\): no exact solution exists.

## 4. Financial application: redundant hedges

Suppose HEDGE_A and HEDGE_B have standardized RATE/CREDIT sensitivities that are proportional:

\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The columns are instrument exposures and the rows are factor equations. HEDGE_B has exactly twice HEDGE_A's sensitivity to both factors. A target \(b=(3,6)^\mathsf T\) is attainable but does not identify a unique hedge allocation. A target \(b=(3,7)^\mathsf T\) is unattainable with these instruments.

Rank deficiency matters because it exposes missing independent hedge directions. A unique portfolio may require additional constraints or an optimization objective, such as minimizing cost, turnover, or risk. Those choices are **not** determined by the linear equations alone.

## 5. Rectangular systems

A system with more equations than unknowns may be consistent and uniquely solvable if the columns are independent and the target lies in their span. Otherwise it is inconsistent. A system with fewer independent equations than unknowns cannot have a unique solution without additional constraints.

For example, the \(1\times2\) system \(x+y=4\) has infinitely many solutions. The \(2\times1\) system \(x=2,\ 2x=4\) has a unique solution; changing the second target to 5 makes it inconsistent.

## 6. Numerical rank and tolerance

Exact rank is an algebraic property. Floating-point computations estimate **numerical rank** relative to a tolerance. A tiny nonzero pivot can be classified as zero at one tolerance and nonzero at another. Consequently, rank reports must disclose the tolerance and scale convention.

The companion lab performs row-echelon elimination with partial pivoting on both \(A\) and \([A\mid b]\), after dividing each nonzero column by its largest absolute entry; zero columns stay zero. Both normalized arrays use the disclosed tolerance as the pivot threshold. The normalized coefficient columns are identical in both arrays, so changing the target cannot change the reported rank of A. Independent nonzero column rescaling preserves exact rank, but this numerical convention measures dependence after column equilibration rather than the original magnitude of a small column. This teaching method is not a substitute for singular-value decomposition (SVD), condition diagnostics, or a production-grade rank-revealing factorization.

**Important:** The rank of an augmented matrix is sensitive to units and scaling. Production exposure targets must use consistent, documented units; numerical rank thresholds should be chosen using the model's precision requirements.

## 8. Exercises with worked answers

**1.** What is the maximum rank of a \(3\times5\) matrix?  
*Answer:* 3.

**2.** What is the rank of the \(2\times2\) identity?  
*Answer:* 2.

**3.** What is the rank of the zero \(3\times2\) matrix?  
*Answer:* 0.

**4.** Find the rank of \(\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}\).  
*Answer:* 1.

**5.** What does full column rank mean for \(m\times n\) matrix \(A\)?  
*Answer:* \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=n\).

**6.** What is the consistency criterion for \(Ax=b\)?  
*Answer:* \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=\operatorname{rank}([A\mid b])\).

**7.** If both ranks are 3 and \(n=3\), how many solutions exist?  
*Answer:* Exactly one.

**8.** If both ranks are 2 and \(n=4\), how many free variables exist?  
*Answer:* 2; infinitely many solutions.

**9.** If \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=1\) and \(\operatorname{rank}([A\mid b])=2\), is the system consistent?  
*Answer:* No.

**10.** Solve \(x+2y=3\) parametrically.  
*Answer:* \((x,y)=(3-2t,t)\), \(t\in\mathbb R\).

**11.** Is \(x+2y=3,\ 2x+4y=7\) consistent?  
*Answer:* No.

**12.** Is \(x+y=4\) a unique two-variable solution?  
*Answer:* No; one free variable.

**13.** Solve \(x=2,\ 2x=4\).  
*Answer:* Unique solution \(x=2\).

**14.** Solve \(x=2,\ 2x=5\).  
*Answer:* No exact solution.

**15.** What is the rank of a square invertible \(n\times n\) matrix?  
*Answer:* \(n\).

**16.** Why do proportional hedge columns prevent a unique allocation?  
*Answer:* They represent the same exposure direction, leaving an undetermined allocation degree of freedom.

**17.** Does a consistent rank-deficient system determine the cheapest hedge?  
*Answer:* No; cost and additional constraints are separate modeling inputs.

**18.** Why disclose the numerical rank tolerance?  
*Answer:* Near-dependent systems can change classification when tolerance changes.

## 9. Applied assessment

Classify three labeled hedge-matching systems: a full-rank unique match, a redundant but attainable target, and a redundant unattainable target. Permute target equation labels and verify unchanged classification. Reject ragged matrices, duplicate labels, missing equations, nonfinite inputs, and invalid tolerance.

**Acceptance:** unique system ranks \(2/2\); attainable redundant system ranks \(1/1\) and has one free variable; inconsistent redundant system ranks \(1/2\); valid permutations preserve classifications.
