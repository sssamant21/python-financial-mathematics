## Learning objectives

Define determinants and inverses; calculate the determinant and inverse of a \(2\times2\) matrix; connect invertibility to rank and unique solvability; interpret determinant scaling; and distinguish mathematical invertibility from numerical stability in financial exposure matching.

## 1. The determinant

For a square matrix \(A\), its determinant \(\det(A)\) is a scalar describing the signed volume scaling of the associated linear transformation. For

\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \boxed{\det(A)=ad-bc}.
\]

If \(\det(A)=0\), the transformation collapses at least one dimension and the matrix is singular. A nonzero determinant is equivalent to invertibility in exact arithmetic.

**Example:**

\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \det(A)=2(-1)-1(1)=-3.
\]

The negative sign indicates orientation reversal; the absolute value, 3, is the area scaling factor.

## 2. Inverse matrices

An inverse \(A^{-1}\) exists for a square matrix only when

\[
AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I.
\]

For a \(2\times2\) matrix with nonzero determinant,

\[
\boxed{A^{-1}=\frac{1}{ad-bc}
\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}}.
\]

For the preceding example,

\[
A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1/3&1/3\\1/3&-2/3\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Verify that multiplying \(A\) by this matrix gives \(I_2\). A nonsquare matrix has no two-sided inverse of this form; pseudoinverses and least-squares methods are separate concepts.

## 3. Connection to linear systems and rank

For square \(A\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}\), these statements are equivalent in exact arithmetic:

- \(\det(A)\ne0\).
- \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=n\).
- \(A^{-1}\) exists.
- \(Ax=b\) has exactly one solution for every \(b\in\mathbb R^n\).
- The only solution of \(Ax=0\) is \(x=0\).

When invertible, the formal solution is \(x=A^{-1}b\). In computational practice, **solve \(Ax=b\) directly** rather than explicitly computing the inverse just to obtain \(x\).

## 4. Determinant identities

For square \(n\times n\) matrices:

\[
\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B),\qquad
\det(A^\mathsf T)=\det(A),\qquad
\det(I_n)=1.
\]

Swapping two rows reverses the determinant sign. Multiplying one row by \(c\) multiplies the determinant by \(c\). Multiplying the entire matrix by \(c\) multiplies its determinant by \(c^n\).

A triangular matrix has determinant equal to the product of its diagonal entries. Gaussian elimination with pivoting provides a practical route to determinant calculation, provided row swaps are tracked.

## 5. Financial application: exposure matching

Suppose two instruments, BOND_A and BOND_B, have standardized factor sensitivities:

| Factor | BOND_A | BOND_B |
|---|---:|---:|
| RATE | 2 | 1 |
| CREDIT | 1 | -1 |

The coefficient matrix is

\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix},\quad
b=\begin{bmatrix}5\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Because \(\det(A)=-3\ne0\), a unique matching allocation exists:

\[
x=A^{-1}b=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Verification gives RATE \(=2(2)+1=5\) and CREDIT \(=2-1=1\). These are illustrative standardized exposures, not dollar P&L.

## 6. Redundant instruments and singularity

Consider

\[
B=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Here \(\det(B)=4-4=0\). The columns are proportional: the two instruments cannot independently span both factor directions. As Section 12.5 showed, an attainable target can have infinitely many allocations; an unattainable target has none.

## 7. Near-singularity and numerical caution

A determinant can be nonzero yet the system can be highly sensitive to input changes. Consider

\[
C=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1+10^{-10}\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Its determinant is approximately \(10^{-10}\), so it is invertible in exact arithmetic, but its columns are nearly dependent. A tiny perturbation in exposures may produce a large change in allocations.

**Do not use the raw determinant magnitude as a general condition-number test.** Determinants depend on matrix dimension and units; a small determinant alone does not establish ill-conditioning. Numerical conditioning and perturbation analysis are covered in Section 12.7.

## 9. Exercises with worked answers

**1.** Find \(\det\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\).  
*Answer:* \(-3\).

**2.** Find \(\det\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}\).  
*Answer:* \(0\).

**3.** What does determinant zero mean for a square matrix?  
*Answer:* It is singular and has no two-sided inverse.

**4.** Write the \(2\times2\) inverse formula.  
*Answer:* \(A^{-1}=(ad-bc)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}\).

**5.** Find the inverse of \(\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\).  
*Answer:* \(\begin{bmatrix}1/3&1/3\\1/3&-2/3\end{bmatrix}\).

**6.** Find \(\det(I_3)\).  
*Answer:* 1.

**7.** What is \(\det(A^\mathsf T)\)?  
*Answer:* \(\det(A)\).

**8.** What is \(\det(AB)\)?  
*Answer:* \(\det(A)\det(B)\).

**9.** What happens to the determinant after swapping two rows?  
*Answer:* Its sign reverses.

**10.** Find the determinant of \(\operatorname{diag}(2,3,4)\).  
*Answer:* 24.

**11.** What is \(\det(2A)\) for a \(3\times3\) matrix?  
*Answer:* \(8\det(A)\).

**12.** Does a rectangular \(2\times3\) matrix have an ordinary determinant?  
*Answer:* No.

**13.** Does a nonzero determinant guarantee good numerical conditioning?  
*Answer:* No.

**14.** What is the formal solution of \(Ax=b\) when \(A\) is invertible?  
*Answer:* \(x=A^{-1}b\).

**15.** What method is preferred computationally for solving \(Ax=b\)?  
*Answer:* A direct linear-system solver.

**16.** What is the financial allocation in the worked example?  
*Answer:* BOND_A \(=2\), BOND_B \(=1\).

**17.** Why are proportional hedge sensitivities problematic?  
*Answer:* They cannot independently match arbitrary two-factor targets.

**18.** Why is determinant magnitude not a general condition-number diagnostic?  
*Answer:* It depends on scale, units, and dimension and does not by itself measure sensitivity to perturbations.
