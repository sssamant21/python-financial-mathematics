## Learning objectives

Define matrix condition numbers, distinguish conditioning from algorithmic stability, derive relative perturbation bounds, calculate the infinity-norm condition number for \(2\times2\) matrices, and interpret fragile hedge allocations in financial exposure matching.

## 1. Why conditioning matters

Consider \(Ax=b\). An invertible matrix guarantees a unique exact solution, but it does not guarantee that the solution changes only slightly when \(A\) or \(b\) changes. **Conditioning** describes sensitivity inherent in the mathematical problem; **numerical stability** describes the behavior of a computational algorithm.

A well-conditioned problem can be solved poorly by an unstable algorithm. Conversely, even a stable solver cannot eliminate the intrinsic sensitivity of an ill-conditioned problem.

## 2. Vector and matrix norms

The vector infinity norm is

\[
\|x\|_\infty=\max_i|x_i|.
\]

The induced matrix infinity norm is the largest absolute row sum:

\[
\|A\|_\infty=\max_i\sum_j|a_{ij}|.
\]

For a nonsingular square matrix,

\[
\boxed{\kappa_\infty(A)=\|A\|_\infty\|A^{-1}\|_\infty}.
\]

A condition number is at least 1 for every nonsingular matrix in an induced norm. Singular matrices have infinite condition number by convention.

The condition number depends on the norm and on variable/equation scaling. It is **not** a probability, a percentage, or an estimate of actual error in a particular run.

## 3. Perturbing the right-hand side

For fixed invertible \(A\), let \(Ax=b\) and \(A(x+\delta x)=b+\delta b\). Then

\[
A\delta x=\delta b,\qquad \delta x=A^{-1}\delta b.
\]

For nonzero \(b,x\),

\[
\boxed{\frac{\|\delta x\|}{\|x\|}
\le\kappa(A)\frac{\|\delta b\|}{\|b\|}}.
\]

This is a **worst-case upper bound**, not an equality in general. A condition number of 1,000 means some directions of relative input error may be amplified by up to approximately 1,000 in the corresponding norm.

## 4. Perturbing the coefficient matrix

When both \(A\) and \(b\) change,

\[
(A+\delta A)(x+\delta x)=b+\delta b.
\]

Provided \(\|A^{-1}\delta A\|<1\), one useful bound is

\[
\frac{\|\delta x\|}{\|x\|}
\le
\frac{\kappa(A)}
{1-\kappa(A)\|\delta A\|/\|A\|}
\left(
\frac{\|\delta A\|}{\|A\|}
+
\frac{\|\delta b\|}{\|b\|}
\right),
\]

for nonzero \(A,b,x\), using compatible induced norms. This inequality requires \(\kappa(A)\|\delta A\|/\|A\|<1\); do not apply it outside that regime.

## 5. Well-conditioned financial exposure matching

Consider

\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
b=\begin{bmatrix}5\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad x=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Here

\[
\|A\|_\infty=3,\qquad
A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1/3&1/3\\1/3&-2/3\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \|A^{-1}\|_\infty=1.
\]

Thus \(\kappa_\infty(A)=3\). If the CREDIT target changes from 1 to 1.03, then \(\delta b=(0,0.03)^\mathsf T\) and

\[
\delta x=A^{-1}\delta b=(0.01,-0.02)^\mathsf T.
\]

The new allocation is \((2.01,0.98)\).

## 6. Nearly dependent hedges

Now consider

\[
C=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1+\varepsilon\end{bmatrix},
\quad \varepsilon=10^{-4}.
\]

The two hedge instruments have nearly identical sensitivities. The determinant is \(\varepsilon\), so \(C\) is invertible in exact arithmetic, but

\[
C^{-1}=\frac1{\varepsilon}
\begin{bmatrix}1+\varepsilon&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Consequently,

\[
\kappa_\infty(C)
=(2+\varepsilon)\frac{2+\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}
=\frac{(2+\varepsilon)^2}{\varepsilon}
=40004.0001.
\]

Take \(b=(2,2+\varepsilon)^\mathsf T\). The solution is \(x=(1,1)^\mathsf T\). If only the second target increases by \(10^{-3}\), then

\[
\delta x=C^{-1}(0,0.001)^\mathsf T=(-10,10)^\mathsf T,
\]

and the new allocation is \((-9,11)^\mathsf T\). The target changed slightly, but hedge positions moved dramatically.

**Financial interpretation:** Near-duplicate hedges can create extreme long/short allocations even when aggregate factor targets are matched. Monitor gross exposure, funding requirements, turnover, constraints, and uncertainty in sensitivities. A numerically exact match is not automatically a prudent hedge.

## 7. Residual versus forward error

The **residual** is \(r=b-A\hat x\); the **forward error** is \(\hat x-x\). An ill-conditioned system can have a tiny residual and still produce a large forward error when data or arithmetic is perturbed. The residual describes equation satisfaction; the condition number describes potential sensitivity.

For a nonzero \(x,b\), a standard bound for the error induced by a residual in the same fixed system is

\[
\frac{\|x-\hat x\|}{\|x\|}
\le
\kappa(A)\frac{\|r\|}{\|b\|}.
\]

This bound assumes \(x\) solves \(Ax=b\) exactly and uses compatible induced norms.

## 8. Practical safeguards

Use consistent financial units, label-aligned axes, and appropriate scaling. Prefer direct solvers over explicitly forming inverses for production workloads. Log the residual, condition estimate, gross and net positions, and sensitivity to plausible data shocks. Reject nonfinite inputs and singular matrices. Treat a high condition number as a reason for investigation or model redesign, not an automatic universal threshold.

The companion lab intentionally uses transparent \(2\times2\) arithmetic for teaching. Larger or production systems should use established LAPACK-backed routines and SVD-based condition estimates.

## 10. Exercises with worked answers

**1.** Define the vector infinity norm.  
*Answer:* The largest absolute component.

**2.** Define the induced matrix infinity norm.  
*Answer:* The largest absolute row sum.

**3.** What is \(\kappa_\infty(I_2)\)?  
*Answer:* 1.

**4.** What is the condition number of a singular matrix?  
*Answer:* Infinite by convention.

**5.** Is a condition number a measured solution error?  
*Answer:* No; it measures potential sensitivity.

**6.** What is \(\|A\|_\infty\) for \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\)?  
*Answer:* 3.

**7.** What is \(\|A^{-1}\|_\infty\) for this matrix?  
*Answer:* 1.

**8.** Find \(\kappa_\infty(A)\).  
*Answer:* 3.

**9.** For \(\delta b=(0,0.03)^\mathsf T\), find \(\delta x\).  
*Answer:* \((0.01,-0.02)^\mathsf T\).

**10.** What is the updated allocation?  
*Answer:* \((2.01,0.98)^\mathsf T\).

**11.** What is the determinant of \(C=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1+\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}\)?  
*Answer:* \(\varepsilon\).

**12.** What is \(\kappa_\infty(C)\) for positive \(\varepsilon\)?  
*Answer:* \((2+\varepsilon)^2/\varepsilon\).

**13.** Evaluate it for \(\varepsilon=10^{-4}\).  
*Answer:* 40004.0001.

**14.** What is \(\delta x\) when \(\delta b=(0,0.001)^\mathsf T\)?  
*Answer:* \((-10,10)^\mathsf T\).

**15.** What is the new allocation if the original is \((1,1)\)?  
*Answer:* \((-9,11)\).

**16.** What is the new allocation's gross exposure?  
*Answer:* 20 standardized quantity units.

**17.** Does a tiny residual prove a small forward error?  
*Answer:* Not without considering conditioning and data accuracy.

**18.** What is the distinction between conditioning and numerical stability?  
*Answer:* Conditioning belongs to the mathematical problem; stability belongs to the algorithm.

**19.** Why must units be consistent before computing a condition number?  
*Answer:* Scaling of equations and unknowns changes norm-based condition numbers and their interpretation.

**20.** What should an SRE/DBRE monitor for a production hedge solver?  
*Answer:* Input quality, residuals, conditioning, position magnitudes, model version, and sensitivity to perturbations.

Deliberately introduce a singular matrix, mismatched factor labels, and nonfinite inputs; confirm they fail closed.
