## Learning outcomes

Formulate linear programs (LPs), recognize feasibility and boundedness, solve a two-variable financial allocation LP by inspecting vertices, explain weak and strong duality, compute dual shadow prices, and distinguish mathematical certificates from solver success flags.

## 1. What is linear programming?

A linear program optimizes an affine objective over linear equality and inequality constraints:

\[
\min_{x\in\mathbb R^n} c^\mathsf Tx\quad
\text{subject to }Ax\le b,\ Ex=d,\ x\ge0.
\]

The feasible region is a polyhedron, hence convex. Linear programs can be feasible with a finite optimum, infeasible, or unbounded in the improving objective direction. A nonempty bounded feasible polyhedron is a polytope.

An affine constant in the objective shifts the optimal value but does not change its minimizers.

## 2. Standard form and sign conventions

A common minimization standard form is

\[
\min c^\mathsf Tx\quad\text{subject to }Ax=b,\ x\ge0.
\]

An inequality \(a^\mathsf Tx\le b\) becomes \(a^\mathsf Tx+s=b\), \(s\ge0\), by adding a slack variable. A free variable may be represented as the difference of two nonnegative variables. Such transformations can increase problem size and affect numerical conditioning.

## 3. Geometry and extreme points

An LP with a finite optimum and a feasible region containing at least one extreme point has an optimal extreme point. For a bounded nonempty polyhedron, an optimal vertex always exists. Not every feasible polyhedron has an extreme point (for example, an affine line), so the common slogan “the optimum occurs at a vertex” needs its assumptions.

When an objective is parallel to a binding edge, multiple optimal solutions may exist. Degeneracy can also cause more active constraints than are needed to define a vertex.

## 4. Financial example: allocate limited funding

A team allocates nonnegative funding amounts \(x\) and \(y\), measured in **thousands of dollars**, across two projects. Their modeled benefits are 5 and 4 benefit units per thousand dollars, respectively. The constraints are:

\[
x+y\le8,\qquad 2x+y\le10,\qquad x,y\ge0.
\]

Maximize

\[
\boxed{5x+4y.}
\]

The first inequality is an overall funding limit; the second is a synthetic risk/resource capacity limit. The benefit coefficients are hypothetical and do not represent guaranteed investment returns.

The feasible vertices are \((0,0),(5,0),(2,6),(0,8)\). The objective values are \(0,25,34,32\), respectively. Thus

\[
\boxed{(x^*,y^*)=(2,6),\qquad B^*=34.}
\]

Both resource constraints bind at the optimum. The dollar allocation is \(\$2{,}000\) and \(\$6{,}000\).

## 5. Dual formulation and weak duality

For the maximization problem \(\max c^\mathsf Tx\) subject to \(Ax\le b,\ x\ge0\), with \(b\ge0\), the dual is

\[
\min_{u\ge0} b^\mathsf Tu\quad\text{subject to }A^\mathsf Tu\ge c.
\]

For the funding example,

\[
\min_{u_1,u_2\ge0}8u_1+10u_2
\]

subject to

\[
u_1+2u_2\ge5,\qquad u_1+u_2\ge4.
\]

Every primal-feasible \(x\) and dual-feasible \(u\) satisfy \(c^\mathsf Tx\le b^\mathsf Tu\) (**weak duality**). The pair \(u^*=(3,1)\) is dual-feasible and has objective \(8(3)+10(1)=34\). Its value matches the primal feasible value 34, certifying global optimality.

For feasible LPs with a finite optimum, strong duality guarantees matching primal and dual optimal values under the usual finite-dimensional LP assumptions.

## 6. Complementary slackness

For the maximization form, complementary slackness is

\[
u_i(b_i-(Ax)_i)=0,\qquad
x_j((A^\mathsf Tu)_j-c_j)=0.
\]

At \(x^*=(2,6)\), both resource slacks are zero. At \(u^*=(3,1)\), both dual constraint slacks are zero:

\[
3+2(1)-5=0,\qquad 3+1-4=0.
\]

Primal feasibility, dual feasibility, and zero duality gap together provide a complete optimality certificate for this LP.

## 7. Shadow prices and sensitivity

The dual values \(u_1=3\) and \(u_2=1\) are local marginal benefit values for relaxing the two resource limits, while the same optimal basis remains valid. Raising the overall budget limit by a small \(\delta\) increases optimal benefit by approximately \(3\delta\), not indefinitely. Shadow prices can change when a different constraint becomes active.

A dual multiplier is denominated in benefit units per unit of its constraint's right-hand side. Do not label it as a cash return without a valid unit conversion.

## 8. Practical LP solvers

The simplex method traverses basic feasible solutions; interior-point methods follow interior trajectories; modern LP implementations may also use presolve, scaling, crossover, and numerical safeguards. SciPy's \`scipy.optimize.linprog\` offers a production-oriented interface, but this section's companion code intentionally uses a small deterministic vertex enumerator to make every candidate auditable.

Vertex enumeration is **only a teaching tool for this two-variable example**, not a general-purpose replacement for an LP solver. Its combinatorial cost and numerical fragility make it unsuitable for large, ill-conditioned, or high-dimensional problems.

## 9. Numerical validation and failure cases

Independently verify nonnegativity, each signed resource slack, objective value, dual inequalities, dual objective, and duality gap. Use explicit tolerances and retain raw residuals.

An infeasible model has no feasible point; an unbounded improving objective has no finite optimum. A solver's failure to converge is not itself a proof of either property. Conflicting funding constraints and unconstrained positive-benefit directions illustrate distinct failure modes.

## 10. Twelve-step hands-on lab

1. Record decision-variable units and objective coefficients.
2. Translate the funding and resource restrictions into inequalities.
3. List candidate intersections of boundary lines.
4. Reject candidates violating nonnegativity or resource limits.
5. Calculate benefits at every feasible vertex.
6. Select the maximum-benefit candidate.
7. Formulate the dual LP.
8. Verify dual feasibility at \((3,1)\).
9. Compute the primal and dual objectives.
10. Verify complementary slackness and zero duality gap.
11. Perturb the funding limit within a stable active-set range and compare the marginal benefit.
12. Run the independent reference checks and preserve runtime evidence for the deferred validation phase.

## 11. Twenty worked exercises

**1.** Define a linear objective.  
*Solution:* An affine function \(c^\mathsf Tx+c_0\).

**2.** Is the intersection of linear half-spaces convex?  
*Solution:* Yes.

**3.** What is a slack variable?  
*Solution:* A nonnegative variable converting a \(\le\) inequality to an equality.

**4.** What does an infeasible LP mean?  
*Solution:* No point satisfies all constraints.

**5.** What does an unbounded maximization LP mean?  
*Solution:* Feasible objective values can grow without finite upper bound.

**6.** State the funding constraint.  
*Solution:* \(x+y\le8\).

**7.** State the second resource constraint.  
*Solution:* \(2x+y\le10\).

**8.** State nonnegativity.  
*Solution:* \(x,y\ge0\).

**9.** Give the benefit objective.  
*Solution:* Maximize \(5x+4y\).

**10.** Find the intersection of the two resource boundaries.  
*Solution:* \((2,6)\).

**11.** Give the feasible axis intercept on the \(x\)-axis.  
*Solution:* \((5,0)\).

**12.** Give the feasible axis intercept on the \(y\)-axis.  
*Solution:* \((0,8)\).

**13.** Find the benefit at \((5,0)\).  
*Solution:* \(25\).

**14.** Find the benefit at \((0,8)\).  
*Solution:* \(32\).

**15.** Find the benefit at \((2,6)\).  
*Solution:* \(34\).

**16.** Give the optimal allocation in dollars.  
*Solution:* \(\$2{,}000\) and \(\$6{,}000\).

**17.** State the dual objective.  
*Solution:* Minimize \(8u_1+10u_2\).

**18.** Find the optimal dual multipliers.  
*Solution:* \((3,1)\).

**19.** What is the duality gap?
