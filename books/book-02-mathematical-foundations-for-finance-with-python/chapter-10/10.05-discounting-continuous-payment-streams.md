## Learning objectives

Derive present value (PV) from continuously paid cash-flow rates; distinguish undiscounted accumulation from discounted valuation; calculate flat-rate and growing-stream PV; generalize to time-varying short-rate paths; derive rate sensitivity; and independently validate analytic results through numerical quadrature.

## 1. Cash-flow rates and the present-value integral

Let \(c(t)\) denote a deterministic net payment rate in currency/year, with \(t\) measured in years. A short interval \(dt\) contributes approximately \(c(t)\,dt\) currency. To value this contribution at time zero, multiply by a dimensionless discount factor \(D(0,t)\):

\[
\boxed{PV_0=\int_0^T c(t)D(0,t)\,dt.}
\]

For a flat continuously compounded annual rate \(r\),

\[
D(0,t)=e^{-rt},\qquad
\boxed{PV_0(r)=\int_0^T c(t)e^{-rt}\,dt.}
\]

The undiscounted accumulated cash amount, \(\int_0^T c(t)\,dt\), is a different quantity. If \(r>0\) and \(c(t)\ge0\), discounting weakly reduces value; for negative rates it can increase value. For signed streams, simple comparisons need not hold.

## 2. Derivation from discrete payments

Partition \([0,T]\) into intervals with widths \(\Delta t_i\) and select sample times \(\xi_i\). Approximate each interval's payment by \(C_i=c(\xi_i)\Delta t_i\). Discounting these discrete approximations gives

\[
PV(P)=\sum_i c(\xi_i)e^{-r\xi_i}\Delta t_i.
\]

If \(c(t)e^{-rt}\) is Riemann-integrable, the mesh tends to zero and the sum converges to the PV integral. This explains why a payment **rate** must be multiplied by an interval width; discounting \(c(t)\) alone would give incorrect units.

## 3. Constant payment rate

For a constant rate \(c(t)=C\) currency/year,

\[
PV_0=C\int_0^T e^{-rt}\,dt
=\begin{cases}
C\dfrac{1-e^{-rT}}r,&r\ne0,\\
CT,&r=0.
\end{cases}
\]

The continuous limit at \(r=0\) is \(CT\). Numerically, \(-\operatorname{expm1}(-rT)/r\) is preferable to \( (1-e^{-rT})/r\) near zero.

**Worked example:** A payment rate of \$1200/year over three years at a 5% continuous discount rate has PV

\[
PV=1200\frac{1-e^{-0.15}}{0.05}.
\]

The undiscounted amount is \$3600. The discounted amount is smaller because the rate is positive.

## 4. Exponentially growing payment rate

Suppose \(c(t)=C_0e^{gt}\), where \(g\) is a continuously compounded payment growth rate. Under a flat discount rate \(r\),

\[
PV_0=C_0\int_0^T e^{(g-r)t}\,dt.
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{PV_0=
\begin{cases}
C_0\dfrac{e^{(g-r)T}-1}{g-r},&g\ne r,\\
C_0T,&g=r.
\end{cases}}
\]

The special case \(g=r\) is essential: growth and discounting cancel exactly. Use \`expm1((g-r)*T)\` for numerical stability.

**Worked example:** For \(C_0=1000\) currency/year, \(g=0.02\), \(r=0.05\), \(T=4\),

\[
PV_0=1000\frac{1-e^{-0.12}}{0.03}.
\]

The model assumes deterministic exponential growth and a flat discount curve; it does not by itself establish market-consistent valuation.

## 5. Linear payment rates

For \(c(t)=a+bt\),

\[
PV_0=aI_0(r,T)+bJ(r,T),
\]

where

\[
I_0(r,T)=\int_0^T e^{-rt}\,dt,\qquad
J(r,T)=\int_0^T te^{-rt}\,dt.
\]

For \(r\ne0\),

\[
I_0=\frac{1-e^{-rT}}r,\qquad
J=\frac{1-(1+rT)e^{-rT}}{r^2}.
\]

At \(r=0\), \(I_0=T\) and \(J=T^2/2\). Near zero, evaluate \(J\) using the stable series derived in Section 10.4 rather than the cancellation-prone direct expression.

## 6. Time-varying instantaneous discount rates

Let \(q(u)\) be a deterministic, integrable instantaneous rate (per year). The discount factor from time \(t\) to time zero is

\[
\boxed{D(0,t)=\exp\left(-\int_0^t q(u)\,du\right).}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{PV_0=\int_0^T c(t)\exp\left(-\int_0^t q(u)\,du\right)\,dt.}
\]

For \(q(u)=r_0+ku\),

\[
\int_0^t q(u)\,du=r_0t+\frac12kt^2,
\]

so

\[
PV_0=\int_0^T c(t)e^{-r_0t-\frac12kt^2}\,dt.
\]

In general this last integral may not have an elementary closed form. Numerical quadrature is then appropriate. An instantaneous forward-rate curve and a short-rate model are not interchangeable without explicit assumptions; here \(q(t)\) is a specified deterministic discounting rate path.

## 7. Sensitivity to a parallel continuous-rate shift

Let \(D(0,t)\) be a fixed baseline discount curve and apply a parallel continuously compounded shift \(h\), so the shifted factor is \(D_h(0,t)=D(0,t)e^{-ht}\). Then

\[
PV(h)=\int_0^T c(t)D(0,t)e^{-ht}\,dt.
\]

For bounded horizons and sufficiently regular, integrable cash-flow rates, differentiating under the integral sign gives

\[
\boxed{\frac{dPV}{dh}=-\int_0^T t\,c(t)D(0,t)e^{-ht}\,dt,}
\]

\[
\boxed{\frac{d^2PV}{dh^2}=\int_0^T t^2c(t)D(0,t)e^{-ht}\,dt.}
\]

At \(h=0\), positive payment rates imply negative first derivative and nonnegative second derivative. For \(PV(0)>0\), duration and convexity analogues are

\[
D=-\frac{PV'(0)}{PV(0)},\qquad
K=\frac{PV''(0)}{PV(0)}.
\]

The first derivative has currency·years units when differentiating with respect to a rate measured in inverse years; normalized duration has years.

## 8. Numerical validation and auditability

A reliable report records the payment-rate model, time horizon, rate convention, valuation date, curve assumptions, units, integration method, and numerical tolerance. Analytic formulas should be cross-checked against independent quadrature for representative scenarios, including \(r=0\), \(g=r\), negative rates, and small net rates.

The midpoint method approximates

\[
PV_0\approx\frac{T}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}
c\left(\left(i+\frac12\right)\frac{T}{n}\right)
D\left(0,\left(i+\frac12\right)\frac{T}{n}\right).
\]

A quadrature agreement check validates arithmetic under the assumed model, not the appropriateness of its cash flows or discount curve.

## 10. Exercises with worked solutions

**1.** A constant payment rate is \$1000/year for two years. Find its undiscounted amount.

*Solution:* \(1000(2)=2000\).

**2.** Write the PV of Exercise 1 at a flat continuous rate of 4%.

*Solution:* \(1000(1-e^{-0.08})/0.04\).

**3.** What is the PV of Exercise 1 when \(r=0\)?

*Solution:* \(2000\), the continuous zero-rate limit.

**4.** Explain why \(c(t)\,dt\) has units of currency.

*Solution:* \(c(t)\) has currency/year and \(dt\) has years.

**5.** Derive PV for \(c(t)=C_0e^{gt}\) under a flat rate \(r\).

*Solution:* Integrate \(C_0e^{(g-r)t}\) from 0 to \(T\).

**6.** What happens when \(g=r\) in Exercise 5?

*Solution:* The integrand is constant \(C_0\), so \(PV=C_0T\).

**7.** For \(c(t)=a+bt\), state the zero-discount PV.

*Solution:* \(aT+bT^2/2\).

**8.** If \(q(t)=r_0+kt\), find \(D(0,t)\).

*Solution:* \(\exp(-r_0t-\frac12kt^2)\).

**9.** For \(c(t)\ge0\) and \(r>0\), compare discounted and undiscounted amounts.

*Solution:* Since \(0<e^{-rt}\le1\), \(PV\le\int_0^T c(t)\,dt\).

**10.** Why can a negative discount rate increase PV above undiscounted accumulation?

*Solution:* \(e^{-rt}>1\) for \(r<0\) and \(t>0\), amplifying positive future flows.

**11.** Find \(dPV/dr\) for a flat rate and regular payment-rate stream.

*Solution:* \(-\int_0^T t\,c(t)e^{-rt}\,dt\).

**12.** Find \(d^2PV/dr^2\).

*Solution:* \(\int_0^T t^2c(t)e^{-rt}\,dt\).

**13.** Convert a +50 bp parallel rate shift to a decimal annual rate.

*Solution:* \(50/10000=0.005\).

**14.** Explain why a successful numerical quadrature check does not certify a financial valuation.

*Solution:* It verifies numerical agreement under the assumptions, not cash-flow accuracy, discount-curve suitability, market calibration, or independent review.
