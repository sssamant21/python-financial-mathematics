## Learning objectives

Distinguish discrete payments from continuous payment rates; derive finite-sum and integral present values; explain timing effects; prove the Riemann-sum limit; quantify approximation error for constant payment rates; and validate results using Python.

## 1. Two payment models

A **discrete cash flow** consists of individual amounts \(C_i\) paid at times \(t_i\). Under a flat continuously compounded discount rate \(r\),

\[
\boxed{PV_{\mathrm{discrete}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n} C_i e^{-rt_i}.}
\]

A **continuous cash-flow stream** has a rate \(c(t)\), measured in currency/year. Its present value over \([0,T]\) is

\[
\boxed{PV_{\mathrm{continuous}}=\int_0^T c(t)e^{-rt}\,dt.}
\]

The difference is not merely mathematical notation. Discrete amounts have units of currency; a continuous rate has units of currency/year and must be multiplied by a time increment before it becomes a payment amount.

## 2. From payment schedules to a continuous stream

Divide \([0,T]\) into \(n\) equal intervals of width \(\Delta t=T/n\). Suppose each interval's payment is approximated by

\[
C_i=c(\xi_i)\Delta t
\]

at a chosen time \(\xi_i\) in that interval. The discrete present value is

\[
PV_n=\sum_{i=1}^n c(\xi_i)e^{-r\xi_i}\Delta t.
\]

If \(c(t)e^{-rt}\) is Riemann-integrable, then as the maximum interval width approaches zero,

\[
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}PV_n=\int_0^T c(t)e^{-rt}\,dt.}
\]

This convergence describes a sequence of increasingly frequent payment schedules whose individual amounts shrink with interval width. It does **not** mean that fixed-size payments can be made infinitely often without changing the total payment amount.

## 3. Payment timing: beginning, midpoint, or end

For a constant payment rate \(c(t)=C\), consider \(n\) payments, each of amount \(C\Delta t\).

- **Beginning of interval:** pay at \(t_i=i\Delta t\), \(i=0,\ldots,n-1\).
- **Midpoint of interval:** pay at \(t_i=(i+1/2)\Delta t\).
- **End of interval:** pay at \(t_i=i\Delta t\), \(i=1,\ldots,n\).

For \(r>0\) and \(C>0\),

\[
PV_{\mathrm{begin}}>PV_{\mathrm{mid}}>PV_{\mathrm{end}}.
\]

Earlier receipts are worth more under positive discounting. At \(r=0\), all three schedules have the same value \(CT\). With negative \(r\), the inequalities reverse for positive payments.

## 4. Exact formula for end-of-period payments

Let \(h=T/n\) and \(q=e^{-rh}\). Each end-of-period payment is \(Ch\), so

\[
PV_{\mathrm{end}}=Ch\sum_{i=1}^n q^i.
\]

For \(r\ne0\),

\[
\boxed{PV_{\mathrm{end}}=Ch\frac{q(1-q^n)}{1-q}.}
\]

For \(r=0\), \(PV_{\mathrm{end}}=CT\). The beginning-of-period value is

\[
PV_{\mathrm{begin}}=Ch\frac{1-q^n}{1-q}
\]

when \(r\ne0\). The midpoint value is

\[
PV_{\mathrm{mid}}=Ch\,q^{1/2}\frac{1-q^n}{1-q}.
\]

For small \(|rh|\), use \`math.expm1\` or an equivalent stable ratio instead of subtracting nearly equal exponentials.

## 5. Continuous-payment limit

The continuous value for constant rate \(C\) is

\[
PV_{\mathrm{continuous}}=
\begin{cases}
C(1-e^{-rT})/r,&r\ne0,\\
CT,&r=0.
\end{cases}
\]

For fixed \(T\), as \(n\to\infty\), \(h\to0\), and all three discrete conventions converge to the continuous value.

A particularly useful exact relationship for \(r\ne0\) is

\[
\boxed{\frac{PV_{\mathrm{end}}}{PV_{\mathrm{continuous}}}
=\frac{rh}{e^{rh}-1}.}
\]

Similarly,

\[
\frac{PV_{\mathrm{begin}}}{PV_{\mathrm{continuous}}}
=\frac{rh\,e^{rh}}{e^{rh}-1},\qquad
\frac{PV_{\mathrm{mid}}}{PV_{\mathrm{continuous}}}
=\frac{rh\,e^{rh/2}}{e^{rh}-1}.
\]

These ratios depend on the interval width and discount rate, not the total horizon or payment scale. The formulas extend continuously to 1 at \(r=0\).

## 6. Error order and financial meaning

For small \(x=rh\),

\[
\frac{x}{e^x-1}=1-\frac{x}{2}+\frac{x^2}{12}+O(x^4),
\]

\[
\frac{xe^x}{e^x-1}=1+\frac{x}{2}+\frac{x^2}{12}+O(x^4),
\]

\[
\frac{xe^{x/2}}{e^x-1}=1-\frac{x^2}{24}+O(x^4).
\]

Thus, for a smooth constant-rate stream, beginning- and end-of-period conventions generally have **first-order** timing bias, while midpoint timing has **second-order** bias. The sign of the midpoint error for positive \(r\) follows from the convexity of the exponential discount factor: midpoint quadrature underestimates its integral.

These statements concern numerical approximations to a continuous stream. A real contractual payment schedule should be valued at its actual dates; it is not an approximation error simply because its PV differs from a continuous-payment model.

## 7. Worked financial example

Suppose \(C=1200\) currency/year, \(T=2\) years, and \(r=0.06\) continuously compounded.

The continuous PV is

\[
PV_{\mathrm{continuous}}=1200\frac{1-e^{-0.12}}{0.06}.
\]

Compare end-of-period payments at \(n=2\) (annual), \(n=8\) (quarterly), \(n=24\) (monthly), and \(n=730\) (roughly daily over two 365-day years). Each individual payment is \(CT/n\), so total nominal payments remain \(CT=2400\). Compute the three timing conventions at each frequency and inspect convergence.

## 8. Irregular schedules and actual cash flows

Real payments may be irregular. For payment amounts \(C_i\) at dates converted to year fractions \(t_i\),

\[
PV=\sum_i C_i D(0,t_i).
\]

The day-count convention, calendar, payment date, and discount curve must be specified. Do not replace irregular payments with a continuous rate without an explicit modeling justification. A payment at time zero is valued without discounting.

## 10. Exercises with worked solutions

**1.** State the discrete PV formula under flat continuous discounting.

*Solution:* \(\sum_i C_i e^{-rt_i}\).

**2.** State the continuous PV formula.

*Solution:* \(\int_0^T c(t)e^{-rt}dt\).

**3.** Why does \(c(t)\Delta t\) have currency units?

*Solution:* Currency/year multiplied by years gives currency.

**4.** For \(C=1000\), \(T=2\), and \(n=4\), what is each equal payment amount?

*Solution:* \(1000(2/4)=500\).

**5.** Give the four end-of-period payment times for Exercise 4.

*Solution:* \(0.5,1.0,1.5,2.0\) years.

**6.** For positive \(C,r\), which timing convention has the highest PV?

*Solution:* Beginning of interval.

**7.** What happens when \(r=0\)?

*Solution:* All timing conventions and the continuous value equal \(CT\).

**8.** What happens to the timing ordering when \(r<0\) and \(C>0\)?

*Solution:* It reverses: end exceeds midpoint exceeds beginning.

**9.** Derive the end-of-period geometric-series factor.

*Solution:* With \(q=e^{-rh}\), \(\sum_{i=1}^nq^i=q(1-q^n)/(1-q)\).

**10.** State the end-payment to continuous-PV ratio.

*Solution:* \(rh/(e^{rh}-1)\) for \(r\ne0\), with limit 1 at zero.

**11.** What is the leading end-of-period relative timing error for small \(rh\)?

*Solution:* Approximately \(-rh/2\).

**12.** What is the leading midpoint relative timing error?

*Solution:* Approximately \(-(rh)^2/24\).

**13.** Why must individual payments shrink when \(n\) increases in a continuous-stream approximation?

*Solution:* To preserve the total payment amount as the number of intervals grows.

**14.** Why is a real monthly-payment schedule not automatically an inaccurate model?

*Solution:* If monthly dates are contractual, its discrete PV is the correct model for those payments; continuous PV is a different payment assumption.
