## Learning objectives

Define level sets, explain why gradients are normal to regular level surfaces, derive implicit trade-off slopes, distinguish local sensitivity-neutral paths from exact invariance, and interpret portfolio risk and valuation isoquants with financial units and constraints.

## 1. Level sets

For a scalar function \(f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R\), a **level set** at value \(c\) is

\[
\boxed{L_c=\{x\in\operatorname{dom}(f):f(x)=c\}}.
\]

In two dimensions, regular level sets are typically curves; in three dimensions, surfaces. In finance, they can represent combinations of model inputs producing the same portfolio variance, discounted value, or forecast return.

A level set fixes a **modeled output**, not every economically important attribute: two points with the same valuation may differ in liquidity, uncertainty, transaction cost, or feasibility.

## 2. Gradient geometry

If \(x(s)\) is a differentiable path lying on a level set, then \(f(x(s))=c\). By the chain rule,

\[
0=\frac{d}{ds}f(x(s))=\nabla f(x(s))^\mathsf T x'(s).
\]

Therefore, the gradient is orthogonal to tangent directions of a regular level set, provided \(\nabla f\ne0\). At a critical point where \(\nabla f=0\), this normal-vector interpretation does not determine a unique tangent.

For \(f(x,y)=c\), if \(f_y\ne0\), the implicit function theorem gives the local slope

\[
\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{f_x}{f_y}}.
\]

This is the marginal trade-off needed to keep the modeled output unchanged.

## 3. Simple example: an ellipse

Let

\[
f(x,y)=x^2+4y^2.
\]

Its level curves \(x^2+4y^2=c\) are ellipses for \(c>0\). The gradient is \((2x,8y)\). At \((2,1)\), the level is \(8\), the gradient is \((4,8)\), and the tangent slope is

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{4}{8}=-\frac12.
\]

The tangent vector \((2,-1)\) is orthogonal to the gradient: \((4,8)\cdot(2,-1)=0\). A finite move along this tangent generally leaves the ellipse unless the path curves appropriately; first-order neutrality is not exact neutrality.

## 4. Financial level curves: fixed future value

For

\[
V(P,r,t)=Pe^{rt},\quad P>0,
\]

hold \(P\) fixed and set \(V=c>0\). Taking logarithms,

\[
rt=\log(c/P).
\]

For \(r\ne0\), the level curve can be written \(t=\log(c/P)/r\). Implicit differentiation gives

\[
\boxed{\left.\frac{dt}{dr}\right|_V=-\frac{V_r}{V_t}=-\frac{t}{r}},
\]

when \(r\ne0\). At \(r=0.05,t=2\), the local slope is \(-40\) years per unit change in the decimal annual rate. Thus a small rate increase of \(0.001\) suggests a time adjustment of approximately \(-0.04\) years to keep \(V\) locally unchanged.

The **exact** time adjustment for a rate shock \(\Delta r\), assuming \(r+\Delta r\ne0\), is

\[
\boxed{\Delta t_{\rm exact}=\frac{rt}{r+\Delta r}-t}.
\]

For \(\Delta r=0.001\), \(t_{\rm new}=0.1/0.051\), so \(\Delta t_{\rm exact}\approx-0.0392156863\). This differs from the linear estimate \(-0.04\).

## 5. Present-value level curves

For \(PV(F,r,t)=Fe^{-rt}\) with fixed \(F>0\), a fixed present value also implies a constant product \(rt\). Consequently, the same local trade-off holds:

\[
\left.\frac{dt}{dr}\right|_{PV}=-\frac{t}{r}.
\]

Although the partial derivatives of present value have opposite signs from future value, the ratio yields the same iso-value slope. This does **not** imply identical valuation levels or risks.

## 6. Portfolio variance contours

For a symmetric covariance matrix

\[
C=\begin{bmatrix}0.04&0.01\\0.01&0.09\end{bmatrix},
\]

portfolio variance is

\[
q(w_1,w_2)=0.04w_1^2+0.02w_1w_2+0.09w_2^2.
\]

The gradient is

\[
\nabla q=(0.08w_1+0.02w_2,\;0.02w_1+0.18w_2).
\]

At \(w=(0.6,0.4)\),

\[
q=0.0336,\qquad \nabla q=(0.056,0.084).
\]

The local slope along an **unconstrained** constant-variance contour is

\[
\boxed{\frac{dw_2}{dw_1}=-\frac{0.056}{0.084}=-\frac23}.
\]

But a fully invested two-asset portfolio must also satisfy \(w_1+w_2=1\), which has slope \(-1\). These slopes differ: a generic constant-variance contour tangent does not preserve the full-investment constraint. Along feasible trades \(w_2=1-w_1\),

\[
\boxed{\frac{dq}{dw_1}=q_{w_1}-q_{w_2}=0.056-0.084=-0.028}.
\]

This is the feasible directional rate of variance change per unit increase in \(w_1\), not a zero-risk-change trade.

## 7. Sensitivity-neutral directions

At a regular point of \(f(x,y)\), the vector

\[
d=(f_y,-f_x)
\]

is tangent to the level curve because \(\nabla f\cdot d=0\). Normalize it if change per unit Euclidean distance is required. In finance, input coordinates often have different units, so any normalized direction requires an explicit scaling convention.

For multiple outputs \(F:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m\), a first-order output-neutral displacement satisfies

\[
J_F(x)d=0.
\]

Nonzero solutions exist when the Jacobian has a nontrivial null space. Such directions are **locally** neutral, not necessarily exactly neutral for finite shocks.

## 8. Curvature and finite moves

If \(d\) is tangent to a level set at \(x\), then the first-order term \(\nabla f(x)^\mathsf Td\) vanishes. A straight move \(x+hd\) can still change the output by

\[
f(x+hd)-f(x)\approx\tfrac12h^2d^\mathsf TH_f(x)d.
\]

Maintaining exact output equality may require a curved adjustment path, re-solving the implicit equation, or full revaluation after each move. Near singular gradients or model boundaries, implicit slopes can be unstable or undefined.

## 10. Twenty worked exercises

**1.** Define a level set of \(f\).  
*Answer:* \(\{x:f(x)=c\}\) for a fixed output \(c\).

**2.** State the level-curve tangent condition.  
*Answer:* \(\nabla f\cdot d=0\).

**3.** When does the gradient supply a nonzero normal?  
*Answer:* At a regular point where \(\nabla f\ne0\).

**4.** Give the implicit slope formula.  
*Answer:* \(dy/dx=-f_x/f_y\) when \(f_y\ne0\).

**5.** Find the gradient of \(x^2+4y^2\).  
*Answer:* \((2x,8y)\).

**6.** Evaluate it at \((2,1)\).  
*Answer:* \((4,8)\).

**7.** Find the ellipse level at \((2,1)\).  
*Answer:* \(8\).

**8.** Find the ellipse tangent slope there.  
*Answer:* \(-1/2\).

**9.** Give one tangent vector.  
*Answer:* \((2,-1)\).

**10.** What equation defines a fixed-\(V\) curve for \(V=Pe^{rt}\)?  
*Answer:* \(rt=\log(V/P)\), with \(P>0\).

**11.** Find the fixed-value rate–time slope.  
*Answer:* \(dt/dr=-t/r\) for \(r\ne0\).

**12.** Evaluate that slope at \(r=0.05,t=2\).  
*Answer:* \(-40\).

**13.** Estimate the time shock for \(\Delta r=0.001\).  
*Answer:* \(-0.04\) years.

**14.** Give the exact adjusted maturity.  
*Answer:* \(t_{\rm new}=0.1/0.051\).

**15.** Give the exact time change.  
*Answer:* Approximately \(-0.0392156863\) years.

**16.** Find the reference portfolio variance.

**17.** Find its gradient at \((0.6,0.4)\).

**18.** Find the unconstrained iso-variance slope.
