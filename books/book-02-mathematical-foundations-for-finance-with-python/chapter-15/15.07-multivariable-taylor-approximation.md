## Learning objectives

Derive first- and second-order multivariable Taylor approximations, interpret gradient and Hessian contributions, distinguish approximation error from scenario change, calculate cross-factor interactions, and compare estimates against full financial revaluation.

## 1. Taylor expansion about a base point

For a twice continuously differentiable scalar function \(f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R\), a base point \(x\), and a sufficiently small displacement \(h\),

\[
\boxed{T_1(x;h)=f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\mathsf Th}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{T_2(x;h)=f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\mathsf Th+\tfrac12h^\mathsf TH_f(x)h}.
\]

These estimate the **new level** \(f(x+h)\). Subtract \(f(x)\) to estimate the **change**. For a function with continuous second derivatives, the second-order remainder is \(o(\|h\|^2)\) as \(h\to0\). An explicit \(O(\|h\|^3)\) bound requires stronger third-derivative assumptions over the relevant segment.

## 2. Two-factor expansion

For \(f(x,y)\) and shocks \(h=(\Delta x,\Delta y)\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
T_2-f(x,y)={}&f_x\Delta x+f_y\Delta y\\
&+\tfrac12 f_{xx}(\Delta x)^2
+f_{xy}\Delta x\Delta y
+\tfrac12 f_{yy}(\Delta y)^2,
\end{aligned}
\]

assuming the Hessian is symmetric. The cross term is not halved because the quadratic form contains both off-diagonal entries.

## 3. Exact quadratic worked example

Let

\[
f(x,y)=x^2+3xy+2y^2.
\]

At \((1,2)\), \(f=15\), \(\nabla f=(8,11)^\mathsf T\), and

\[
H_f=\begin{bmatrix}2&3\\3&4\end{bmatrix}.
\]

For \((\Delta x,\Delta y)=(0.1,-0.2)\), the first-order change is \(-1.4\). The second-order correction is

\[
\tfrac12\left[2(0.1)^2+6(0.1)(-0.2)+4(-0.2)^2\right]=0.03.
\]

Thus the second-order change is \(-1.37\), and the predicted level is \(13.63\). Since \(f\) is quadratic, the second-order expression is exact for every displacement.

## 4. Financial example: simultaneous rate and maturity changes

For continuously compounded future value,

\[
V(P,r,t)=Pe^{rt},
\]

hold principal \(P\) fixed and expand in rate \(r\) and maturity \(t\). Define \(v=Pe^{rt}\). The first derivatives are \(V_r=tv\) and \(V_t=rv\); the second derivatives are

\[
V_{rr}=t^2v,\qquad
V_{rt}=V_{tr}=(1+rt)v,\qquad
V_{tt}=r^2v.
\]

The first-order change estimate is

\[
\boxed{\Delta V_1=v(t\Delta r+r\Delta t)}
\]

and the second-order change estimate is

\[
\boxed{\Delta V_2=v\left[
t\Delta r+r\Delta t+
\tfrac12t^2(\Delta r)^2+
(1+rt)\Delta r\Delta t+
\tfrac12r^2(\Delta t)^2
\right]}.
\]

The exact change is

\[
\boxed{\Delta V_{\rm exact}
=P\left(e^{(r+\Delta r)(t+\Delta t)}-e^{rt}\right)}.
\]

At \(P=1000\), \(r=0.05\), \(t=2\), \(\Delta r=0.01\), and \(\Delta t=0.5\),

\[
\Delta V_1=0.045(1000e^{0.1}),
\qquad
\Delta V_2=0.0510125(1000e^{0.1}),
\]

while the exact change is \(1000(e^{0.15}-e^{0.1})\). Full revaluation is the reference result, not either Taylor estimate.

## 5. Where the approximation error comes from

The new exponent can be written

\[
(r+\Delta r)(t+\Delta t)
=rt+t\Delta r+r\Delta t+\Delta r\Delta t.
\]

The interaction \(\Delta r\Delta t\) affects the exponent even before the exponential nonlinearity is considered. The Hessian's mixed term captures this effect to second order, together with the nonlinear cross contribution from the exponential.

A first-order approximation ignores quadratic and higher-order effects. A second-order approximation includes quadratic terms but still omits higher-order effects. Increasing the shock can increase error; a smaller error in one example is not a universal guarantee of a more accurate second-order estimate for all finite shocks.

## 6. Discounted cash-flow Taylor approximation

For

\[
PV(F,r,t)=Fe^{-rt},
\]

hold \(F\) fixed. Writing \(p=Fe^{-rt}\),

\[
\nabla_{r,t}PV=(-tp,-rp)^\mathsf T,
\]

\[
H_{r,t}PV=
p\begin{bmatrix}
t^2&rt-1\\
rt-1&r^2
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Hence

\[
\begin{aligned}
\Delta PV_2={}&p(-t\Delta r-r\Delta t)\\
&+\tfrac12p\left[t^2(\Delta r)^2
+2(rt-1)\Delta r\Delta t
+r^2(\Delta t)^2\right].
\end{aligned}
\]

A rate increase typically lowers this present value when \(F>0,t>0\); the mixed sensitivity can have either sign depending on \(rt\).

## 7. Error metrics and model controls

For each scenario, report the base value, exact shocked value, first-order and second-order estimated changes, and **signed errors** \(E_1=\Delta V_1-\Delta V_{\rm exact}\), \(E_2=\Delta V_2-\Delta V_{\rm exact}\). Absolute errors are \(|E_i|\). Relative error should not be reported when the exact change is zero or too close to zero without a clearly stated convention.

Document decimal-rate units, maturity units, which inputs are fixed, domain restrictions, and whether model parameters are recalibrated under stress. These formulas assume a fixed mathematical model and do not capture liquidity, volatility, or counterparty changes unless explicitly modeled.

## 8. Numerical verification

Compare analytic derivatives with centered finite differences at interior points, using scale-appropriate steps. For a second derivative, extremely small steps may amplify floating-point cancellation. For the reference valuation, compare Taylor estimates against direct evaluation using \`math.exp\`. Do not treat source-level check code as executed evidence.

## 10. Twenty worked exercises

**1.** State the first-order multivariable Taylor approximation.  
*Answer:* \(T_1=f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\mathsf Th\).

**2.** State the second-order approximation.  
*Answer:* \(T_2=T_1+\tfrac12h^\mathsf TH_fh\).

**3.** What does \(T_2\) approximate?  
*Answer:* The shocked function level \(f(x+h)\).

**4.** How do you approximate the change instead?  
*Answer:* Subtract the base level \(f(x)\).

**5.** Find the gradient of \(x^2+3xy+2y^2\).  
*Answer:* \((2x+3y,3x+4y)\).

**6.** Find its Hessian.  
*Answer:* \(\begin{bmatrix}2&3\\3&4\end{bmatrix}\).

**7.** Evaluate the gradient at \((1,2)\).  
*Answer:* \((8,11)\).

**8.** Evaluate the base polynomial.  
*Answer:* \(15\).

**9.** Find its first-order change for \(h=(0.1,-0.2)\).  
*Answer:* \(-1.4\).

**10.** Find the quadratic correction.  
*Answer:* \(0.03\).

**11.** Find the exact shocked polynomial level.  
*Answer:* \(13.63\).

**12.** Why is the second-order approximation exact for this polynomial?  
*Answer:* There are no terms of degree three or higher.

**13.** Find \(V_r\) for \(V=Pe^{rt}\).  
*Answer:* \(tPe^{rt}\).

**14.** Find \(V_t\).  
*Answer:* \(rPe^{rt}\).

**15.** Find \(V_{rr}\).  
*Answer:* \(t^2Pe^{rt}\).

**16.** Find \(V_{rt}\).  
*Answer:* \((1+rt)Pe^{rt}\).

**17.** Find \(V_{tt}\).  
*Answer:* \(r^2Pe^{rt}\).

**18.** Give the reference first-order future-value change.  
*Answer:* \(0.045(1000e^{0.1})\).

**19.** Give the reference second-order future-value change.  
*Answer:* \(0.0510125(1000e^{0.1})\).
