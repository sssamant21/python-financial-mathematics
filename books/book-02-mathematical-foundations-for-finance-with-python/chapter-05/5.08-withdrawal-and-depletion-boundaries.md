## Learning objectives

Define depletion and withdrawal feasibility; derive a constant-rate depletion threshold; distinguish beginning- and end-of-period withdrawals; implement a no-overdraft policy; detect first infeasible payments; and distinguish mathematical balances from practical liquidity constraints.

## 1. Withdrawal recurrence and feasibility

Let \(B_0\ge0\), periodic effective rate \(i>-1\), and fixed withdrawal \(W>0\). For **end-of-period** withdrawals,

\[
\boxed{B_n=(1+i)B_{n-1}-W.}
\]

A payment is feasible under a strict no-borrowing policy when the balance **after** that payment is nonnegative:

\[
\boxed{(1+i)B_{n-1}\ge W.}
\]

The first period violating this inequality is the **first infeasible withdrawal**. An exact zero closing balance is feasible but indicates the account is depleted after payment.

For a **beginning-of-period** withdrawal, cash must be available *before* growth. The feasibility condition is \(B_{n-1}\ge W\), and the update is

\[
\boxed{B_n=(B_{n-1}-W)(1+i).}
\]

Checking only the closing balance without specifying timing can misrepresent liquidity.

## 2. Closed-form depletion boundary

Set \(q=1+i\). The end-of-period recurrence yields

\[
B_n=q^nB_0-W\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}q^k.
\]

For \(i\ne0\),

\[
\boxed{B_n=q^nB_0-W\frac{q^n-1}{i}.}
\]

For \(i=0\),

\[
\boxed{B_n=B_0-nW.}
\]

When \(i>0\), the fixed-point balance is \(B^*=W/i\). If \(B_0=B^*\), the deterministic balance remains constant. If \(0\le B_0<B^*\), repeated withdrawals eventually become infeasible. If \(B_0>B^*\), this constant-rate model predicts growth rather than depletion. The fixed point is unstable to changes in starting balance or assumptions; it is not a guarantee of financial sustainability.

For \(-1<i<0\) and \(W>0\), a nonnegative initial balance eventually depletes under continuing withdrawals.

## 3. Finding the first infeasible payment

When \(i=0\), the first infeasible end-of-period withdrawal occurs at

\[
\boxed{n_{\mathrm{fail}}=\left\lfloor\frac{B_0}{W}\right\rfloor+1}
\]

for \(B_0\ge0\), \(W>0\). Exact exhaustion at an earlier integer period is allowed; the following payment fails.

For \(i>0\) and \(0\le B_0<W/i\), write \(A=W/i-B_0>0\). The balance is

\[
B_n=\frac{W}{i}-A(1+i)^n.
\]

The first infeasible integer \(n\) is the smallest \(n\ge1\) with

\[
(1+i)^n>\frac{W/i}{A}.
\]

Because the inequality is strict, equality corresponds to an **exactly depleted but feasible** payment. In floating-point implementations, compute candidate indices cautiously and verify the recurrence around the boundary; near-equality and rounding can change the result.

For irregular rates or withdrawals, simulate the schedule period by period and apply the chosen feasibility policy.

## 4. Worked example: zero interest

An account begins with 500 and withdraws 200 at the end of each period with no interest.

\[
B_1=300,\quad B_2=100,\quad B_3=-100.
\]

The third full withdrawal is infeasible under a no-overdraft policy. The maximum feasible third withdrawal is 100. If the policy allows a **partial final withdrawal**, the account can pay 100 and close at zero; otherwise the third withdrawal is rejected.

## 5. Worked example: positive interest

With \(B_0=500\), \(i=0.01\), and \(W=200\), the unconstrained path is

\[
B_1=305,\quad B_2=108.05,\quad B_3=-90.8695.
\]

The first two withdrawals are feasible. At period 3, the available post-interest balance is \(108.05(1.01)=109.1305\), less than 200. The first infeasible withdrawal is therefore period 3.

## 6. Withdrawal policies

A model must explicitly choose how to handle a requested withdrawal larger than available funds:

- **Reject:** do not execute the infeasible payment; report the first failure.
- **Partial:** pay up to the available balance, then stop or continue according to a separate policy.
- **Borrow:** permit a negative balance, if the financial product supports borrowing and its rate/fees are modeled.

The companion lab implements a **reject-and-stop** policy for a fixed schedule, with optional partial payment. It does not model credit facilities or fees.

## 7. Variable-rate stress tests

With variable returns \(i_n\) and requested withdrawals \(W_n\), define the available amount for end-of-period payment \(A_n=(1+i_n)B_{n-1}\). If \(A_n<W_n\), the requested payment is infeasible.

Stress tests should consider lower returns, larger withdrawals, and withdrawal timing. Deterministic scenarios do not establish a probability of depletion; stochastic simulation and a defensible return model are separate topics.

## 8. Numerical and modeling cautions

Use tolerances only for diagnostic comparisons, not to silently authorize withdrawals that exceed available funds. For currency systems, specify decimal precision and transaction rounding before enforcing financial policy. The examples use unrounded floating-point arithmetic and abstract equal-length periods.
