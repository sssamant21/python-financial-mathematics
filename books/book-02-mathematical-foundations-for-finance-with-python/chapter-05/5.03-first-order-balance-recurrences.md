## 1. A first-order recurrence

A first-order recurrence defines the next state from the immediately preceding state. For an account balance \(B_n\), periodic rate \(i_n\), and end-of-period cash flow \(C_n\),

\[
\boxed{B_n=(1+i_n)B_{n-1}+C_n,\qquad n=1,\ldots,N.}
\]

The initial condition \(B_0\) is essential. A recurrence alone does not determine a unique balance path.

Here, \(C_n>0\) denotes a deposit and \(C_n<0\) a withdrawal. The rate \(i_n\) is the return credited **during period n**, before its end-of-period cash flow. We require \(1+i_n>0\) when using the positive-factor growth model; other accounting systems may use different conventions.

## 2. Deriving the update rule

At the beginning of period \(n\), the balance is \(B_{n-1}\). Growth over the period adds \(i_n B_{n-1}\). The end-of-period contribution then adds \(C_n\):

\[
B_n=B_{n-1}+i_nB_{n-1}+C_n.
\]

Collecting terms yields the recurrence above.

If a cash flow occurs at the **beginning** of period \(n\), it participates in that period's growth:

\[
\boxed{B_n=(B_{n-1}+C_n)(1+i_n).}
\]

These models should not be mixed silently.

## 3. Worked example: end-of-period deposits

Let \(B_0=1000\), \(i_n=0.02\), and \(C_1=C_2=C_3=100\). Then

\[
B_1=1000(1.02)+100=1120,
\]

\[
B_2=1120(1.02)+100=1242.40,
\]

\[
B_3=1242.40(1.02)+100=1367.248.
\]

For beginning-of-period deposits, the first period instead gives \((1000+100)(1.02)=1122\), so timing changes the result.

## 4. Withdrawals and depletion

Suppose \(B_0=500\), the rate is 1% per period, and the end-of-period withdrawal is 200:

\[
B_1=500(1.01)-200=305,\quad
B_2=305(1.01)-200=108.05,
\]

\[
B_3=108.05(1.01)-200=-90.8695.
\]

A negative mathematical balance may represent debt, overdraft, or an infeasible withdrawal depending on the account rules. **The recurrence does not automatically enforce a no-overdraft policy.** An application must explicitly choose whether to reject a withdrawal, stop the schedule, or allow borrowing.

## 5. Variable rates and cash flows

The recurrence naturally supports varying rates and cash flows:

\[
B_n=(1+i_n)B_{n-1}+C_n.
\]

For example, let \(B_0=1000\), rates \((0.01,-0.02,0.03)\), and end-of-period cash flows \((100,0,-50)\):

\[
B_1=1110,\quad
B_2=1087.8,\quad
B_3=1087.8(1.03)-50=1070.434.
\]

A single constant average rate generally does not reproduce a variable-rate path when cash flows occur at different times.

## 6. Balance identity and reconciliation

For each end-of-period update,

\[
\boxed{B_n-B_{n-1}=i_nB_{n-1}+C_n.}
\]

The left side is the observed balance change. The right side separates credited growth from external cash flow. Summing over periods gives

\[
B_N-B_0=\sum_{n=1}^N i_nB_{n-1}+\sum_{n=1}^N C_n.
\]

This telescoping identity is a useful independent reconciliation check for implementations and financial reports.

For beginning-of-period cash flows, growth is \(i_n(B_{n-1}+C_n)\), so the decomposition must change accordingly.

## 7. Repeated substitution

For constant \(i\) and \(C\), let \(q=1+i\). Expanding the first few steps,

\[
B_1=qB_0+C,
\]

\[
B_2=q^2B_0+qC+C,
\]

\[
B_3=q^3B_0+q^2C+qC+C.
\]

The pattern is

\[
\boxed{B_N=q^NB_0+C\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^k.}
\]

The next section develops this closed form and its edge cases systematically. For now, the recurrence is the primary operational model.

## 9. Exercises

**1.** Explain why the initial condition is required to compute a recurrence path.

**2.** Derive \(B_n=(1+i_n)B_{n-1}+C_n\) for end-of-period deposits.

**3.** Compute the first two balances for \(B_0=1000\), rate 2%, and end-of-period deposits of 100.

**4.** Compute the first balance for the same data if the deposit occurs at the beginning of the period.

**5.** Find the balance after three end-of-period withdrawals of 200 from an initial balance of 500 at 1% per period. Explain the negative result.

**6.** Compute the final balance for rates \(1\%,-2\%,3\%\) and cash flows \(100,0,-50\), starting from 1000.

**7.** State the telescoping reconciliation identity for an end-of-period balance recurrence.

**8.** Expand the constant-rate, constant-payment recurrence through three periods and identify the geometric sum.

### Worked solutions

1. Different \(B_0\) values produce different paths under the same update rule.
2. Add period growth \(i_nB_{n-1}\) and end-of-period cash flow \(C_n\) to \(B_{n-1}\).
3. \(B_1=1120\), \(B_2=1242.40\).
4. \(B_1=(1000+100)(1.02)=1122\).
5. \(B_3=-90.8695\); a no-overdraft account would require an explicit rejection or adjustment rule.
6. \(B_1=1110\), \(B_2=1087.8\), \(B_3=1070.434\).
7. \(B_N-B_0=\sum i_nB_{n-1}+\sum C_n\).
8. \(B_3=q^3B_0+C(q^2+q+1)\), where \(q=1+i\).
