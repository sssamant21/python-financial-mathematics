## Learning objectives

Derive closed forms from first-order balance recurrences; identify geometric sums; handle zero-interest limits; distinguish end-of-period and beginning-of-period contributions; generalize to variable cash flows and rates; and verify formulas numerically.

## 1. From recurrence to closed form

Consider a constant per-period rate \(i>-1\), constant end-of-period contribution \(C\), and initial balance \(B_0\):

\[
B_n=(1+i)B_{n-1}+C,\qquad n\ge1.
\]

Let \(q=1+i>0\). Substitution gives

\[
B_1=qB_0+C,
\]

\[
B_2=q(qB_0+C)+C=q^2B_0+qC+C,
\]

\[
B_3=q^3B_0+q^2C+qC+C.
\]

By induction,

\[
\boxed{B_n=q^nB_0+C\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}q^k.}
\]

For \(q\ne1\), the geometric sum yields

\[
\boxed{B_n=q^nB_0+C\frac{q^n-1}{q-1}
=(1+i)^nB_0+C\frac{(1+i)^n-1}{i}.}
\]

For \(i=0\), use the limit directly:

\[
\boxed{B_n=B_0+nC.}
\]

This is a **closed form**: it computes \(B_n\) without iterating through all earlier balances.

## 2. Why the timing convention matters

The formula above assumes each contribution arrives **after** the period's growth. For beginning-of-period contributions,

\[
B_n=q(B_{n-1}+C)=qB_{n-1}+qC.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{B_n=q^nB_0+qC\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}q^k.}
\]

The initial-balance term is identical; only the contribution term gains one additional factor \(q\). At zero rate, both conventions give \(B_0+nC\).

## 3. Worked example

Let \(B_0=1000\), \(i=0.02\), \(C=100\), and \(n=3\). Then

\[
B_3=1000(1.02)^3+
100\frac{(1.02)^3-1}{0.02}.
\]

Since \((1.02)^3=1.061208\) and the contribution factor is \(3.0604\),

\[
B_3=1061.208+306.04=\boxed{1367.248}.
\]

This agrees with the period-by-period path from Section 5.3.

For beginning-of-period deposits,

\[
B_3=1061.208+1.02(306.04)=\boxed{1373.3688}.
\]

## 4. Variable cash flows with constant rates

When the rate is constant but the end-of-period cash flows vary,

\[
B_n=qB_{n-1}+C_n,
\]

the closed form becomes

\[
\boxed{B_N=q^NB_0+\sum_{k=1}^{N}q^{N-k}C_k.}
\]

The payment \(C_k\) accumulates for \(N-k\) additional periods. In particular, \(C_N\) earns no additional interest before the final measurement.

## 5. Variable rates and variable cash flows

With positive period factors \(q_j=1+i_j\),

\[
B_n=q_nB_{n-1}+C_n.
\]

Repeated substitution yields

\[
\boxed{
B_N=B_0\prod_{j=1}^{N}q_j+
\sum_{k=1}^{N}C_k\prod_{j=k+1}^{N}q_j.
}
\]

An empty product equals one. Thus the final cash flow \(C_N\) has weight one. This formula is a useful independent check against recurrence iteration, although its direct implementation may require more work than the recurrence.

## 6. Numerical stability and limitations

When \(i\) is small, direct evaluation of \(((1+i)^n-1)/i\) may lose precision through cancellation. For \(i\ne0\), use

\[
\boxed{\frac{\operatorname{expm1}(n\operatorname{log1p}(i))}{i}}.
\]

At \(i=0\), return \(n\) exactly as the limiting factor.

The closed form is not automatically superior to the recurrence. Iteration is often easier for irregular rates, changing contributions, account restrictions, or transaction-level rounding. Closed forms are valuable for algebraic insight, independent reconciliation, and fast evaluation of constant-parameter models.

The mathematical examples use unrounded balances and a fixed rate per completed period. They do not implement fees, taxes, day-count rules, or contractual rounding.

## 8. Exercises

**1.** Starting from \(B_n=qB_{n-1}+C\), derive \(B_2\) and \(B_3\) by substitution.

**2.** Prove the constant-payment closed form by induction.

**3.** Compute \(B_3\) for \(B_0=1000\), \(i=0.02\), \(C=100\), with end-of-period deposits.

**4.** Recompute the result for beginning-of-period deposits.

**5.** What does the constant-payment formula become when \(i=0\)?

**6.** Write the closed form for varying end-of-period cash flows \(C_1,\ldots,C_N\) at a fixed rate \(i\).

**7.** For variable rates \(i_1,i_2\) and cash flows \(C_1,C_2\), expand \(B_2\) explicitly.

**8.** Explain why \`expm1(n*log1p(i))/i\` is preferable to the naïve expression when \(i\) is tiny.

### Worked solutions

1. \(B_2=q^2B_0+qC+C\); \(B_3=q^3B_0+(q^2+q+1)C\).
2. Assume \(B_n=q^nB_0+C\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}q^k\). Then \(B_{n+1}=qB_n+C=q^{n+1}B_0+C\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^k\). The base case \(n=0\) holds by the empty-sum convention.
3. \(1367.248\).
4. \(1373.3688\).
5. \(B_n=B_0+nC\).
6. \(B_N=q^NB_0+\sum_{k=1}^Nq^{N-k}C_k\).
7. \(B_2=(1+i_2)(1+i_1)B_0+(1+i_2)C_1+C_2\).
8. \`log1p\` and \`expm1\` reduce cancellation and preserve small changes that \`(1+i)**n - 1\` may round away.
