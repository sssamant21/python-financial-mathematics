## Learning objectives

Define a sequence and its index set; distinguish zero-based and one-based financial time conventions; map indices to calendar or year-fraction times; construct balances and cash-flow schedules; detect off-by-one errors; and implement a reproducible Python lab with explicit validation.

## 1. What is a sequence?

A sequence is a function whose domain is an ordered set of integers. For a discrete financial model, a sequence might be

\[
(B_n)_{n=0}^{N}=(B_0,B_1,\ldots,B_N),
\]

where \(B_n\) is the account balance at time index \(n\). The subscript is an **index**, not necessarily a number of years. The time interpretation must be supplied separately.

For example, with a starting balance of 1000 and a 2% return per completed period, before contributions or withdrawals,

\[
B_n=1000(1.02)^n,\quad n=0,1,2,3.
\]

The sequence begins at \(B_0=1000\). The first credited interest occurs between \(B_0\) and \(B_1\), not at index zero.

## 2. Indexing conventions

**Zero-based indexing** typically labels the initial state \(B_0\) and the state after the first period \(B_1\).

**One-based indexing** may label the first payment \(C_1\), occurring at the **end of period 1**. Mixing \(B_0\) with \(C_1\) is valid if the convention is explicit.

| Index | Elapsed periods | Balance interpretation | End-of-period cash flow |
|---:|---:|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | Initial balance \(B_0\) | None |
| 1 | 1 | Balance after period 1 \(B_1\) | \(C_1\) |
| 2 | 2 | Balance after period 2 \(B_2\) | \(C_2\) |
| 3 | 3 | Balance after period 3 \(B_3\) | \(C_3\) |

A common mistake is to discount a payment at \(n=1\) as if it occurred at time zero. The present value of an end-of-period payment \(C_1\) at per-period rate \(i>-1\) is \(C_1/(1+i)\), not \(C_1\).

## 3. From index to time

If each period has duration \(\Delta t>0\) years, then

\[
t_n=n\Delta t.
\]

For monthly periods using a simple year-fraction convention, \(\Delta t=1/12\), so \(t_{12}=1\) year.

This regular-grid convention is **not** a calendar day-count rule. Actual months have different lengths, and financial contracts may use Actual/365, Actual/360, 30/360, business-day adjustments, or other conventions. Do not treat \(n/12\) as the elapsed year fraction for every contract without checking its rules.

## 4. Cash-flow timing and balance recurrences

Consider a period rate \(i\), a starting balance \(B_0\), and an **end-of-period** contribution \(C_n\), where negative values represent withdrawals:

\[
\boxed{B_n=(1+i)B_{n-1}+C_n,\qquad n=1,\ldots,N.}
\]

The contribution is applied **after** that period's growth. For a contribution at the beginning of each period instead,

\[
B_n=(B_{n-1}+C_n)(1+i).
\]

The two formulas generally produce different balances.

### Worked example

Starting with \(B_0=1000\), a 1% periodic rate, and contributions of 100 at the end of each of two periods:

\[
B_1=1000(1.01)+100=1110,
\]

\[
B_2=1110(1.01)+100=1221.10.
\]

If the contributions instead occur at each period's beginning:

\[
B_1=(1000+100)(1.01)=1111,
\]

\[
B_2=(1111+100)(1.01)=1223.11.
\]

The timing difference matters even though the contribution amounts and quoted rate are unchanged.

## 5. Separate time labels from data positions

Python lists are zero-indexed. If \`balances[0]\` stores \(B_0\), then \`balances[n]\` can store \(B_n\) directly. But if \`payments[0]\` stores \(C_1\), the mapping is

\`\`\`python
payment_for_period_n = payments[n - 1]
\`\`\`

for \(n\ge1\). Writing \`payments[n]\` silently shifts the schedule by one period and may fail only at the final period.

Keep the following distinctions explicit:

- **State index:** \(B_n\), balance after \(n\) completed periods.
- **Payment index:** \(C_n\), payment assigned to period \(n\).
- **Array position:** Python offset, starting at zero.
- **Time:** \(t_n\), the elapsed time associated with state \(B_n\).

## 6. Building and checking a time grid

For \(N\ge0\) completed periods, the time grid contains \(N+1\) points:

\[
(t_0,\ldots,t_N)=(0,\Delta t,\ldots,N\Delta t).
\]

For monthly periods and a one-year horizon, the grid has 13 observations (including time zero) and 12 intervals. This is a useful invariant for tests.

An irregular schedule may instead provide an explicit sequence of increasing times. In that case, time differences \(\Delta t_n=t_n-t_{n-1}\) vary and should not be replaced with a fixed \(\Delta t\).

## 8. Exercises

**1.** Define a sequence \(B_n\) and explain the meaning of \(B_0\) in a savings model.

**2.** How many time-grid points are needed for 24 monthly intervals if the initial time is included?

**3.** Under the regular-grid convention, what is \(t_9\) for monthly intervals measured in years?

**4.** Derive the recurrence for an end-of-period contribution \(C_n\).

**5.** Compute \(B_2\) for \(B_0=1000\), \(i=0.01\), and two end-of-period contributions of 100.

**6.** Recompute the result if both contributions occur at the beginning of their periods.

**7.** A payment of 500 occurs at the end of period 1. Write its present value at a 5% per-period rate.

**8.** Explain why \`payments[n-1]\` is appropriate when \`payments[0]\` represents \(C_1\).

### Worked solutions

1. \(B_n\) is the balance after \(n\) completed periods; \(B_0\) is the initial balance.
2. 25 grid points, indexed 0 through 24.
3. \(t_9=9/12=0.75\) years under this convention.
4. \(B_n=(1+i)B_{n-1}+C_n\).
5. \(B_1=1110\); \(B_2=1221.10\).
6. \(B_1=1111\); \(B_2=1223.11\).
7. \(PV=500/1.05\).
8. List positions begin at zero, while the first end-of-period payment is labeled \(C_1\).
