## Learning objectives

Define a fixed point of a balance recurrence; derive its existence and uniqueness conditions; analyze deviations from equilibrium; distinguish stable, unstable, and neutral equilibria; interpret equilibrium withdrawals and contributions; and verify results with Python.

## 1. What is a fixed point?

Consider the first-order affine recurrence

\[
B_{n+1}=qB_n+C,\qquad q=1+i>0,
\]

where \(i\) is the constant per-period rate and \(C\) is the constant **end-of-period** cash flow (positive for a deposit, negative for a withdrawal).

A **fixed point** \(B^*\) is a balance that remains unchanged after one complete update:

\[
B^*=qB^*+C.
\]

Rearranging,

\[
(1-q)B^*=C.
\]

When \(q\ne1\), the unique equilibrium is

\[
\boxed{B^*=\frac{C}{1-q}=-\frac{C}{i}.}
\]

The result is an algebraic equilibrium, not a promise that an actual account can sustain the associated cash flows indefinitely.

## 2. Zero-rate special cases

When \(i=0\), the recurrence becomes \(B_{n+1}=B_n+C\).

- If \(C=0\), **every** starting balance is a fixed point; there is no unique equilibrium.
- If \(C\ne0\), **no** fixed point exists because the balance changes by \(C\) each period.

Therefore, the expression \(-C/i\) must never be evaluated at \(i=0\).

## 3. Deviations from equilibrium

Subtract the fixed-point equation from the recurrence:

\[
B_{n+1}-B^*=q(B_n-B^*).
\]

Iterating,

\[
\boxed{B_n-B^*=q^n(B_0-B^*)},
\]

or

\[
\boxed{B_n=B^*+q^n(B_0-B^*)}.
\]

This formula is equivalent to the constant-rate closed form derived in Section 5.4. It makes the dynamics particularly clear: the distance from equilibrium is multiplied by \(q\) each period.

## 4. Stability and convergence

For a general affine recurrence \(B_{n+1}=qB_n+C\), the fixed point is asymptotically stable if \(|q|<1\), unstable if \(|q|>1\), and non-asymptotically stable or neutral at \(|q|=1\) when a fixed point exists. For the positive-factor financial model \(q=1+i>0\):

- **\(-1<i<0\):** \(0<q<1\). Every path converges to the unique fixed point.
- **\(i>0\):** \(q>1\). The fixed point is unstable: any nonzero deviation grows.
- **\(i=0,C=0\):** every balance is fixed; paths are constant, but there is no attracting unique fixed point.
- **\(i=0,C\ne0\):** no fixed point exists.

A negative periodic rate may describe decay, fees, or other value loss; the financial interpretation depends on the application. The restriction \(q>0\) excludes oscillating sign-changing factors.

## 5. Worked example: equilibrium withdrawal

Suppose \(i=0.02\) and \(C=-100\). The fixed point is

\[
B^*=-\frac{-100}{0.02}=\boxed{5000}.
\]

Starting at \(B_0=5000\), one period of 2% growth adds 100 and an end-of-period withdrawal of 100 returns the balance to 5000:

\[
B_1=5000(1.02)-100=5000.
\]

Starting instead at 4000,

\[
B_n=5000+(1.02)^n(4000-5000)
=5000-1000(1.02)^n.
\]

The gap from equilibrium grows because \(q=1.02>1\); eventually the model balance becomes negative. The mathematical equilibrium is therefore **unstable**, even though the exact 5000 balance is unchanged under the idealized constant-rate assumptions.

This model ignores market uncertainty, inflation, transaction costs, taxes, and account constraints. A sustainable withdrawal policy requires more than the equality \(iB^*+C=0\).

## 6. Worked example: stable decay with deposits

Let \(i=-0.10\), \(q=0.90\), and \(C=50\). Then

\[
B^*=-\frac{50}{-0.10}=500.
\]

Starting at \(B_0=100\),

\[
B_n=500+0.9^n(100-500)
=500-400(0.9)^n.
\]

Because \(0<0.9<1\), the balance approaches 500 as \(n\to\infty\).

## 7. Beginning-of-period cash flows

If the same cash flow is made at the beginning of each period,

\[
B_{n+1}=q(B_n+C).
\]

The fixed point solves \(B^*=q(B^*+C)\). For \(q\ne1\),

\[
\boxed{B^*=\frac{qC}{1-q}=-\frac{(1+i)C}{i}.}
\]

It differs from the end-of-period fixed point by a factor \(q\). The deviation dynamics remain \(B_n-B^*=q^n(B_0-B^*)\).

## 8. Numerical considerations

The equilibrium formula becomes ill-conditioned when \(i\) is close to zero: small rate changes can create large changes in \(-C/i\). An enormous equilibrium magnitude may be mathematically correct while financially implausible.

For direct evaluation of the balance path, the deviation formula may lose precision if \(B^*\) and \(q^n(B_0-B^*)\) are very large and nearly cancel. The iterative recurrence or a stable closed form from Section 5.4 may be preferable, depending on the inputs. Avoid claiming that one representation is uniformly best.
