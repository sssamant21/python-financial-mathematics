## Learning objectives

Define eigenvalues and eigenvectors; derive the characteristic equation for a real \(2\times2\) matrix; compute and verify eigenpairs; explain why eigenvectors are directions rather than uniquely normalized vectors; and interpret symmetric two-factor risk matrices without confusing algebraic eigenvalues with empirical risk forecasts.

## 1. The eigenvalue equation

For a square matrix \(A\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}\), a **nonzero** vector \(v\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\lambda\) when

\[
\boxed{Av=\lambda v}.
\]

The matrix maps that particular direction onto itself, possibly reversing it if \(\lambda<0\). The zero vector is excluded because it satisfies the equation for every scalar and carries no directional information.

If \(v\) is an eigenvector, then so is \(cv\) for every nonzero scalar \(c\). A normalized eigenvector has \(\|v\|_2=1\), but its sign remains arbitrary. Repeated eigenvalues may also have multiple independent eigenvector directions.

## 2. Characteristic equation

Rearrange:

\[
(A-\lambda I)v=0.
\]

A nonzero solution exists only when \(A-\lambda I\) is singular, giving the characteristic equation

\[
\boxed{\det(A-\lambda I)=0}.
\]

For

\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix},
\]

the characteristic polynomial is

\[
\lambda^2-(a+d)\lambda+(ad-bc)=0.
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{a+d\pm\sqrt{(a-d)^2+4bc}}{2}}
\]

when the discriminant is nonnegative. A general real matrix can have complex eigenvalues; the companion lab deliberately accepts only real symmetric \(2\times2\) matrices, whose eigenvalues are always real.

## 3. Worked example: symmetric two-factor risk matrix

Consider a hypothetical symmetric matrix

\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Its characteristic polynomial is

\[
(2-\lambda)^2-1=\lambda^2-4\lambda+3
=(\lambda-3)(\lambda-1).
\]

The eigenvalues are \(\lambda_1=3\) and \(\lambda_2=1\), with unit eigenvectors

\[
v_1=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
v_2=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Direct verification:

\[
Av_1=3v_1,\qquad Av_2=v_2,\qquad v_1^\mathsf Tv_2=0.
\]

If the matrix represents a consistently scaled quadratic risk model, the \((1,1)\) direction has larger associated eigenvalue than the \((1,-1)\) direction. This does not by itself establish a future return, probability, or portfolio allocation.

## 4. Trace, determinant, and verification

For a \(2\times2\) matrix, the sum of eigenvalues equals the trace and their product equals the determinant:

\[
\lambda_1+\lambda_2=\operatorname{tr}(A),\qquad
\lambda_1\lambda_2=\det(A).
\]

In the example, \(3+1=4\) and \(3\cdot1=3\), matching the trace and determinant.

A numerical report should check:

- \(\|Av_i-\lambda_iv_i\|_2\), the eigenpair residual.
- \(\|v_i\|_2\approx1\), the unit-length convention.
- \(v_1^\mathsf Tv_2\approx0\), for distinct eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix.
- Trace and determinant identities, within scale-aware tolerances.

## 5. Important edge cases

**Repeated eigenvalues:** For \(A=\alpha I\), every nonzero vector is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\alpha\). No single direction is uniquely identified.

**Zero eigenvalues:** A zero eigenvalue means the matrix is singular. For symmetric matrices, a zero eigenvector belongs to the null space.

**Negative eigenvalues:** Real symmetric matrices can have negative eigenvalues. A matrix intended to be a covariance matrix must instead be positive semidefinite, with nonnegative eigenvalues (allowing small numerical roundoff).

**Nearly repeated eigenvalues:** Individual eigenvectors can be sensitive to perturbations even when eigenvalues remain accurate. Treat directions as potentially unstable when the eigenvalue gap is tiny.

## 6. Stable numerical computation

A direct quadratic formula is useful pedagogically, but robust general eigenvalue computation uses specialized algorithms such as those in \`numpy.linalg.eigh\` for symmetric matrices. The educational lab uses a stable symmetric \(2\times2\) eigenvalue formula based on \`math.hypot\`, with deterministic normalized eigenvectors and independent residual checks.

Never rely on an eigenvector's sign to label an economic factor. An eigenvector and its negative describe the same one-dimensional direction.

## 7. Financial interpretation and unit discipline

Eigenanalysis is meaningful only when the matrix's entries are dimensionally compatible. A symmetric risk matrix might describe a quadratic sensitivity approximation or an estimated covariance structure after appropriate scaling. Mixing unrelated raw factor units can produce misleading eigen-directions. The source, estimation window, factor definitions, and matrix conditioning must be recorded separately from the eigenvalue calculation.

Eigenvalues do not automatically rank the importance of financial factors across scenarios. Their interpretation depends on what the matrix actually represents.

## 8. Worked exercises

**1.** Define an eigenvector.  
*Answer:* A nonzero \(v\) satisfying \(Av=\lambda v\).

**2.** Why exclude the zero vector?  
*Answer:* It satisfies the equation for every \(\lambda\) and does not define a direction.

**3.** What is the characteristic equation?  
*Answer:* \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\).

**4.** State the characteristic polynomial of a \(2\times2\) matrix.  
*Answer:* \(\lambda^2-(a+d)\lambda+(ad-bc)\).

**5.** What is the trace of the reference matrix?  
*Answer:* 4.

**6.** What is its determinant?  
*Answer:* 3.

**7.** Find the reference eigenvalues.  
*Answer:* 3 and 1.

**8.** Give a unit eigenvector for eigenvalue 3.  
*Answer:* \((1,1)/\sqrt2\).

**9.** Give a unit eigenvector for eigenvalue 1.  
*Answer:* \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\).

**10.** Are the reference eigenvectors orthogonal?  
*Answer:* Yes; their dot product is zero.

**11.** What happens when an eigenvector is multiplied by \(-1\)?  
*Answer:* It remains an eigenvector for the same eigenvalue.

**12.** What is the eigenvalue sum identity?  
*Answer:* The sum equals the trace.

**13.** What is the eigenvalue product identity?  
*Answer:* The product equals the determinant.

**14.** Does a real nonsymmetric matrix always have real eigenvalues?  
*Answer:* No.

**15.** Do real symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues?  
*Answer:* Yes.

**16.** What does a zero eigenvalue indicate?  
*Answer:* Singularity.

**17.** What is the eigenspace of \(2I\)?  
*Answer:* All nonzero directions have eigenvalue 2; the full vector space is its eigenspace.

**18.** What does a negative eigenvalue of a symmetric quadratic-form matrix suggest?  
*Answer:* The quadratic form is negative along the associated direction.

**19.** Can a valid covariance matrix have a strictly negative eigenvalue?  
*Answer:* No, apart from numerical error in a computed estimate.

**20.** What is the next chapter topic?  
*Answer:* Symmetric matrices and spectral decomposition.

The Python lab computes the larger-magnitude quadratic root without subtracting nearly equal trace and gap terms. It recovers the other eigenvalue from the determinant divided by that root, using exact rational arithmetic on the represented matrix entries for this intermediate determinant. This preserves selected small representable eigenvalues that the direct trace-minus-gap formula rounded to zero.
