## Learning objectives

Distinguish positive definite (PD), positive semidefinite (PSD), negative definite, negative semidefinite, and indefinite symmetric matrices; connect their signs to eigenvalues and quadratic forms; use Sylvester's criterion for a symmetric 2×2 matrix; interpret covariance and optimization implications; and distinguish exact mathematics from floating-point tolerance policies.

## 1. Definiteness is about every direction

For a real symmetric matrix \(A\), define \(q(x)=x^\mathsf TAx\).

- **Positive definite (PD):** \(q(x)>0\) for every nonzero \(x\).
- **Positive semidefinite (PSD):** \(q(x)\ge0\) for every \(x\).
- **Negative definite (ND):** \(q(x)<0\) for every nonzero \(x\).
- **Negative semidefinite (NSD):** \(q(x)\le0\) for every \(x\).
- **Indefinite:** there are vectors \(u,v\) with \(q(u)>0\) and \(q(v)<0\).

A zero matrix is both PSD and NSD, but neither PD nor ND. The strict inequalities distinguish definiteness from semidefiniteness.

## 2. Spectral characterization

The spectral theorem gives \(A=Q\Lambda Q^\mathsf T\). For \(y=Q^\mathsf Tx\),

\[
x^\mathsf TAx=\sum_i\lambda_i y_i^2.
\]

Consequently, a symmetric matrix is PD exactly when all eigenvalues are strictly positive, PSD exactly when all are nonnegative, ND exactly when all are strictly negative, NSD exactly when all are nonpositive, and indefinite exactly when it has both positive and negative eigenvalues.

A PSD matrix can have zero eigenvalues. Its zero-eigenvalue directions form a null space and have zero quadratic value, despite potentially nonzero positions.

## 3. Two-by-two criteria

For symmetric \(A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{bmatrix}\), define \(\Delta=ad-b^2\).

- **PD:** \(a>0\) and \(\Delta>0\) (Sylvester's criterion).
- **ND:** \(a<0\) and \(\Delta>0\).
- **PSD:** \(a\ge0\), \(d\ge0\), and \(\Delta\ge0\).
- **NSD:** \(a\le0\), \(d\le0\), and \(\Delta\ge0\).
- **Indefinite:** \(\Delta<0\).

For symmetric \(2\times2\) matrices, \(\Delta=0\) marks at least one zero eigenvalue. A zero determinant alone does not tell us whether the matrix is PSD, NSD, or zero; inspect the remaining signs.

## 4. Five worked examples

| Matrix | Eigenvalues | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| \(\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\) | \(3,1\) | PD |
| \(\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\) | \(2,0\) | PSD, not PD |
| \(\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}\) | \(1,-1\) | Indefinite |
| \(\begin{bmatrix}-2&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}\) | \(-1,-2\) | ND |
| \(\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\) | \(0,-1\) | NSD, not ND |

For the PSD rank-one matrix \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\),

\[
q(x)=(x_1+x_2)^2\ge0.
\]

But \(q(1,-1)=0\), proving it is not PD. For the indefinite diagonal matrix, \(q(1,0)=1\) while \(q(0,1)=-1\).

## 5. Financial application: covariance matrices

For a random return vector \(R\) with finite second moments, covariance \(\Sigma\) satisfies

\[
w^\mathsf T\Sigma w=\operatorname{Var}(w^\mathsf TR)\ge0.
\]

Thus a mathematically valid covariance matrix is PSD. It need not be PD: if one return series is an exact linear combination of others, there may be a nonzero portfolio direction with zero modeled variance.

A negative eigenvalue in an estimated covariance matrix can indicate estimation, data alignment, matrix repair, or numerical problems. It must not be silently treated as acceptable without an explicit tolerance and governance policy. A covariance model does not capture all real financial risks, including liquidity and model error.

## 6. Financial application: optimization curvature

For \(f(x)=\tfrac12x^\mathsf THx+c^\mathsf Tx\) with symmetric \(H\), the Hessian is \(H\). If \(H\) is PSD, \(f\) is convex; if \(H\) is PD, it is strictly convex and has a unique unconstrained minimizer. If \(H\) is indefinite, the quadratic objective is not convex. PSD alone does not guarantee existence or uniqueness of a minimizer: a linear term can make the objective unbounded below along a null-space direction.

## 7. Cholesky connection

A real symmetric PD matrix has a Cholesky factorization \(A=LL^\mathsf T\) with positive diagonal entries of \(L\). A singular PSD matrix generally does not admit the standard strictly positive-pivot Cholesky factorization. This distinction motivates Section 14.5.

## 8. Floating-point policy

Exact signs are mathematical properties. A numerical solver must define how near-zero eigenvalues are interpreted. The companion lab computes eigenvalues of symmetric 2×2 matrices and labels results using an explicitly supplied *absolute* eigenvalue tolerance. If an eigenvalue lies within that tolerance of zero, the lab labels it zero for diagnostic classification; this is a **tolerance-dependent classification**, not a proof of exact PSD.

Scale-aware production diagnostics should consider matrix norm, data uncertainty, and algorithmic error. A large tolerance can conceal a genuinely negative eigenvalue. The lab therefore reports both raw eigenvalues and the tolerance used.

## 10. Twenty worked exercises

**1.** Define PD.  
*Answer:* \(x^\mathsf TAx>0\) for every nonzero \(x\).

**2.** Define PSD.  
*Answer:* \(x^\mathsf TAx\ge0\) for every \(x\).

**3.** Does PD imply PSD?  
*Answer:* Yes.

**4.** Does PSD imply PD?  
*Answer:* No.

**5.** What eigenvalue signs characterize PD?  
*Answer:* All strictly positive.

**6.** What eigenvalue signs characterize PSD?  
*Answer:* All nonnegative.

**7.** What characterizes an indefinite symmetric matrix?  
*Answer:* Both positive and negative eigenvalues.

**8.** Classify \(\operatorname{diag}(2,3)\).  
*Answer:* PD.

**9.** Classify \(\operatorname{diag}(2,0)\).  
*Answer:* PSD, not PD.

**10.** Classify \(\operatorname{diag}(2,-3)\).  
*Answer:* Indefinite.

**11.** Classify \(\operatorname{diag}(-2,-3)\).  
*Answer:* ND.

**12.** Classify \(\operatorname{diag}(-2,0)\).  
*Answer:* NSD, not ND.

**13.** Classify the zero matrix.  
*Answer:* PSD and NSD.

**14.** State the 2×2 PD Sylvester criterion.  
*Answer:* \(a>0\) and \(ad-b^2>0\).

**15.** Compute the determinant of \(\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\).  
*Answer:* 3.

**16.** Why is \(\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\) not PD?  
*Answer:* The nonzero direction \((1,-1)\) has zero quadratic value.

**17.** Why must covariance matrices be PSD?  
*Answer:* Every portfolio return variance is nonnegative.

**18.** Does PSD guarantee a unique unconstrained quadratic optimizer?  
*Answer:* No.

**19.** Which definiteness class guarantees standard Cholesky?  
*Answer:* PD.

**20.** What is the next section?  
*Answer:* 14.5 — Cholesky Factorization.
