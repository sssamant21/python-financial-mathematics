## Project brief

You are reviewing a hypothetical two-instrument portfolio and a three-factor exposure model. Build a transparent mathematical analysis that combines quadratic risk, matrix definiteness, Cholesky factorization, least-squares replication, and rank-one compression. Validate every result independently. The matrices below are educational examples, not estimated market data or approved risk parameters.

## Learning outcomes

1. Calculate portfolio variance and volatility from a positive-definite covariance matrix.
2. Verify eigenvalues and Cholesky factors.
3. Solve an SPD linear system without matrix inversion.
4. Analyze a rectangular exposure matrix using SVD.
5. Compute a least-squares hedge, residual, and rank-one compression error.
6. Document units, tolerances, assumptions, and the distinction between numerical correctness and model validity.

## 1. Input contract

Use a covariance matrix in **annualized return-squared units**,

\[
C=\begin{bmatrix}0.04&0.01\\0.01&0.09\end{bmatrix},
\]

and portfolio weights

\[
w=\begin{bmatrix}0.6\\0.4\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The weights sum to one, but the numerical methods also apply to general exposure vectors. Use a separate illustrative factor-by-instrument exposure matrix

\[
E=\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&2\\0&0\end{bmatrix}
\]

and a target factor exposure \(t=(8,4,5)^\mathsf T\). **Do not mix the covariance and factor-exposure units.** They represent different mathematical objects.

## 2. Quadratic portfolio variance

For a covariance matrix, portfolio variance is

\[
q(w)=w^\mathsf TCw.
\]

Expand the terms:

\[
q(w)=(0.6)^2(0.04)+2(0.6)(0.4)(0.01)+(0.4)^2(0.09)
=0.0144+0.0048+0.0144
=\boxed{0.0336}.
\]

Portfolio volatility is \(\sqrt{0.0336}\approx\boxed{0.183303}\), or about **18.33% annualized**, if the supplied covariance is annualized consistently. The quadratic gradient with respect to the unconstrained weight vector is

\[
\nabla q(w)=2Cw=2(0.028,0.042)^\mathsf T=(0.056,0.084)^\mathsf T.
\]

This gradient is not a recommendation to change weights; constraints, objectives, estimation uncertainty, and transaction costs are outside this numerical exercise.

## 3. Definiteness gate

For \(C\),

\[
a=0.04>0,\qquad \det C=(0.04)(0.09)-(0.01)^2=\boxed{0.0035}>0.
\]

Sylvester's criterion proves \(C\) is SPD. The eigenvalues are

\[
\lambda_\pm=\frac{0.13\pm\sqrt{(0.04-0.09)^2+4(0.01)^2}}{2}
=\frac{0.13\pm\sqrt{0.0029}}{2},
\]

both strictly positive. This makes the standard Cholesky factorization valid.

## 4. Cholesky factorization

Using the lower-triangular convention,

\[
L=\begin{bmatrix}
0.2&0\\
0.05&\sqrt{0.0875}
\end{bmatrix}
\]

because \(0.01/0.2=0.05\) and \(0.09-0.05^2=0.0875\). Verify

\[
LL^\mathsf T=C.
\]

Use forward and backward substitution to solve \(Cx=Cw\). The expected solution is \(x=w\), and the independent solve residual should be near zero.

## 5. Rectangular exposure and SVD

For

\[
E=\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&2\\0&0\end{bmatrix},
\]

the singular values are \(4,2\), the exact rank is \(2\), and the full-column-rank condition number is \(4/2=\boxed{2}\).

The reduced singular directions are the first two standard basis directions in factor space and instrument space. A reduced SVD reconstructs \(E\) exactly.

## 6. Least-squares factor replication

Solve

\[
\min_x\|Ex-t\|_2^2,\qquad t=(8,4,5)^\mathsf T.
\]

The unique least-squares positions are

\[
\boxed{x^*=(2,2)^\mathsf T}.
\]

The achieved factor exposure is \(Ex^*=(8,4,0)^\mathsf T\), and the signed residual is

\[
\boxed{t-Ex^*=(0,0,5)^\mathsf T}.
\]

Thus \(\|t-Ex^*\|_2=5\) and the half-squared-residual objective is \(12.5\). The third factor is not represented by either instrument in this model.

## 7. Rank-one compression

Truncate the exposure matrix to its dominant singular component:

\[
E_1=\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Then

\[
\|E-E_1\|_F=2,\quad
\|E-E_1\|_2=2,\quad
\frac{4^2}{4^2+2^2}=\boxed{0.8}.
\]

Although 80% of squared singular-value energy is retained, the second factor exposure is lost entirely. For \(x^*=(2,2)\),

\[
Ex^*-E_1x^*=(0,4,0)^\mathsf T,
\]

with Euclidean norm \(4\). This demonstrates why retained energy alone cannot establish hedge adequacy.
