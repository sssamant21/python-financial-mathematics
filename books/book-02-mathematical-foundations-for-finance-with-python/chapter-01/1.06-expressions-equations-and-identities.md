## 1.6.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you should be able to:

- Distinguish an expression to evaluate, an equation to solve, and an identity to justify.
- State the domain and unknown before manipulating an equation.
- Recognize one-solution, no-solution, and all-domain cases for a linear equation.
- Preserve conditions when dividing, canceling factors, squaring, or clearing denominators.
- Verify candidates in the original equation and interpret a residual correctly.
- Separate exact algebraic equality from Python assignment, numeric comparison, and approximate equality.

Financial models use formulas in several ways. Confusing their roles can produce a plausible number while leaving the actual question unanswered.

## 1.6.2 An Expression Denotes a Quantity

An expression combines symbols, values, and operations to denote a quantity wherever its operations are defined. For example:

$$P(1+r)^2$$

denotes a two-period compound value once P and r are specified. It contains no equality or condition to solve.

At P=1000 and r=0.05:

$$1000(1.05)^2=1102.5.$$

Here we evaluated the expression. If the question asks for a numerical value, the inputs and conventions must be supplied. If it asks for a simplification, an equivalent expression may be the desired output instead.

Expression equivalence includes the domain. For instance, (x^2-1)/(x-1) agrees with x+1 when x!=1. The fraction is not defined at one, so replacing it with x+1 without retaining that exclusion changes its specification. Section 1.4 introduced this domain distinction; it applies throughout algebraic work.

## 1.6.3 An Equation Imposes a Condition

An equation states that two expressions are equal. Its solution set contains every admissible value of the unknown that makes the equality true.

For the one-period target equation:

$$100(1+r)=110,$$

rate r is unknown. Principal 100, target amount 110, and the one-period timing convention are fixed. Use the positive-growth-factor domain r>-1.

Divide both sides by the nonzero constant 100:

$$1+r=1.1.$$

Subtract one:

$$r=0.1.$$

Substitution into the original equation gives 100(1.1)=110, confirming the candidate. The admissible solution set is {0.1}.

The expression 100(1+r) and the equation 100(1+r)=110 are different objects. The first can be evaluated for many rates. The second selects rates that reach a specified target. Reference 1 discusses conditional equations and their solution sets.

## 1.6.4 An Identity Holds Throughout Its Stated Domain

An identity is an equation true for every admissible value of its variables. For example:

$$(1+r)^2=1+2r+r^2$$

holds for every real r. A derivation follows from the distributive law:

$$(1+r)(1+r)=1+r+r+r^2=1+2r+r^2.$$

This argument establishes the identity. Checking three rates illustrates it but does not establish a statement about every real rate.

The expression 1+2r is not identically equal to (1+r)^2. At r=0.05 the missing term is r^2=0.0025. For a principal of 1000, omitting it understates value by 2.5.

An identity may have a restricted domain. For x!=1:

$$\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=x+1.$$

Calling this an identity does not erase the exclusion. Always state the domain over which an all-values claim is made.

## 1.6.5 One Solution, No Solutions, or All Values

Consider the linear equation:

$$ax+b=cx+d,$$

with fixed real coefficients. Rearranging gives:

$$(a-c)x=d-b.$$

There are three cases:

| Coefficient condition | Consequence | Real solution set |
|---|---|---|
| a-c is nonzero | Divide by a-c | One value, (d-b)/(a-c) |
| a=c and b=d | Both sides are identical | All real values before further domain restrictions |
| a=c and b differs from d | Equal x terms cannot resolve the constant mismatch | Empty set |

Examples are 2x+3=x+7, which gives x=4; 2x+3=2x+3, an identity; and 2x+3=2x+4, which is inconsistent.

These classifications describe the unrestricted real equation. An additional financial domain can remove a unique algebraic solution or narrow an all-values case. An all-real solution set intersected with x>=0 becomes the nonnegative reals; it does not become a unique point.

Zero coefficients require a branch before division. A formula that divides by a-c without checking it cannot represent all three cases correctly.

## 1.6.6 A Financial Degeneracy Is a Mathematical Case

The one-period growth equation can be written:

$$Pr+P=V.$$

When P>0, it gives r=V/P-1. Under r>-1, a strictly positive target V is compatible; a target V=0 would require the excluded boundary r=-1.

When P=0, the equation becomes 0=V. If V=0, every admissible rate satisfies it. If V is nonzero, no rate satisfies the no-contribution model.

These are not merely software exceptions. They express the information content of the question. An account starting with zero cannot reach a nonzero target through proportional growth alone. Returning a default rate would answer a different question without saying so.

This also shows why algebraic solving and financial interpretation are separate steps. The generic equation 100r+100=0 has the real solution r=-1. The selected financial model excludes it. A solver should report a candidate and its domain status, or implement the domain explicitly; a bare algebraic number is incomplete.

## 1.6.7 Equivalent Transformations Need Conditions

Adding or subtracting the same defined expression on both sides preserves equality. Multiplying or dividing by a known nonzero quantity also preserves equivalence. If a quantity may be zero, a division step requires a case split.

For example:

$$x(x-1)=0$$

has solutions x=0 and x=1. Dividing by x silently assumes x!=0 and loses the first solution. Instead, use the zero-product property or consider x=0 separately.

Multiplying by zero has the opposite danger: it can turn distinct expressions into the same zero expression, losing the original condition. A transformed equation may therefore be a consequence of the original without being equivalent to it.

Annotate important transformations with their conditions. “Divide by P, assuming P>0” communicates both the operation and its permitted scope. It also identifies what to do when P=0.

## 1.6.8 Clearing Denominators and Checking Exclusions

Consider:

$$\frac{x}{x-1}=\frac{1}{x-1},\qquad x\neq1.$$

Multiplying by x-1 within the original domain gives x=1. But that candidate is excluded from the original fraction. The original equation therefore has no solution.

This is not a contradiction in algebra. The candidate condition and the domain must both hold; their intersection is empty. The mistake would be reporting x=1 without carrying the exclusion forward.

A practical procedure is:

1. Record denominator exclusions before changing the equation.
2. Perform transformations under their required conditions.
3. List candidates from the transformed equation.
4. Remove candidates outside the original domain.
5. Substitute survivors into the original equation.

For a discounted-value equation involving 1+r in a denominator, record the financial condition r>-1 before multiplying. Clearing denominators does not authorize a rate that the original model excluded.

## 1.6.9 Squaring Can Introduce Candidates

The equation:

$$\sqrt{x+1}=x-1$$

requires x>=1, because the principal square root is nonnegative and must equal x-1. Squaring both sides gives:

$$x+1=(x-1)^2,$$

$$x^2-3x=0,$$

so the squared equation has candidates x=0 and x=3.

Checking the original equation rejects zero: its left side is 1 while its right side is -1. At x=3, both sides equal 2. The original solution set is therefore {3}.

Squaring preserves equality in the forward direction, but equal squares do not establish equal original signs. Candidate verification restores the information that the transformation lost. Reference 2 develops this issue for radical and related equations.

Chapter 02 will cover broader algebraic solving techniques. This introductory example establishes the habit of preserving domains and checking the original relationship.

## 1.6.10 Residuals, Exact Equality, and Approximation

For an equation L(x)=R(x), define the residual:

$$F(x)=L(x)-R(x).$$

An admissible exact solution has residual zero. For 100(1+r)=110:

$$F(r)=100r-10.$$

At r=0.1, F=0. At r=0.0999, F=-0.01. The latter is a one-cent target shortfall if amounts are measured in currency units.

A small residual alone does not prove exact equality, domain validity, uniqueness, or completeness of a root search. Its scale matters. Multiplying an equation by a nonzero constant preserves its solution set but rescales the residual. Report which residual and units were checked.

An identity, by contrast, has residual zero across its stated domain in exact mathematics. A numerical sample can reveal a defect, but successful samples do not prove the all-values claim. Even rational exact arithmetic checks only the cases executed unless paired with an algebraic derivation.

In Python, ordinary assignment = binds a name. Numeric == compares values produced by computation. Neither operation performs a general symbolic proof or solves an equation automatically. Floating-point comparisons may require a justified approximate-equality policy; the exact-rational lab below avoids that representation issue for its supported inputs.

## 1.6.11 Executable Exact-Equation Lab

The following standalone program solves linear equations with rational coefficients. It uses Fraction to preserve exact arithmetic for small instructional inputs. It is not a general symbolic algebra system.

Supported coefficient and candidate inputs are built-in int, Fraction, and strings accepted by Fraction, such as '0.05' or '1/20'. Booleans and floats are deliberately rejected. This avoids silently converting an existing binary approximation into an exact rational intention. Excessive-size inputs still require resource limits in a production application.

Save the complete example as [code/section_1_06.py](code/section_1_06.py).

```python
from fractions import Fraction


def rational(value):
    """Exact rational input policy; numeric strings are parsed explicitly."""
    if type(value) is int or isinstance(value, Fraction):
        return Fraction(value)
    if type(value) is str:
        try:
            return Fraction(value)
        except (ValueError, ZeroDivisionError) as exc:
            raise ValueError("invalid rational text") from exc
    raise TypeError("use int, Fraction, or rational text; bool/float are excluded")


def solve_linear_equation(*, a, b, c, d):
    """Classify a*x+b=c*x+d over the reals, with rational coefficients.

    Return ('unique', rational_solution), ('all', None), or ('none', None).
    Financial domains must be applied separately.
    """
    a, b, c, d = (rational(value) for value in (a, b, c, d))
    coefficient, target = a - c, d - b
    if coefficient == 0:
        return ("all", None) if target == 0 else ("none", None)
    return "unique", target / coefficient


def equation_residual(*, a, b, c, d, x):
    a, b, c, d, x = (rational(value) for value in (a, b, c, d, x))
    return (a*x + b) - (c*x + d)


def original_quotient(x):
    """Evaluate (x*x-1)/(x-1) without removing the original exclusion."""
    x = rational(x)
    if x == 1:
        raise ValueError("original quotient excludes x=1")
    return (x*x - 1) / (x - 1)


def main():
    principal, rate = Fraction(1000), Fraction("0.05")
    value = principal * (1 + rate)**2
    print(f"Evaluated two-period expression: {float(value):.2f}")
    kind, solution = solve_linear_equation(a=100, b=100, c=0, d=110)
    assert kind == "unique" and solution == Fraction(1, 10)
    residual = equation_residual(a=100, b=100, c=0, d=110, x=solution)
    assert residual == 0 and solution > -1
    print(f"One-period rate solution: {solution}")
    print(f"Original-equation residual: {residual}")
    identity = solve_linear_equation(a=3, b=6, c=3, d=6)
    inconsistent = solve_linear_equation(a=3, b=6, c=3, d=7)
    print(f"Identical linear expressions: {identity[0]}")
    print(f"Inconsistent linear equation: {inconsistent[0]}")
    near_residual = equation_residual(a=100, b=100, c=0, d=110, x="0.0999")
    print(f"Residual at rate 0.0999: {near_residual}")
    assert near_residual == Fraction(-1, 100)
    for r in (Fraction(-1, 10), Fraction(0), Fraction(1, 20)):
        assert (1 + r)**2 == 1 + 2*r + r*r
    assert original_quotient(2) == 3


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

Expected output:

```text
Evaluated two-period expression: 1102.50
One-period rate solution: 1/10
Original-equation residual: 0
Identical linear expressions: all
Inconsistent linear equation: none
Residual at rate 0.0999: -1/100
```

The 'all' result means all real values solve the unrestricted coefficient equation, including irrational values even though the implementation represents coefficients and unique solutions rationally. No infinite set is enumerated.

The solver does not apply an investment domain automatically. Its unique output must still be checked against the specific model. It also does not replace an exactly nonzero coefficient by zero because it is small. Introducing a numerical threshold would change the classification policy and require separate justification.

The identity samples in main illustrate the expansion; the distributive-law derivation in Section 1.6.4 establishes it. The original_quotient function preserves its excluded point instead of treating a simplified expression as permission to extend the domain.

## 1.6.13 Practice Problems and Worked Answers

**Problem 1 — Classification.** Classify 200(1+r), 200(1+r)=210, and 200(1+r)=200+200r. State the relevant action for each.

**Answer.** The first is an expression to evaluate or manipulate once its purpose is stated. The second is a conditional equation to solve; its rate is 0.05. The third is an identity over real r, established by distribution, with any additional financial domain retained when it is used in a financial model.

**Problem 2 — Linear cases.** Solve 4x+2=2x+10, 4x+2=4x+2, and 4x+2=4x+3 over the real numbers.

**Answer.** The first gives 2x=8, so x=4. The second holds for all real x. The third reduces to 2=3 and has no solution. Check whether a later application imposes a narrower domain before interpreting these sets financially.

**Problem 3 — Lost solution.** Explain why dividing x(x-2)=0 by x is not a complete solution procedure.

**Answer.** Division assumes x!=0 and produces x=2, losing the valid solution x=0. The original zero-product equation has solution set {0,2}. Consider the zero case separately or factor without division by a possibly zero expression.

**Problem 4 — Extraneous candidate.** Solve sqrt(x+1)=x-1 by squaring, and show why the transformed equation is not an equivalent condition on all real x.

**Answer.** The original equation requires x>=1. Squaring gives x^2-3x=0, hence candidates zero and three. Zero gives 1=-1 in the original equation and is rejected. Three gives 2=2 and is retained. The squared equation permits equal magnitudes with opposite signs, so its solution set is broader.

**Problem 5 — Excluded denominator.** Solve x/(x-1)=1/(x-1). Why must x=1 be rejected even after it appears in a transformed equation?

**Answer.** The original domain excludes one. Multiplying by x-1 yields x=1 within that domain, which is impossible. The solution set is empty. The transformed candidate cannot override the original denominator exclusion.

**Problem 6 — Residual scaling.** At r=0.0999, compute the residuals of 100r+100=110 and the equivalent equation r+1=1.1. Explain their different magnitudes.

**Answer.** The first residual is -0.01 currency units. The second is -0.0001 in decimal-rate units. Dividing the equation by 100 preserves the solution but rescales its residual. A tolerance must identify the equation, reference scale, and units; the raw magnitudes are not directly interchangeable error policies.

## 1.6.14 Common Mistakes and Summary

| Mistake | Consequence | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Treating an expression as a solved equation | An input-dependent quantity is mistaken for a determined answer | Identify the requested action and supplied values |
| Treating one true substitution as an identity proof | A conditional fact becomes an all-values claim | Supply algebraic reasoning and state the domain |
| Dividing by an unknown that may be zero | Valid solutions disappear | Split the zero case before division |
| Clearing denominators without exclusions | Undefined points appear as solutions | Carry original restrictions through every step |
| Squaring and accepting every candidate | Opposite-sign or inadmissible cases survive | Substitute into the original equation |
| Ignoring zero coefficients | All-solution and no-solution cases become division errors | Classify coefficient cases first |
| Taking a small residual as a complete certificate | Domain, uniqueness, and scale remain unchecked | Report the residual's definition and check the model separately |
| Treating Python assignment or == as algebraic solving | Code behavior is confused with mathematics | Implement the intended operation explicitly |

Expressions denote quantities, equations impose conditions, and identities state equality throughout a domain. Reliable solving preserves conditions, classifies degenerate cases, and verifies candidates against the original problem. Exact arithmetic makes selected checks auditable but does not turn a finite sample into a general proof.
