## 1.1.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you should be able to:

- Explain how a financial question becomes a mathematical model and a Python calculation.
- Identify inputs, outputs, assumptions, units, timing, and domain restrictions in a simple model.
- Derive a compound-growth formula rather than rely only on memorization.
- Distinguish a mathematical error, an implementation error, and an inappropriate model assumption.
- Check a result with a known value, a boundary case, and an inverse relationship.
- Interpret a calculated value without claiming more than its assumptions support.

Book 01 introduced tools for computing financial quantities. Book 02 examines why the formulas work, when they apply, and how to recognize misleading answers. The same Python expression can be correct for one question and inappropriate for another.

## 1.1.2 Begin with the Question

Suppose someone asks:

> If I place 1,000 currency units into an account earning 5% per year, what will I have after two years?

The numbers alone do not determine a unique answer. We must clarify whether the rate is effective annually, whether earned interest remains invested, whether fees apply, and whether any additional deposits or withdrawals occur.

For this first example, choose a simple specification:

| Item | Assumption |
|---|---|
| Initial principal | 1,000 units of a single currency |
| Rate | Constant 5% effective per year, represented as 0.05 |
| Horizon | Two complete annual periods |
| Interest treatment | Retained and included in the next year's balance |
| Other cash flows | None |
| Fees and tax | Excluded |
| Payment certainty | Deterministic scenario; no default or market uncertainty modeled |
| Rounding | Keep calculation precision; round only the displayed result |

These choices define the question we will actually answer. If a real account uses a different convention, its model must change.

A useful working sequence is:

**Question → Assumptions → Mathematical relationship → Python implementation → Checks → Interpretation.**

Each stage can fail independently. Reliable computation requires attention to all of them.

## 1.1.3 Identify the Quantities Before Calculating

Let:

- $P$ denote initial principal, in currency units.
- $r$ denote the effective rate per modeled period, as a decimal.
- $n$ denote the number of complete periods.
- $V_n$ denote the value at the end of period $n$.

For the example, $P=1000$, $r=0.05$, and $n=2$.

The rate must correspond to the period count. Here both use years. A monthly period count cannot be combined directly with an annual effective rate as though it were a monthly rate.

The symbols also make the model reusable. A formula written with $P$, $r$, and $n$ explains an entire family of questions. The numerical example is one member of that family.

For now, use the domain:

$$P\geq 0,\qquad r>-1,\qquad n\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}.$$

All numerical inputs must be finite. The growth-factor condition $1+r>0$ keeps the model within a conventional positive-factor interpretation. A finite integer-power expression may exist for some excluded rates, but those cases are outside this financial specification.

## 1.1.4 Derive One-Period Growth

Interest earned during the first period is:

$$I_1=Pr.$$

The ending balance combines starting principal and earned interest:

$$V_1=P+Pr=P(1+r).$$

For the chosen inputs:

$$I_1=1000(0.05)=50,$$

$$V_1=1000+50=1050.$$

The derivation explains the meaning of the growth factor $1+r$. The $1$ preserves the existing principal; the $r$ adds the period's proportional change.

This also provides a units check. Principal has currency units, and the effective rate for one period is dimensionless. Their product is a monetary amount. Adding $P+r$ would combine incompatible quantities even though Python can evaluate the numbers.

## 1.1.5 Derive Multiple-Period Growth

At the beginning of the second period, the balance is $V_1$, not the original principal. Under the same rate and reinvestment assumption:

$$V_2=V_1(1+r).$$

Substitute the first-period expression:

$$V_2=P(1+r)(1+r)=P(1+r)^2.$$

Continuing the same argument for $n$ periods gives:

$$V_n=P(1+r)^n.$$

Equivalently, the model is a recurrence:

$$V_0=P,\qquad V_k=(1+r)V_{k-1}\quad\text{for }k=1,\ldots,n.$$

The recurrence explains the mechanism. The closed form calculates the same value directly. Chapter 05 develops this connection in detail, and Chapter 04 develops the relevant exponential functions. See Reference 1 for an independent treatment of exponential growth and compound interest.

For two years:

$$V_2=1000(1.05)^2=1102.5.$$

The first year's interest is 50. The second year's interest is $1050(0.05)=52.5$. Total interest is therefore 102.5.

Under simple interest, the alternative assumption that each period's interest is calculated only on the original principal gives:

$$V_{simple}=P+nPr=P(1+nr)=1100.$$

The two outputs differ because the models differ. Neither result can be selected correctly without knowing the intended interest treatment.

## 1.1.6 Timing Changes the Model

Now add a fee of 10. Two possible schedules are:

| Fee timing | One-year expression | Ending value |
|---|---|---:|
| Deducted at year end | $1000(1.05)-10$ | 1040.00 |
| Deducted before growth | $(1000-10)(1.05)$ | 1039.50 |

The difference is 0.50, the interest that the upfront fee would otherwise earn.

More generally:

$$[P(1+r)-F]-[(P-F)(1+r)]=Fr.$$

This identity follows by expanding both expressions. It is valid algebraically; interpreting it as an account comparison additionally requires a suitable fee and principal policy. For example, a no-borrowing account would normally require $0\leq F\leq P$ for an upfront fee.

A fee amount without a payment date is incomplete information. Mathematical foundations help us notice the missing timing before implementing an apparently reasonable subtraction.

## 1.1.7 Move Values to a Common Date

Compounding moves an amount forward in time under the assumed growth rule. Discounting reverses that operation under the same convention.

Starting with:

$$V_n=P(1+r)^n,$$

solve for $P$:

$$P=\frac{V_n}{(1+r)^n}.$$

A payment of 110 one year from now has a present equivalent of:

$$P=\frac{110}{1.1}=100$$

under a 10% effective annual discount assumption.

Multiplying 110 by 1.1 instead would move that future amount another period forward, producing 121. That is a valid arithmetic calculation answering a different time question.

The phrase “present equivalent” is conditional on the chosen rate and assumptions. The formula does not prove that a counterparty will pay, that 10% is the appropriate discount rate, or that a particular transaction should be undertaken.

## 1.1.8 Three Different Ways to Be Wrong

| Error category | Example | What detects it? |
|---|---|---|
| Mathematical relationship | Claiming $(1+r)^2=1+2r$ for every rate | Algebraic expansion or a counterexample |
| Implementation | Translating $P/(1+r)^n$ with the wrong parentheses | Code review and a known-value check |
| Model specification | Using annual compounding for a contract with different terms | Assumption, timeline, and convention review |

The missing term in the first example is $r^2$:

$$(1+r)^2=1+2r+r^2.$$

At $r=0.05$, ignoring it understates the growth factor by 0.0025 and the value of a 1000 principal by 2.50.

A more accurate implementation cannot repair a wrong assumption. More decimal places cannot turn a simple-interest contract into a compound-interest contract. Conversely, a well-specified mathematical model can still be implemented incorrectly.

This separation will remain important when the later chapters introduce numerical solvers. A tiny equation residual measures one property of the computation; it does not establish that the underlying question was specified correctly.

## 1.1.9 Implement a Small, Explicit Model

The following program uses only Python's standard library. It intentionally models complete periods. Fractional-time conventions and numerically stable exponential evaluation are developed later.

Save it as [code/section_1_01.py](code/section_1_01.py), or copy the complete block into a local Python file.

```python
from math import isclose, isfinite


def compound_value(principal, period_rate, periods):
    """Fixed effective period rate; whole periods; no intermediate cash flows.

    Built-in int/float money and rate inputs only; bool is rejected.
    This is a teaching kernel, not a contractual rounding implementation.
    """
    for name, value in (("principal", principal), ("period_rate", period_rate)):
        if isinstance(value, bool) or not isinstance(value, (int, float)):
            raise TypeError(f"{name} must be a built-in real numeric input")
        try:
            finite = isfinite(value)
        except OverflowError as exc:
            raise ValueError(f"{name} exceeds the supported range") from exc
        if not finite:
            raise ValueError(f"{name} must be finite")
    if type(periods) is not int:
        raise TypeError("periods must be a built-in integer, excluding bool")
    if principal < 0 or period_rate <= -1 or periods < 0:
        raise ValueError("require principal>=0, period_rate>-1, periods>=0")
    if principal == 0:
        return 0.0
    try:
        factor = (1 + period_rate)**periods
        result = principal * factor
    except OverflowError as exc:
        raise ArithmeticError("result exceeds supported numeric range") from exc
    if not isfinite(result) or result == 0:
        raise ArithmeticError("nonzero value overflowed or underflowed")
    return result


def main():
    principal, rate, periods = 1000.0, 0.05, 2
    compound = compound_value(principal, rate, periods)
    simple = principal * (1 + rate * periods)
    recovered = compound / (1 + rate)**periods
    end_fee = compound_value(1000, rate, 1) - 10
    upfront_fee = compound_value(990, rate, 1)
    assert isclose(compound, 1102.5, rel_tol=1e-12)
    assert isclose(simple, 1100.0, rel_tol=1e-12)
    assert isclose(recovered, principal, rel_tol=1e-12)
    assert isclose(end_fee, 1040.0, rel_tol=1e-12)
    assert isclose(upfront_fee, 1039.5, rel_tol=1e-12)
    print(f"Compound value: {compound:.2f}")
    print(f"Simple-interest value: {simple:.2f}")
    print(f"Recovered principal: {recovered:.2f}")
    print(f"Fee at year end: {end_fee:.2f}")
    print(f"Fee before growth: {upfront_fee:.2f}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

Expected output:

```text
Compound value: 1102.50
Simple-interest value: 1100.00
Recovered principal: 1000.00
Fee at year end: 1040.00
Fee before growth: 1039.50
```

The code separates input admissibility from formula evaluation. It accepts a valid negative rate, such as -0.02, but rejects a rate of -1. It also distinguishes a finite mathematical specification from an output outside its supported numerical range.

The type policy is deliberately narrow: strings, booleans, Decimal, Fraction, and NumPy scalar types are outside this interface. That does not make those representations mathematically invalid; it makes the example's software contract explicit.

## 1.1.10 Validate More Than One Property

Start with independent hand-calculated values:

| Check | Expected relationship | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| One period | $1000(1.05)=1050$ | Known-value benchmark |
| Two periods | $1000(1.05)^2=1102.5$ | Exponent and reinvestment |
| Zero periods | $P(1+r)^0=P$ | Initial condition |
| Zero rate | $P(1+0)^n=P$ | No-growth boundary |
| Negative rate | $1000(0.98)=980$ | Valid decline scenario |
| Inverse operation | $V_n/(1+r)^n=P$ | Growth/discount consistency |
| Invalid boundary | Reject $r=-1$ | Domain enforcement |
| Fee timing | Difference equals $Fr$ | Schedule interpretation |

The companion [reference checks](code/check_section_1_01.py) execute these ideas, compare iterative growth with the closed form, and investigate invalid inputs.

An inverse check alone is insufficient. Two functions using the same incorrect period convention may still reverse each other. Pair inverse relationships with known values and an independent reading of the timeline.

Finite-precision arithmetic introduces another distinction. Algebraically equivalent expressions need not produce bit-for-bit identical floating-point outputs. Use a justified tolerance rather than expecting arbitrary real-valued calculations to compare exactly. References 3 and 4 explain Python's comparison behavior and binary floating-point representation.

For these moderate-sized examples, relative tolerance $10^{-12}$ is far smaller than a cent at the displayed monetary scale. Where a reference value can be zero, the reference checks also use absolute tolerance $10^{-10}$ in currency units. These choices suit this demonstration; a production accounting policy needs its own materiality and rounding specification.

## 1.1.12 Practice Problems

**Problem 1 — Derivation.** Starting with principal 2000 and a fixed 3% effective annual rate, derive and calculate the two-year balance. State the reinvestment assumption.

**Problem 2 — Fee schedule.** For principal 500, rate 4%, and fee 5, compare a fee deducted before one year's growth with the same fee deducted at year end.

**Problem 3 — Valuation date.** Find today's equivalent of 121 due in two years at a 10% effective annual discount rate. Explain what multiplying 121 by $1.1^2$ would calculate instead.

**Problem 4 — Counterexample.** Disprove the assertion that a 10% gain followed by a 10% loss returns an account to its initial value.

**Problem 5 — Limits of a result.** A correctly executed program returns 1102.50 for the opening example. List three assumptions that would need review before comparing this number with a real account statement.

### Worked Answers

1. First year: $2000(1.03)=2060$. Second year: $2060(1.03)=2121.8$. Equivalently, $2000(1.03)^2=2121.8$. Interest remains invested; the same effective annual rate applies in both periods; no other cash flows occur.
2. Upfront fee: $(500-5)(1.04)=514.8$. Year-end fee: $500(1.04)-5=515$. The difference is $5(0.04)=0.2$.
3. $PV=121/(1.1)^2=100$. Multiplying instead gives 146.41, the amount at year four obtained by growing the already-year-two payment for two additional years under the same assumption.
4. Starting at 100, the ending value is $100(1.1)(0.9)=99$. Percentage changes use different intermediate bases, so equal opposite percentage changes do not cancel.
5. Examples include rate convention, payment timing, actual fees, tax treatment, intervening deposits or withdrawals, realized rate changes, and contractual rounding. Any three with an explanation are acceptable. Computation under the chosen model does not establish that the model matches the account.

## 1.1.13 Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Entering 5 for a formula expecting 5% as 0.05 | A 500% period rate is used | Convert the quoted percentage deliberately |
| Treating annual effective rate as monthly effective rate | Growth periods and rate basis differ | Match or convert conventions first |
| Forgetting reinvestment | Simple and compound models are confused | State how interest is treated |
| Omitting the date of a fee | Two different schedules appear identical | Place every amount on the timeline |
| Adding nominal amounts from different dates | Values are combined without date alignment | Choose a common valuation date |
| Using one successful test as proof | Other inputs or assumptions remain unchecked | Combine derivation and independent checks |
| Rounding each intermediate value without a policy | The computed model changes | Keep precision or implement the stated contract rule |
| Interpreting scenario output as a forecast | Conclusions exceed the model | Keep interpretation conditional on assumptions |

## 1.1.14 Summary and Connection to the Next Section

Mathematical foundations make the reasoning behind a financial calculation explicit. We identify quantities, define relationships, derive formulas, preserve units and timing, implement the model, and check its consequences.

The example $V_n=P(1+r)^n$ is useful because its assumptions are visible. Its derivation explains reinvestment; its inverse explains discounting; its boundary cases support validation; and its fee variations show why timing matters.
