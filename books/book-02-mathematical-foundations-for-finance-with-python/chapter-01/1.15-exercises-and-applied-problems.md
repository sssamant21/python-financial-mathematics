## 1.15.1 Purpose and Learning Outcomes

These twenty problems consolidate Chapter 01 rather than introduce another mathematical topic. They ask you to interpret a financial statement, define the relevant objects and domains, write a precise claim or constraint, translate it into code where appropriate, and explain what the resulting evidence establishes.

The problems cover every part of the chapter: number systems, variable roles, sets and intervals, indexing, equations, inequalities, logic, counterexamples, units and conventions, formulation, translation, result checking, and the integrated toolkit.

The [companion solutions](solutions/1.15-solutions-and-reference-implementations.md) provide full worked answers. The [reference calculations](code/section_1_15.py) produce reproducible examples, and the [reference checks](code/check_section_1_15.py) verify their results. Automated checks support the computational portions; they do not grade the completeness of your written reasoning.

## 1.15.2 How to Work Through the Problems

Attempt each problem before reading its solution. State the interpretation, domain, units, and assumptions before calculating. Keep exact values when a rational model is specified, and distinguish an exact result from a decimal display approximation.

Use the same principles throughout: a negative answer may be a valid outcome or a domain violation depending on the specification; zero is a value rather than a substitute for missing data; and an infeasible request is different from a zero-action solution.

No paid data, external service, or additional Python package is required. Unless stated otherwise, all monetary inputs within a problem use one currency and scale. The supplied models omit taxes, settlement delays, rounding, and other features unless the problem names them. Those assumptions limit the answers; they are not claims about actual products.

## 1.15.3 Objects, Roles, Domains, and Indices

### Problem 01 — Split a Payment in Minor Units

Allocate 101 US cents to four recipients. Each allocation must be a nonnegative whole number of cents, the total must be preserved, and allocations may differ by at most one cent. Give the earliest recipients any remainder.

State the domains of the total and recipient count, give the allocation, and explain why the exact rational share 101/4 cents is not itself an allowed allocation. Explain why a Boolean value should not be accepted as a recipient count in this interface.

### Problem 02 — Keep Parameters Fixed Across Scenarios

Use \(A=P(1+r)^n\) with principal 500 and two periods. Compare periodic rates 2% and 4%. Identify which values are held fixed, which parameter varies, and which quantity is the output. Calculate both balances exactly. Does this comparison make the rate a decision chosen by an optimizer?

### Problem 03 — Intersect Financial Domains

A long-only weight must lie in \([0,1]\), and a separate policy requires \(0.25\leq w<0.75\). Write the intersection in interval and set-builder notation. Determine membership for 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, and −0.01. Explain why strictness at the upper endpoint matters.

### Problem 04 — Read an Indexed Schedule

A position has cash flows \(C_0=-200,C_1=110,C_2=121\). Positive means receipt and negative means payment. All times are whole matching periods. At a fixed periodic discount rate of 10%, write the complete indexed sum and evaluate its time-zero value.

Give the sequence passed to the Section 1.12 interface and explain why three entries do not imply three future periods. State what the sign convention tells you and what it does not tell you about investment risk.

## 1.15.4 Equations and Inequalities

### Problem 05 — Classify Before Solving

Over the real numbers, classify and solve where appropriate:

\[
2x+7=x+12,\qquad 3(x+2)=3x+6,\qquad 2x+1=2x+3.
\]

Distinguish a unique solution, an identity, and an inconsistent equation. Substitute any proposed unique solution into the original equation. Would a real solution automatically satisfy a separately imposed financial domain such as \(x\geq10\)?

### Problem 06 — A Fee Budget with an Amount Cap

For transaction amount \(x\geq0\), define fee \(f(x)=3+0.02x\). Require \(f(x)\leq13\) and \(x\leq600\). Find the feasible set and maximum amount. Check proposals 500 and 505, reporting the fee-budget slack for each.

In this problem the affine fee formula applies at zero as well. Do not import the no-action fee exemption from the transfer model. Explain why that distinction belongs in the specification.

### Problem 07 — Reverse the Inequality Correctly

Solve \(-2x+10\leq4\) over the real numbers, then impose \(x\geq0\). Check \(x=2,3,4\). Explain the sign change when dividing and whether the boundary is included. What would change if the original inequality were strict?

## 1.15.5 Logic, Quantifiers, and Counterexamples

### Problem 08 — Positive Revenue and Positive Profit

Let revenue \(v\geq0\), cost \(c\geq0\), and profit \(\pi=v-c\). Assess the universal claim “positive revenue implies positive profit.” Give an admissible counterexample and write the converse and contrapositive of the claim.

Which of those two statements is logically equivalent to the original implication? Does writing its contrapositive make the original false claim true?

### Problem 09 — Negate a Schedule Statement

For payments \((1,0,-1)\), assess “every payment is strictly positive.” Write its negation using an existential quantifier and identify all witness indices under zero-based indexing. Explain why the negation does not mean every payment is nonpositive. How would the corresponding universal statement behave on an empty mathematical domain?

### Problem 10 — Repair a Strict Claim

For positive amount \(x\), fixed fee \(F\geq0\), and proportional fee \(k\geq0\), define \(e(x)=(F+kx)/x\). Disprove the claim that \(e\) strictly decreases for every allowed fixed fee. Use \(F=0,k=0.005\) and amounts 100 and 200.

Then prove the non-strict claim for all \(F\geq0\), and identify an additional condition that gives strict decrease. Your proof should state why division preserves the sign.

## 1.15.6 Units and Financial Conventions

### Problem 11 — Keep Quantities and Rate Changes Distinct

A position holds 80 shares priced at 12.50 USD per share. Calculate position value with units. Separately, a quoted rate rises from 3% to 3.75%. Express that change in percentage points, basis points, and relative percentage terms. Explain why none of those rate changes should be added directly to the position value.

### Problem 12 — A Leap-Day Accrual

Compute simple interest on 9,000 USD at a simple annual rate of 4% from 2024-02-28 to 2024-03-01, including the start and excluding the end. Use ACT/360 and ACT/365F. Keep exact results and explain the numerator and denominator in each case.

Does a result under ACT/365F become ACT/Actual merely because the interval contains February 29? Identify which further convention would be needed before rounding either result to cents.

## 1.15.7 Formulation and Translation

### Problem 13 — Minimum Transfer with Two Constraints

Use the Section 1.11 model: the source pays a fee in addition to destination credit; a positive transfer incurs \(F+kx\); zero transfer incurs no fee. Let source balance be 1,200, destination balance 150, source reserve 400, destination target 650, fixed fee 6, and decimal fee rate 0.02.

Formulate the minimum-transfer problem, solve it, and give fee and final balances. Check the accounting identity for the two-account total. Then change only the target to 950 and determine feasibility. Explain why increasing the transfer cannot repair a source-reserve violation.

### Problem 14 — Parentheses Change the Quantity

For principal 80, periodic rate 25%, and two periods, compare the intended expression \(P(1+r)^n\) with \((P(1+r))^n\). Write and evaluate both Python expressions. Also compare `2 ** 3` with `2 ^ 3`. Explain why successful execution alone does not validate a translation.

### Problem 15 — Preserve Cash-Flow Timing

Calculate the time-zero value of cash flows \((-80,44,48.4)\) at periodic rate 10%, and then at zero rate. Use rational input for 48.4. Separately compare schedules \((0,0,121)\) and \((0,121)\) at 10%.

Explain why deleting an entry with amount zero can change value when sequence position determines time. State whether a list of future-only payments could use the same indexing convention without modification.

## 1.15.8 Checking and Numerical Acceptance

### Problem 16 — A Numerical Tolerance Is Not a Policy Waiver

Compare observed value \(10^{-10}\) with reference zero using relative tolerance \(10^{-6}\). First use absolute tolerance zero, then \(10^{-10}\). Apply the closeness rule from Section 1.13.

A separate transfer calculation leaves the source 0.001 USD below its reserve. May a 0.01 USD numerical comparison tolerance automatically turn that violation into a feasible result? State which policy decision is missing.

### Problem 17 — Audit a Missing Period

With principal 300, periodic rate 10%, and two periods, audit candidate balance 330. Compute the correct balance, signed discrepancy, absolute error, and relative error against the correct reference.

Which of the weak nonnegative and positive-rate lower-bound checks pass? Give a reference calculation with a different operation structure and explain why the round-trip check is useful but related to the power formula.

## 1.15.9 Applied Toolkit Cases

### Problem 18 — Distinguish Three Outcomes

Build a three-record toolkit batch using schema version 1 and distinct identifiers. All records use currency label USD:

- Growth: principal 200, rate 5 with representation `percent`, periods 2, observed balance `"220.5"`.
- Growth: principal 100, rate 10, periods 2, observed balance 121, but omit `representation`.
- Transfer: source 1000, destination 200, reserve 300, target 900, fixed fee 5, decimal fee rate `"0.01"`, transfer 700. Report fee 12, source after 288, and destination after 900.

Predict each status, counts, overall success, and CLI exit code. For the transfer, identify whether the arithmetic fields or a constraint causes failure. Would replacing the three identifiers with one duplicate identifier preserve those per-case outcomes?

### Problem 19 — Repair Data Without Changing the Contract

Starting with Problem 18, add the missing growth representation. For the transfer, change target to 700 and transfer to 500, then update every affected observed value. Keep the original source reserve and fee model.

Predict the repaired counts and exit code. Explain why merely lowering the transfer's target, without changing the transfer and reported balances, would not make its source reserve pass. Preserve the original batch and repair a separate copy.

## 1.15.10 Final Evidence Challenge

### Problem 20 — Design Checks That Can Fail

For the exact growth model, demonstrate scaling with principal 100, rate 3%, horizon 5, and scale factor 4. Include a test that rejects a candidate with one period missing. Add boundary and invalid-input cases to your written test plan.

Run the chapter reference checks with assertions enabled and record the runtime, commands, exit codes, and exact files tested. Explain why a passing finite suite is neither a proof for all possible inputs nor canonical editorial approval. Identify at least one input-convention mistake that two agreeing implementations could share.

The reference code gives reproducible numerical examples for this challenge. Your test-plan explanation and interpretation still require human review.

## 1.15.11 Submission Requirements

For each problem, submit the interpretation, assumptions, derivation or counterexample, result with units where applicable, and the relevant computational evidence. Do not replace a proof with a list of successful examples or a domain explanation with a caught exception.

For Problems 18–19, retain both JSON batches and their reports. A nonzero command exit may be the expected result of a deliberately failing case; distinguish that from a reference-check script that unexpectedly fails.

For Problem 20, preserve the failing-candidate evidence as well as the passing run. A credible checking explanation should say what a test could miss, not only what passed. The companion solutions show one acceptable approach, not every possible valid derivation or test design.
