## 1.8.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you should be able to distinguish a proposition from a predicate, combine conditions with logical operators, and interpret an implication without assuming its converse. You should also be able to identify necessary and sufficient conditions, state a model's assumptions explicitly, negate quantified statements, and translate a finite checklist into Python without silently accepting missing evidence.

The central skill is reading a financial statement as a conditional claim. A formula does not carry its assumptions along automatically. A computer can evaluate an expression successfully even when the interpretation attached to that expression is unjustified.

Our examples use hypothetical quantities and deliberately small models. They illustrate reasoning about stated conditions, rather than recommendations about actual investments.

## 1.8.2 Propositions, Predicates, and Domains

A **proposition** is a declarative statement with a truth value in the setting under discussion. “The account balance is nonnegative” becomes a proposition once the account, observation time, and balance are specified. “The balance should be higher” is not a precise mathematical proposition until “should” and the comparison are defined.

A **predicate** contains variables. For example,

\[
P(x): x\geq 0
\]

is a predicate on a chosen domain. Substituting the real number 120 gives a true proposition; substituting −20 gives a false one. Asking whether a missing database value satisfies the intended real-number predicate requires an input policy first. Missing data is not a newly discovered negative number.

Domains matter even before truth is evaluated. The expression \(1/x>0\) is defined for nonzero real \(x\). At \(x=0\), division is undefined; the expression is not simply a false inequality. Either restrict the domain or formulate a guarded statement that checks the domain before evaluating the expression.

In practice, record three things together: the predicate, its mathematical domain, and the meaning of the observed input. A number labeled “rate” is incomplete without a period and a convention. Logic organizes well-defined statements; it cannot supply omitted definitions.

## 1.8.3 Combining Conditions

For propositions \(P\) and \(Q\), conjunction \(P\land Q\) means both hold. Disjunction \(P\lor Q\) means at least one holds, including the case where both hold. Negation \(\neg P\) reverses a truth value.

| P | Q | P and Q | P or Q |
|---|---|---|---|
| True | True | True | True |
| True | False | False | True |
| False | True | False | True |
| False | False | False | False |

Suppose a proposed transaction must be nonnegative and stay within an available cash amount \(C\). Its amount \(x\) must satisfy

\[
(x\geq0)\land(x\leq C).
\]

Checking only the upper bound can admit negative amounts. Checking only nonnegativity can admit amounts above the budget. The conjunction expresses the complete pair of stated restrictions.

“In cash or in a bond” can mean different things in ordinary language. Mathematical \(\lor\) is inclusive. If a specification requires exactly one of two alternatives, write that explicitly as

\[
(P\lor Q)\land\neg(P\land Q).
\]

Parentheses help when a rule combines several operators. Write the grouping that represents the policy before translating it into code; do not use operator precedence as a substitute for deciding what the policy means.

## 1.8.4 What an Implication Says

The implication

\[
P\Rightarrow Q
\]

states that whenever \(P\) holds, \(Q\) holds. Here \(P\) is the antecedent and \(Q\) is the consequent. In classical two-valued logic,

\[
P\Rightarrow Q\quad\text{is equivalent to}\quad\neg P\lor Q.
\]

| P | Q | P implies Q |
|---|---|---|
| True | True | True |
| True | False | False |
| False | True | True |
| False | False | True |

Only a true antecedent paired with a false consequent contradicts the implication. When the antecedent is false, the implication is true in this formal sense, sometimes called vacuous truth. This does **not** establish the antecedent or certify an input.

For example, a rule might say: “If all required checks pass, accept the record.” An incomplete record can fail the antecedent while the implication remains true. If your program asks only whether the implication is true, it has not asked whether the record passed its checks.

To accept a record under stated requirements, evaluate their conjunction directly. To evaluate whether a conditional relationship is violated, evaluate the implication. These are different questions and deserve different function names.

## 1.8.5 A Financial Implication with Explicit Assumptions

Consider one-period simple interest

\[
I=Pr,
\]

where \(P\) is principal and \(r\) is the rate for that period. For real \(P\) and \(r\),

\[
(P>0\land r>0)\Rightarrow I>0.
\]

For example, \(P=1{,}000\) and \(r=0.04\) give \(I=40>0\). Multiplication of two positive real numbers establishes the conclusion; the numerical example illustrates the claim but does not prove it for every principal and rate.

The assumptions identify the scope of the assertion. If \(P=0\), then \(I=0\), even when \(r>0\). Replacing \(P>0\) with \(P\geq0\) while keeping the strict conclusion would make the claim false.

The relationship also assumes that the specified formula describes the quantity called interest. Fees, taxes, default, and changes in purchasing power are outside this model. A positive modeled interest amount does not establish a positive net or real return.

Notice the difference between a mathematical deduction and an empirical premise. Algebra establishes the sign of \(Pr\) after the inputs and formula have been fixed. It does not establish that a quoted rate will be realized. The implication is a statement about the model, not a forecast.

## 1.8.6 Necessary, Sufficient, and Equivalent Conditions

If \(P\Rightarrow Q\), then \(P\) is a **sufficient** condition for \(Q\), and \(Q\) is a **necessary** condition for \(P\). The terminology describes the direction of the implication, not how important a condition sounds.

In the previous example, positive principal together with positive rate is sufficient for positive simple interest. Positive interest is necessary for that pair of strict positivity conditions to hold under the formula. The pair is not necessary for positive interest over all real inputs: \(P=-100\) and \(r=-0.10\) also yield \(I=10>0\). Whether negative principal has an appropriate financial interpretation is a separate domain decision.

If the domain is restricted to \(P>0\), then

\[
I>0\iff r>0.
\]

The symbol \(\iff\) means “if and only if”: both directions hold. Dividing by the strictly positive principal preserves the inequality, connecting this result to Section 1.7.

Equivalence must be read with its background assumptions. At \(P=0\), a positive rate does not produce positive interest. Writing \(I>0\iff r>0\) without stating \(P>0\) loses a condition essential to the result.

A useful editing habit is to underline each “only if.” “Accept only if checks pass” means acceptance implies passing; it does not by itself say every passing record must be accepted.

## 1.8.7 Converse, Inverse, and Contrapositive

Starting from \(P\Rightarrow Q\), three related statements are easy to confuse:

| Statement | Form | Equivalent to original? |
|---|---|---|
| Converse | \(Q\Rightarrow P\) | Not in general |
| Inverse | \(\neg P\Rightarrow\neg Q\) | Not in general |
| Contrapositive | \(\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P\) | Yes, in classical logic |

Take \(P\) to mean “both principal and rate are positive” and \(Q\) to mean “simple interest is positive,” over real inputs with \(I=Pr\). The negative-principal, negative-rate example disproves the converse. It also disproves the inverse: \(P\) is false but \(Q\) is true.

The contrapositive says that if interest is not positive, principal and rate cannot both be positive. It does not say both must be nonpositive. Negating a conjunction produces an alternative: principal is not positive **or** rate is not positive.

Contraposition is useful for identifying inconsistent claims. If a report asserts positive principal, positive rate, and nonpositive simple interest under this formula, at least one assertion or the formula's applicability needs correction. Logic identifies the conflict; it does not identify which data field is wrong.

## 1.8.8 Negation and Quantifiers

De Morgan's laws state

\[
\neg(P\land Q)\iff(\neg P\lor\neg Q),
\qquad
\neg(P\lor Q)\iff(\neg P\land\neg Q).
\]

Thus “not both limits passed” means at least one limit failed, not necessarily both. An error message that changes “at least one” into “all” misrepresents the evidence.

Quantifiers extend statements across a domain. For payments \(c_1,\ldots,c_n\),

\[
\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},\ c_i>0
\]

means every listed payment is positive. Its negation is

\[
\exists i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}: c_i\leq0.
\]

A single zero payment disproves strict positivity. It does not establish that every payment is nonpositive. The complement \(c_i\leq0\) is appropriate here because each \(c_i\) is a real number.

Negating “there exists a negative payment” yields “every payment is nonnegative.” Keep the domain fixed while taking the negation. Changing the list or silently dropping missing observations changes the claim being assessed.

A finite scan can establish a universal statement about the entire explicitly supplied finite list. It cannot establish a statement about all possible future payment schedules. A collection of examples is not a proof over an unrestricted domain.

## 1.8.9 Empty Collections and Missing Evidence

A universal statement over an empty domain is true in classical logic. There is no element that contradicts it. Correspondingly, Python's `all([])` is `True`; `any([])` is `False`.

That behavior is mathematically consistent but can surprise a financial data pipeline. Suppose a record is supposed to carry three checks. If a loading error produces an empty list, `all(checks)` says nothing about whether those three required checks were obtained.

Separate these questions:

1. Are the required checks present exactly once under the expected names?
2. Are their results actual Boolean values?
3. Are all those results true?

An empty checklist can be appropriate for a deliberately empty mathematical domain. It is inappropriate when the application requires a nonempty checklist. The lab explicitly requires a nonempty schema and rejects missing or unexpected keys.

Even this schema is an assumption: a program cannot prove that its author listed every economically relevant condition. A schema provides completeness relative to a declared specification. Review of that specification remains a separate responsibility.

## 1.8.10 Python Truth Values and Guarded Evaluation

Python accepts many objects in a Boolean context. Nonempty strings are truthy, so the string `"False"` does not behave like the Boolean `False`. Integers also participate in truth testing, and `bool` is a subclass of `int`. For a function intended to receive logical propositions, this section deliberately requires `type(value) is bool`.

Python's `and` and `or` short-circuit and return selected operands, which need not be Boolean values. Use them directly as logical operators when the operands are explicitly Boolean predicates. Do not assume an arbitrary expression involving them returns a Boolean.

Short-circuiting also permits domain guards: a type and finiteness check can run before an ordering comparison. The lab uses this to avoid comparing `None` or strings with numbers. It accepts only built-in integers and floats whose finiteness can be checked using `math.isfinite`; extremely large integers that overflow that interface are rejected. This is a documented software boundary, not a limitation of mathematical integers.

A floating-point NaN needs special attention. For NaN, both `x >= 0` and `x < 0` are false. Therefore replacing `not (x >= 0)` by `x < 0` is justified over real numbers, but not over arbitrary Python floats. Restricting to finite supported inputs preserves the intended domain of the comparison.

## 1.8.11 Worked Python Lab — Assumptions Before Acceptance

For a later discrete-growth calculation, declare these input requirements: principal is nonnegative, period rate exceeds −1, and the number of periods is a nonnegative integer. These are illustrative requirements for that model. They are not universal restrictions on every financial formula. The lab checks the inputs; it does not calculate growth or establish a future return.

Save the following as [code/section_1_08.py](code/section_1_08.py):

```python
"""Classical implication and explicit finite input-check schemas."""
import math


def implies(p, q):
    if type(p) is not bool or type(q) is not bool:
        raise TypeError("implication requires two Boolean values")
    return (not p) or q


def supported_finite(value):
    if type(value) not in (int, float):
        return False
    try:
        return math.isfinite(value)
    except OverflowError:
        return False


def review_checks(checks, *, required):
    if type(required) not in (tuple, list) or not required:
        raise ValueError("declare a nonempty list or tuple of required names")
    if any(type(name) is not str or not name for name in required):
        raise ValueError("required names must be nonempty strings")
    if len(set(required)) != len(required):
        raise ValueError("required names must be unique")
    if type(checks) is not dict:
        raise TypeError("checks must be a dictionary")
    if set(checks) != set(required):
        raise ValueError("check names must exactly match the schema")
    if any(type(checks[name]) is not bool for name in required):
        raise TypeError("check results must be Boolean values")
    return {
        "passed": all(checks[name] for name in required),
        "failed": tuple(name for name in required if not checks[name]),
    }


def growth_inputs_report(*, principal, period_rate, periods):
    conditions = {
        "principal_domain": supported_finite(principal) and principal >= 0,
        "rate_domain": supported_finite(period_rate) and period_rate > -1,
        "period_domain": type(periods) is int and periods >= 0,
    }
    report = review_checks(
        conditions,
        required=("principal_domain", "rate_domain", "period_domain"),
    )
    return {"conditions": conditions, **report}


def main():
    for p, q in [(True, True), (True, False), (False, True), (False, False)]:
        print(f"{p} -> {q}: {implies(p, q)}")
    valid = growth_inputs_report(principal=1000, period_rate=0.04, periods=3)
    invalid = growth_inputs_report(principal=1000, period_rate=-1, periods=3)
    print(f"Valid inputs: {valid['passed']}")
    print(f"Invalid inputs accepted: {invalid['passed']}")
    print(f"Failed conditions: {invalid['failed']}")
    print(f"all([]): {all([])}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

Expected output:

```text
True -> True: True
True -> False: False
False -> True: True
False -> False: True
Valid inputs: True
Invalid inputs accepted: False
Failed conditions: ('rate_domain',)
all([]): True
```

The failed rate requirement is reported by name. It is not replaced with a repaired rate or a default. If `principal=None`, the principal-domain predicate returns false because the observation is unsupported; this does not claim that the unknown mathematical principal is negative.

## 1.8.12 Practice Problems

1. Let \(P\) mean “amount is nonnegative” and \(Q\) mean “amount does not exceed cash.” Write the acceptance rule and its negation.
2. Under \(I=Pr\) over real inputs, disprove \(I>0\Rightarrow(P>0\land r>0)\). What added domain restriction makes \(I>0\iff r>0\) valid?
3. A record is accepted only if its required checks pass. Is passing sufficient for acceptance under that statement alone? Write its implication.
4. Negate “every payment in the supplied schedule is strictly positive.” Does one zero payment establish that all payments are nonpositive?
5. Explain why `all([])` cannot establish that three required observations arrived. What should a validator check first?
6. The lab receives principal 500, period rate −1, and periods `True`. Which conditions fail? Explain why the positive principal does not rescue the record.

## 1.8.13 Worked Answers

1. Acceptance is \(P\land Q\). Its negation is \(\neg P\lor\neg Q\): at least one restriction fails. For finite real cash and amount this is \(x<0\lor x>C\).
2. Choose \(P=-100\) and \(r=-0.10\), giving \(I=10\). Both positivity conditions fail. Restricting principal to \(P>0\) permits division by a positive number and proves the stated equivalence.
3. Write \(A\Rightarrow C\), with \(A\) acceptance and \(C\) passing checks. Passing is necessary under the sentence; it is not sufficient unless an additional rule establishes \(C\Rightarrow A\). Other requirements could still prevent acceptance.
4. There exists at least one payment that is nonpositive. A zero payment is enough to disprove strict positivity of every payment. It gives no conclusion about the signs of the remaining payments.
5. `all` evaluates the supplied iterable and finds no false element in an empty one. First verify that the declared nonempty set of required check names matches the observed names, then validate Boolean result types, then evaluate their conjunction. This establishes completeness relative to the schema.
6. The rate-domain condition fails because −1 is excluded. The period-domain condition also fails: this interface requires a built-in integer and explicitly excludes Boolean values. Acceptance requires all three conditions, so a passing principal condition cannot compensate for either failure.

## 1.8.14 Common Mistakes, Summary, and Sources

Common mistakes include asserting a converse without proof, omitting a strict positivity assumption when dividing an inequality, treating “not all” as “none,” and accepting an empty checklist as evidence of data completeness. Another is using the truth of an implication to conclude its premise holds. A conditional can be true while its antecedent is false.

Before trusting a financial conclusion, identify the domain, state the assumptions, verify the direction of the implication, and distinguish observed evidence from assumptions about a model. In code, make required inputs explicit and keep input acceptance separate from the conditional claims you study.

This section uses classical two-valued logic for well-defined propositions. Missing observations require a separate data policy. Passing the lab's declared checks establishes only those checks; it does not establish a model's economic suitability, computational accuracy for a later calculation, or predictive validity.
