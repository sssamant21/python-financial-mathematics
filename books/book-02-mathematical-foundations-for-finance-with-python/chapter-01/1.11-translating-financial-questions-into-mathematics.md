## 1.11.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you should be able to turn a financial question into a mathematical specification: identify the requested output, distinguish inputs from decisions, define units and timing, express relationships and constraints, and state the assumptions that make the specification meaningful. You should also be able to distinguish calculation, feasibility, and optimization, and translate a mathematical result back into the original question.

Earlier sections supplied the language: variables, sets, equations, inequalities, logic, definitions, and conventions. Here we combine them before choosing a computational method. A correct calculation answers the question encoded in its formulas; our first responsibility is to encode the intended question.

Our main example uses a hypothetical transfer between two accounts with a transaction fee. All amounts use one currency at one modeled instant. The example is an exercise in formulation, not a claim about any bank's actual transfer rules.

## 1.11.2 Begin with the Requested Output

Consider the request:

> How much should we transfer to bring the destination account to its target while keeping enough money in the source account?

Several questions are hidden inside that sentence. Does “how much” mean the amount credited to the destination or the total debited from the source? Does “enough” mean a specified reserve? Should the transfer be the smallest that works or the largest affordable amount? What happens if the target cannot be reached?

A useful first draft of the output is: the smallest nonnegative amount credited to the destination that meets its target without reducing the source below its reserve, or an explicit infeasible result if no such amount exists.

This sentence establishes an objective and a failure outcome. It still needs definitions for the fee and timing. Writing those details is part of the modeling work, not an optional comment to add after the calculation.

Avoid solving for a familiar symbol merely because a formula is available. The same account data can support a proposed-transfer check, a maximum-capacity calculation, or a minimum-transfer decision. Each requires a different output specification.

## 1.11.3 Separate Data, Parameters, and Decisions

Let the decision variable be \(x\), the amount credited to the destination. The remaining quantities are inputs fixed for the current question:

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit and domain |
|---|---|---|
| \(S\) | Initial source balance | Currency, \(S\geq0\) |
| \(D\) | Initial destination balance | Same currency, \(D\geq0\) |
| \(R\) | Required source reserve | Same currency, \(R\geq0\) |
| \(T\) | Destination target | Same currency, \(T\geq0\) |
| \(F\) | Fixed fee for a positive transfer | Same currency, \(F\geq0\) |
| \(k\) | Proportional fee per unit transferred | Decimal fraction, \(k\geq0\) |
| \(x\) | Amount credited to destination | Same currency, \(x\geq0\) |

A parameter is fixed within this problem even if another scenario uses a different value. Comparing scenarios does not make the parameter an independently chosen decision in the current model.

The input domains do not require \(S\geq R\) or \(D\geq T\). A source balance below its required reserve is valid input describing a potentially infeasible situation. Rejecting such input as a formatting error would hide an important modeled outcome.

## 1.11.4 State the Fee and Timing Assumptions

Assume the source pays the fee in addition to the amount credited to the destination. A positive transfer incurs a fixed fee plus a proportional fee, while doing nothing incurs no fee:

\[
g(x)=
\begin{cases}
0,&x=0,\\
F+kx,&x>0.
\end{cases}
\]

After the transfer,

\[
S'=S-x-g(x),\qquad D'=D+x.
\]

These are accounting relationships for the chosen model. We assume immediate settlement, no interest during the transfer, no other cash flows, no currency conversion, and no additional transfer limit. Amounts are continuously divisible in the mathematics; the lab evaluates rational inputs exactly without imposing cent increments or fee rounding.

Timing can change the problem. A fee debited later could require a separate reserve check at that later time. A transfer arriving after the target's deadline would not satisfy the intended operational requirement even if the eventual balance were high enough.

The no-transfer case also matters. Applying \(F+kx\) at \(x=0\) would charge a fixed fee for taking no action, which is not our stated assumption. Piecewise definitions often express business distinctions that a single convenient formula would erase.

## 1.11.5 Translate Verbal Restrictions into Constraints

“Bring the destination to at least its target” becomes

\[
D+x\geq T.
\]

“Keep the source at or above its reserve” becomes

\[
S-x-g(x)\geq R.
\]

Together with \(x\geq0\), these define the feasible set

\[
\mathcal F=\{x\geq0:D+x\geq T,\ S-x-g(x)\geq R\}.
\]

The conjunction is essential: meeting the destination target does not compensate for violating the source reserve. Words such as “at least” and “no more than” determine whether equality is allowed. Here the reserve and target boundaries are included.

Check the constraints against simple stories. If the destination already meets the target and the source already meets its reserve, doing nothing should work. If the source is below reserve, an outgoing transfer cannot repair it. These observations provide useful checks before any algebra.

## 1.11.6 Calculation, Feasibility, and Optimization

Three related tasks can now be stated precisely:

| Task | Input and requested result |
|---|---|
| Calculation | Given \(x\), compute fee and final balances |
| Feasibility | Given \(x\), determine whether every constraint holds |
| Optimization | Among feasible \(x\), choose the smallest |

Our decision problem is

\[
\min_{x\in\mathcal F}x.
\]

If \(\mathcal F\) is empty, there is no feasible minimum. Returning zero without checking feasibility would confuse “no transfer needed” with “no transfer can solve the problem.”

The objective also needs justification. We choose minimum destination credit because that is the clarified request. Minimizing total source debit gives the same choice in this particular model because the debit is increasing on positive transfers and zero at no transfer. Other fee schedules or objectives can change that relationship.

A solver cannot decide which objective the user intended. It can only act on the one supplied. The formulation should therefore be understandable independently of the eventual implementation.

## 1.11.7 Derive the Smallest Required Transfer

The destination constraint and nonnegativity imply

\[
x\geq L:=\max(0,T-D).
\]

If \(L=0\), the destination needs no additional funds. Then \(x=0\) is feasible exactly when \(S\geq R\). Because all allowed decisions are nonnegative, feasible zero is automatically the minimum.

If \(L>0\), every feasible transfer is positive, so the positive-transfer fee branch applies. Rearranging the source constraint gives

\[
S-x-F-kx\geq R
\iff (1+k)x\leq S-R-F
\iff x\leq U:=\frac{S-R-F}{1+k}.
\]

Division preserves the inequality because \(1+k>0\). The positive-gap problem is feasible precisely when \(L\leq U\). If feasible, \(x^*=L\): any smaller value misses the target, while \(L\) itself meets both constraints. If \(L>U\), increasing the transfer only worsens the reserve constraint, so no feasible alternative exists.

This is a complete argument for this small model. No numerical optimizer is required. A negative \(U\) is evidence that no positive transfer is affordable; it is not permission to choose a negative transfer outside the domain.

## 1.11.8 Worked Example and an Infeasible Variant

Use

\[
S=1{,}000,\ D=200,\ R=300,\ T=700,\ F=5,\ k=0.01.
\]

The target gap is \(L=500\), and the upper bound is

\[
U=\frac{1{,}000-300-5}{1.01}
=\frac{69{,}500}{101}\approx688.1188.
\]

Since \(500\leq U\), the smallest transfer is 500. The fee is \(5+0.01(500)=10\). Final balances are source 490 and destination 700. The source reserve has slack \(490-300=190\); the destination target is met exactly.

Now change only the target to 900. The gap becomes 700, above the same upper bound. A transfer of 700 would incur fee 12 and leave source balance 288, which is 12 below reserve. Reporting that proposal as feasible because the destination reaches 900 would ignore half the question.

The correct result is infeasible under the stated assumptions. The model does not automatically authorize lowering the reserve, changing the target, borrowing, or waiving fees. Those would be new scenarios with changed inputs or assumptions.

## 1.11.9 Check the Formulation Through Accounting

The final balances satisfy

\[
S'+D'=S+D-g(x).
\]

A transfer moves money between the two modeled accounts; the fee leaves that two-account total. In the worked example, initial combined balances are 1,200 and final combined balances are 1,190, matching the fee of 10.

This identity is an independent interpretation check. If an implementation subtracts the transfer from both accounts or credits the fee to the destination, the identity can reveal the mistake. It is not sufficient alone: a program could preserve the combined total while allocating balances incorrectly. Verify the separate account equations too.

A useful formulation review has three levels. Check the algebra against the definitions, check units and signs, and check the result against the original story. Tests of the implementation should reflect those distinct questions rather than only repeat the same formula in another function.

## 1.11.10 Ambiguity, Missing Data, and Changed Questions

Suppose instead that the source is debited a gross transfer \(y\), and the fee is deducted before the destination receives funds. If the fee is \(F+ky\), destination credit becomes \(y-F-ky\). That is a different model from our extra source-paid fee. Reusing \(D+x\geq T\) while treating \(x\) as the gross debit would misstate the target condition.

Other changes also require reformulation: a maximum transfer size, whole-cent amounts, a fee charged even on cancellation, multiple funding accounts, uncertain future balances, or deadlines. Record each additional restriction before trying to solve the expanded problem.

Missing data should not silently become a zero balance or zero fee. Zero is a meaningful modeled value. The lab rejects unsupported inputs; it cannot infer the missing contractual information. Similarly, a successful arithmetic result cannot establish that an externally supplied balance is available cash rather than a ledger total containing unsettled items.

The purpose of the specification is to expose these decisions early. When information is unavailable, state the assumption that permits the example and keep it visible in the interpretation of the result.

## 1.11.11 Worked Python Lab — From Specification to Decision

Save as [code/section_1_11.py](code/section_1_11.py). The names follow the variable table, with full words in the interface. All amounts must already share one currency and scale. The interface accepts integers, rational strings, and `Fraction` values, rejecting floats and Boolean values. A `None` transfer marks infeasibility; zero marks a feasible decision to do nothing.

```python
"""One-currency transfer formulation with extra source-paid fees."""
from fractions import Fraction


def rational(value):
    if type(value) not in (int, str, Fraction):
        raise TypeError("use an integer, rational string, or Fraction")
    return Fraction(value)


def scenario(*, source, destination, reserve, target, fixed_fee, fee_rate):
    values = dict(source=source, destination=destination, reserve=reserve,
                  target=target, fixed_fee=fixed_fee, fee_rate=fee_rate)
    values = {name: rational(value) for name, value in values.items()}
    if any(value < 0 for value in values.values()):
        raise ValueError("scenario inputs must be nonnegative")
    return values


def evaluate_transfer(*, transfer, **inputs):
    p = scenario(**inputs)
    x = rational(transfer)
    if x < 0:
        raise ValueError("transfer must be nonnegative")
    fee = p["fixed_fee"] + p["fee_rate"] * x if x > 0 else Fraction(0)
    source_after = p["source"] - x - fee
    destination_after = p["destination"] + x
    checks = {"source_reserve": source_after >= p["reserve"],
              "destination_target": destination_after >= p["target"]}
    return {"transfer": x, "fee": fee, "source_after": source_after,
            "destination_after": destination_after, "checks": checks,
            "feasible": all(checks.values())}


def minimum_transfer(**inputs):
    p = scenario(**inputs)
    needed = max(Fraction(0), p["target"] - p["destination"])
    candidate = evaluate_transfer(transfer=needed, **p)
    return {"feasible": candidate["feasible"], "needed": needed,
            "transfer": needed if candidate["feasible"] else None,
            "candidate_report": candidate}


def main():
    inputs = dict(source=1000, destination=200, reserve=300,
                  target=700, fixed_fee=5, fee_rate="0.01")
    result = minimum_transfer(**inputs)
    report = result["candidate_report"]
    print(f"Minimum transfer: {result['transfer']}")
    print(f"Fee: {report['fee']}")
    print(f"Final balances: source={report['source_after']}, "
          f"destination={report['destination_after']}")
    impossible = minimum_transfer(**(inputs | {"target": 900}))
    print(f"Higher target feasible: {impossible['feasible']}")
    print(f"Higher target transfer: {impossible['transfer']}")
    no_action = minimum_transfer(**(inputs | {"target": 200}))
    print(f"Already at target: transfer={no_action['transfer']}, "
          f"fee={no_action['candidate_report']['fee']}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

Expected output:

```text
Minimum transfer: 500
Fee: 10
Final balances: source=490, destination=700
Higher target feasible: False
Higher target transfer: None
Already at target: transfer=0, fee=0
```

The implementation evaluates the minimum required candidate directly. The derivation establishes why a failed candidate cannot be rescued by a larger transfer under nonnegative fees. The `candidate_report` remains available for an infeasible outcome, but its transfer is a diagnostic proposal, not an approved solution.

## 1.11.12 Practice Problems

1. In the transfer model, identify the decision variable, the two final-balance equations, and the objective. Explain why the source debit is not simply the destination credit.
2. With \(S=800,D=100,R=200,T=500,F=4,k=0.02\), calculate the minimum transfer, fee, and final balances. Verify both constraints.
3. Keep the inputs from Problem 2 but set \(T=700\). Determine feasibility using the upper bound for a positive transfer.
4. Let \(D=T\), \(S=R\), and \(F>0\). Is zero transfer feasible? Would it remain feasible if the fixed fee were charged at zero? Identify the changed assumption.
5. If \(S<R\) while \(D\geq T\), can any nonnegative outgoing transfer satisfy both conditions in this model? Explain without searching amounts.
6. A requirement says “transfer no more than 250.” Add its mathematical constraint. How does it change the feasibility test when the target gap is positive?

## 1.11.13 Worked Answers

1. The decision is destination credit \(x\). Final balances are \(S'=S-x-g(x)\) and \(D'=D+x\). Minimize \(x\) subject to nonnegativity and both balance constraints. Source debit is \(x+g(x)\), so it includes the extra source-paid fee.
2. The gap is 400. The fee is \(4+0.02(400)=12\). Source finishes at 388, above reserve 200; destination finishes at 500, exactly at target. Any smaller transfer misses the target, so 400 is minimal.
3. The gap is 600. The positive-transfer upper bound is \((800-200-4)/1.02=29{,}800/51\), approximately 584.3137. Since 600 is larger, the problem is infeasible. The gap proposal would leave source balance \(800-600-16=184\), violating reserve by 16.
4. Under the stated no-action fee rule, zero leaves both balances unchanged and is feasible. Charging \(F\) at zero would leave source below reserve. That changes the fee function at zero; it is not merely a different calculation of the same model.
5. No. Both the transfer and fee are nonnegative, so \(S'\leq S<R\). The reserve constraint is impossible even though the destination already meets its target.
6. Add \(x\leq250\). For positive gap \(L\), feasibility becomes \(L\leq\min(U,250)\). If feasible, the minimum remains \(L\). A cap below the gap makes the target unattainable under this one-transfer model.

## 1.11.14 Common Mistakes, Summary, and Sources

Common formulation errors include solving for the wrong amount, omitting the fee from the source debit, charging a fixed fee at zero despite a no-action exemption, checking only the target, and returning zero for an infeasible request. Another is treating the balance equations as forecasts when they only describe a hypothetical immediate transfer.

Translate a financial question by specifying the output, naming the inputs and decisions, declaring domains and units, stating timing and fee assumptions, writing relationships, and separating constraints from the objective. Solve only after those choices are explicit. Then interpret the answer in the original terms and preserve any reason for infeasibility.

The worked model uses one transfer, one currency, rational arithmetic, no rounding, and continuously divisible amounts. It does not implement settlement timing, borrowing, multiple accounts, cent increments, or contractual fee tiers.
