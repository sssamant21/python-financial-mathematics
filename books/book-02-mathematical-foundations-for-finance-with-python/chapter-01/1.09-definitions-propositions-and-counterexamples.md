## 1.9.1 Learning Objectives

After this section, you should be able to write a definition with an explicit domain, distinguish definitions from assumptions and propositions, prove a simple financial claim, and disprove an overbroad claim with a valid counterexample. You should also be able to explain what a finite Python search establishes and what it leaves unresolved.

Section 1.8 introduced the structure of conditional reasoning. Here we use that structure to decide what a mathematical statement actually requires as evidence. The central distinction is between naming an object, assuming a condition, and establishing a conclusion.

Our running model is a hypothetical affine fee schedule. It is small enough to analyze exactly but rich enough to expose a common error: confusing a higher total fee with a higher fee per unit of transaction amount.

## 1.9.2 Definitions Establish Meaning

A **definition** specifies how a term or symbol will be used. For a transaction amount \(x>0\), fixed fee \(F\geq0\), and proportional fee rate \(k\geq0\), define total fee and effective fee rate by

\[
f(x):=F+kx,\qquad e(x):=\frac{f(x)}x.
\]

The symbol \(:=\) signals a definition. Once these definitions are adopted, \(e(x)=F/x+k\) follows by algebra. The restriction \(x>0\) is essential to the definition of the effective rate. The fee formula itself can be evaluated at zero, but dividing its result by zero is undefined.

A definition should identify the objects, the domain, and the rule. “A small fee” is not yet a usable definition because “small” lacks a reference. “An effective fee rate below 1%” is precise once the rate and denominator are defined.

Definitions can vary between models. An effective fee rate here means fee divided by transaction amount; it is not an annualized interest rate or a percentage of the post-fee balance. The label alone cannot determine the denominator. A useful definition removes that ambiguity rather than relying on a familiar-sounding name.

## 1.9.3 Assumptions and Propositions Do Different Jobs

An **assumption** is a condition accepted for the argument. In our model, nonnegative fees and a positive transaction amount are assumptions. A **proposition** is a statement that can be true or false; in mathematical writing the label also commonly introduces a claim followed by a proof.

Consider this proposition:

> For fixed \(F\geq0\) and \(k\geq0\), the effective fee rate is nonincreasing as the positive transaction amount increases.

The assumptions identify the permitted inputs and keep the fee schedule fixed while comparing amounts. The conclusion is something to establish. It is not part of the definition of effective rate.

| Role | Example | What supports it? |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | \(e(x):=f(x)/x\) for \(x>0\) | Explicit convention |
| Model assumption | \(f(x)=F+kx\), with fixed nonnegative parameters | Stated model scope |
| Proposition | \(e(b)\leq e(a)\) when \(0<a<b\) | Deduction from the model |
| Observed instance | A quoted transaction has amount 200 | Data and its interpretation |

A proof establishes the proposition under its assumptions. It does not establish that a particular provider actually uses the assumed fee schedule. Conversely, a provider's fee table can support a model choice without proving a theorem about every possible fee function.

## 1.9.4 Quantifiers Make the Claim Testable

“Bigger transactions have lower effective fees” is ambiguous. Does “lower” mean strictly lower or no greater? Are the two transactions subject to the same fee parameters? Are all positive amounts allowed?

A precise non-strict claim is

\[
\forall F,k\geq0,\quad\forall a,b\in\mathbb R,
\quad 0<a<b\Rightarrow e(b)\leq e(a),
\]

where both rates use the same \(F\) and \(k\). For fixed parameters, this is the definition of a nonincreasing function on positive amounts.

A strict claim replaces \(\leq\) with \(<\). That small change strengthens the proposition. It demands strict improvement for every pair, which cannot hold when \(F=0\): then \(e(x)=k\) for every positive amount.

The order of quantifiers matters. “For every amount there exists a fee schedule with a low effective rate” does not identify one schedule that works for every amount. Before testing a statement, rewrite it so that each parameter is either fixed or quantified. A test cannot resolve a claim whose objects keep changing unnoticed.

## 1.9.5 A Direct Proof from the Definitions

Fix \(F\geq0\), \(k\geq0\), and \(0<a<b\). Using the definition,

\[
\begin{aligned}
e(a)-e(b)
&=\left(\frac Fa+k\right)-\left(\frac Fb+k\right)\\
&=F\left(\frac1a-\frac1b\right)\\
&=\frac{F(b-a)}{ab}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The denominator is positive because both amounts are positive. The factor \(b-a\) is strictly positive. Therefore the difference is nonnegative when \(F\geq0\), proving \(e(b)\leq e(a)\).

If \(F>0\), the numerator is strictly positive, so the inequality is strict. If \(F=0\), the difference is zero, so the effective rates are equal. These cases establish both the original non-strict proposition and the exact condition that supports strictness within the assumed domain.

Notice that \(k\) cancels. Its nonnegativity is part of the fee model, but the comparison itself only needs the same \(k\) on both sides. Identifying which assumptions a proof actually uses helps when generalizing a result. We retain the original nonnegative fee model here to keep the interpretation consistent.

## 1.9.6 A Counterexample Disproves a Universal Claim

To disprove a statement of the form “for every admissible input, the conclusion holds,” supply one admissible input for which the conclusion fails. That input is a **counterexample**.

Claim: for every \(F,k\geq0\) and \(0<a<b\), \(e(b)<e(a)\).

Choose \(F=0\), \(k=0.01\), \(a=100\), and \(b=200\). Then

\[
e(100)=0.01,\qquad e(200)=0.01.
\]

The assumptions hold, but the strict conclusion does not. This disproves the universal claim. It does not disprove the non-strict proposition, which permits equality.

A proposed counterexample must meet every assumption. Choosing \(a=0\) is invalid because the effective rate is undefined there. Choosing \(F=-1\) falls outside the nonnegative fixed-fee model. Such examples can reveal why assumptions matter, but they do not refute a proposition that explicitly excludes them.

A counterexample is especially useful when it is simple enough to verify by hand. The zero fixed-fee boundary isolates the reason strictness fails without relying on a complicated numerical calculation.

## 1.9.7 Total Fees and Effective Rates

For \(F=5\) and \(k=0.01\), compare amounts 100 and 200:

| Amount | Total fee | Effective fee rate |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 100 | 6 | 0.06 = 6% |
| 200 | 7 | 0.035 = 3.5% |

The larger transaction pays a higher total fee and a lower effective rate. Thus the claim “if total fees rise, effective fee rates must rise” is false in this model.

The two comparisons can be proved independently:

\[
f(b)-f(a)=k(b-a),
\qquad
e(a)-e(b)=\frac{F(b-a)}{ab}.
\]

Total fees strictly increase when \(k>0\); effective rates strictly decrease when \(F>0\). When both parameters are positive, both behaviors occur simultaneously. Neither comparison contradicts the other because they concern different quantities.

This example illustrates a useful review question: what exactly is the proposition comparing? Absolute costs, costs per unit, percentages of different bases, and annualized measures need separate definitions before their directions can be compared.

## 1.9.8 Repairing an Overbroad Statement

Finding a counterexample should lead to a corrected claim. For the failed strict-decrease statement, there are two natural repairs:

1. Keep \(F\geq0\), but weaken the conclusion to nonincreasing effective rates.
2. Keep strict decrease, but strengthen the assumption to \(F>0\).

The proof in Section 1.9.5 establishes both repairs. Merely excluding the single tested value \(a=100\) would miss the issue: for \(F=0\), equality holds for every pair of positive amounts.

Do not repair a theorem by silently changing the model. If different transactions have different fixed fees or proportional rates, the comparison now concerns two schedules. For example, amount 100 at \(F=0,k=0.01\) has rate 1%, while amount 200 at \(F=0,k=0.02\) has rate 2%. This is outside the fixed-schedule proposition.

A corrected statement should say what changed and why the proof now works. In software, the corresponding repair may be a clearer docstring, a stronger input precondition, or a corrected expected result. Altering code until one example passes is not enough to establish the intended rule.

## 1.9.9 Examples, Proofs, and Search Results

Ten successful examples do not prove a universal claim over all positive real amounts. They establish the claim for those ten inputs. A symbolic proof can cover the whole domain because its steps apply to arbitrary admissible inputs.

A finite search has three useful outcomes. It can find a counterexample, exhaust a genuinely finite declared domain, or report that no counterexample was found among the tested candidates. The third outcome must not be relabeled as a proof over a larger domain.

An exact rational counterexample can also refute a claim over real numbers because rational inputs are real inputs. Conversely, searching rational candidates without finding a failure does not establish the claim for every real input.

The lab below searches an explicitly listed finite set. It reports the number of pairs examined and preserves any witness. It raises errors for invalid input instead of counting unsupported cases as successful evaluations. This makes the scope of the evidence visible.

## 1.9.10 Exact Arithmetic and Executable Claims

Binary floating-point rounding can complicate a comparison that should be exact under a small rational model. The lab uses `fractions.Fraction` with integer and string inputs, so the quoted decimal `"0.01"` becomes exactly \(1/100\).

Constructing a fraction from a float preserves that float's value; it does not recover an intended decimal quotation. Our interface therefore rejects floats, Boolean values, and unsupported objects rather than guessing a conversion convention. This is an input design choice, not a claim that all finance computations should use rational arithmetic.

The search is deterministic: candidate amounts must be strictly increasing, and all pairs of distinct candidates are checked in their supplied order. A first witness is reproducible. Returning `None` for a witness means no failure occurred in that finite search; the report says nothing about untested amounts.

For runtime input validation, the example uses explicit exceptions. The companion check script uses assertions to detect violated expectations during testing. Python can remove assertion statements under optimization, so run that script without `-O`. An assertion is executable evidence for the cases actually run; it is not a universal mathematical proof.

## 1.9.11 Worked Python Lab — Preserve the Witness

Save this program as [code/section_1_09.py](code/section_1_09.py). The search accepts one declared comparison at a time and returns the first pair that violates it.

```python
"""Exact affine-fee comparisons and finite counterexample searches."""
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import combinations


def rational(value):
    if type(value) not in (int, str, Fraction):
        raise TypeError("use an integer, rational string, or Fraction")
    return Fraction(value)


def fee_metrics(*, amount, fixed_fee, fee_rate):
    x, fixed, rate = map(rational, (amount, fixed_fee, fee_rate))
    if x <= 0 or fixed < 0 or rate < 0:
        raise ValueError("require amount > 0 and nonnegative fee parameters")
    total = fixed + rate * x
    return {"total": total, "effective": total / x}


def search_pairs(*, amounts, fixed_fee, fee_rate, claim):
    claims = ("effective_nonincreasing", "effective_strictly_decreasing")
    if claim not in claims:
        raise ValueError("unknown claim")
    xs = tuple(rational(x) for x in amounts)
    if len(xs) < 2 or any(x <= 0 for x in xs):
        raise ValueError("provide at least two positive amounts")
    if any(a >= b for a, b in zip(xs, xs[1:])):
        raise ValueError("amounts must be strictly increasing")
    metrics = [fee_metrics(amount=x, fixed_fee=fixed_fee, fee_rate=fee_rate)
               for x in xs]
    checked = 0
    witness = None
    for i, j in combinations(range(len(xs)), 2):
        left, right = metrics[i]["effective"], metrics[j]["effective"]
        holds = right <= left if claim == claims[0] else right < left
        checked += 1
        if not holds and witness is None:
            witness = {"a": xs[i], "b": xs[j],
                       "effective_a": left, "effective_b": right}
    return {"claim": claim, "pairs_checked": checked,
            "counterexample": witness,
            "scope": "supplied finite candidate pairs only"}


def main():
    for x in (100, 200):
        m = fee_metrics(amount=x, fixed_fee=5, fee_rate="0.01")
        print(f"Amount {x}: total={m['total']}, effective={m['effective']}")
    strict = search_pairs(amounts=(100, 200, 400), fixed_fee=0,
                          fee_rate="0.01", claim="effective_strictly_decreasing")
    weak = search_pairs(amounts=(100, 200, 400), fixed_fee=0,
                        fee_rate="0.01", claim="effective_nonincreasing")
    print(f"Strict claim witness: {strict['counterexample']}")
    print(f"Non-strict claim witness: {weak['counterexample']}")
    print(f"Pairs checked per search: {weak['pairs_checked']}")
    print(f"Scope: {weak['scope']}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

Expected output:

```text
Amount 100: total=6, effective=3/50
Amount 200: total=7, effective=7/200
Strict claim witness: {'a': Fraction(100, 1), 'b': Fraction(200, 1), 'effective_a': Fraction(1, 100), 'effective_b': Fraction(1, 100)}
Non-strict claim witness: None
Pairs checked per search: 3
Scope: supplied finite candidate pairs only
```

## 1.9.12 Practice Problems

1. Define the effective fee rate for \(f(x)=F+kx\). State its domain and explain why evaluating total fee at zero does not define an effective rate there.
2. With \(F=3\) and \(k=0.02\), calculate total and effective fees for amounts 100 and 300. Which quantities increase or decrease?
3. Disprove “effective fee rates strictly decrease for every nonnegative fixed fee.” Give all parameters and verify the assumptions.
4. Prove that total fees are nondecreasing for a fixed schedule with \(k\geq0\). What extra condition gives strict increase?
5. A search finds no violation among amounts 1, 2, and 3. What conclusion is justified? How many distinct increasing pairs were tested?
6. A proposed counterexample uses \(F=-2\) to challenge a proposition assuming \(F\geq0\). Is it valid? Give two valid repairs to the strict-decrease claim instead.

## 1.9.13 Worked Answers

1. Define \(e(x):=f(x)/x\) for \(x>0\) in this transaction model. At zero, \(f(0)=F\) is defined, but the quotient divides by zero. When \(F=0\), the quotient remains undefined at zero unless a separate extension is explicitly defined.
2. At 100 the fee is \(3+2=5\), so the effective rate is 5%. At 300 the fee is \(3+6=9\), so the effective rate is 3%. Total fee increases by 4; effective rate decreases by 2 percentage points.
3. Set \(F=0,k=0.01,a=100,b=200\). All assumptions hold. Both rates equal 1%, contradicting strict decrease. Equality is enough to refute a strict inequality.
4. For \(0<a<b\), \(f(b)-f(a)=k(b-a)\geq0\). If \(k>0\), the difference is positive and total fee strictly increases. If \(k=0\), total fee is constant.
5. The claim held for the three supplied pairs: (1,2), (1,3), and (2,3). That is exhaustive for the declared candidate list, not for all positive real amounts. A proof or an explicitly restricted finite claim is needed for a broader conclusion.
6. It is not a counterexample to that proposition because it violates an assumption. Keep nonnegative fixed fees and use a non-strict conclusion, or require strictly positive fixed fees and retain strict decrease. Both repairs follow from \(F(b-a)/(ab)\).

## 1.9.14 Common Mistakes, Summary, and Sources

A definition fixes meaning; it does not prove that a real system follows the chosen model. A proposition requires support. A universal claim can be disproved by one admissible counterexample, while a finite list of successful examples generally cannot prove a statement over an infinite domain.

Common mistakes are omitting domains, switching between strict and non-strict inequalities, varying a parameter that was supposed to remain fixed, and confusing total amounts with ratios. A failed program execution is not automatically a mathematical counterexample: first establish that the inputs satisfy the assumptions and that the calculation evaluates the intended expression correctly.

Use this review sequence: state the definition, list assumptions, write the quantified claim, attempt a derivation, inspect boundary cases, and preserve any counterexample with its exact inputs. If a search finds no failure, report its finite scope. Keep the distinction between a proof, a test, and an observation visible in both prose and code.
