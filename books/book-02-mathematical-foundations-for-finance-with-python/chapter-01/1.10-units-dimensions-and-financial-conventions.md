## 1.10.1 Learning Objectives

After this section, you should be able to annotate financial quantities with units, check the dimensional consistency of a formula, distinguish percentages from percentage points and basis points, and identify the conventions required to interpret a quoted rate. You should also be able to calculate two explicitly supported day-count fractions and explain why a dimensionally consistent calculation can still use the wrong model.

A number such as 5 does not tell us whether it represents dollars, cents, millions of dollars, a percentage quotation, or a number of periods. These distinctions often disappear when values enter a spreadsheet or Python function. Our task is to preserve them before arithmetic begins.

The examples use synthetic quantities and simplified contracts. The Python lab makes a few conventions explicit; it does not infer the terms of a real financial agreement.

## 1.10.2 Quantity, Numerical Value, and Unit

A quantity combines a numerical value with a unit or other interpretation. For example,

\[
V=250\ \mathrm{USD}=25{,}000\ \mathrm{US\ cents}.
\]

The quantity is unchanged; the numerical value changes with the unit. Adding 250 dollars to 100 cents requires conversion before combining their numerical values. Adding 250 dollars to 100 euros requires a specified exchange rate as well as compatible timing and valuation assumptions.

A **dimension** describes the kind of quantity, such as currency, time, or number of shares. A **unit** supplies a particular representation, such as USD, day, or share. In financial software it is useful to treat different currencies as distinct labels even though both represent money. A broad “money” dimension alone would not catch an unconverted USD-plus-EUR sum.

Annotations are not ornamental. A table column named `amount` is weaker than `amount_usd`; `amount_usd_millions` also records scale. A rate needs additional metadata because its interpretation depends on quotation, period, and calculation convention.

## 1.10.3 Addition, Multiplication, and Ratios

Only compatible quantities can be added directly. Multiplication and division combine units. If a position contains \(q=40\) shares and its price is \(p=25\ \mathrm{USD/share}\), then

\[
V=qp=(40\ \mathrm{shares})(25\ \mathrm{USD/share})
=1{,}000\ \mathrm{USD}.
\]

Adding \(q+p\) would mix shares with USD per share and has no intended meaning here.

A ratio of two amounts in the same currency is dimensionless. For instance, a fee of 6 USD on a transaction of 100 USD gives \(6/100=0.06\). Yet dimensionless does not mean interpretation-free. The ratio could represent a fee fraction, a return, a weight, or a tax fraction. These meanings are not interchangeable.

The same warning applies to denominators. Fee divided by transaction amount differs from fee divided by remaining balance. Both are dimensionless; dimensional analysis cannot decide which definition the question requires. Definitions from Section 1.9 and units work together.

## 1.10.4 Checking a Formula's Dimensions

For a simple-interest model, let \(P\) be principal, \(r\) a rate per year, and \(t\) elapsed time measured in years. Then

\[
I=Prt
\]

has units of currency because the time units cancel. The accumulation formula

\[
A=P(1+rt)
\]

also has currency units. The expression inside the parentheses must be dimensionless: adding the pure number 1 to a quantity measured in years would be invalid.

An alternative financial convention treats a quoted annual rate \(r\) as a dimensionless number and uses a dimensionless year fraction \(\alpha\), so \(I=Pr\alpha\). The quotation convention explains how the number \(r\) relates to that fraction. These two descriptions encode the same simple-interest scaling when their conventions agree; do not mix their time representations within one calculation.

For a compound-growth formula \(P(1+i)^n\), \(i\) is the effective rate per compounding period and \(n\) is a count of matching periods. A count of 12 months does not match an effective annual rate unless the model has first converted the period consistently.

## 1.10.5 Percentages, Percentage Points, and Basis Points

A percentage is a scaled representation of a fraction:

\[
5\%=\frac5{100}=0.05.
\]

A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, or \(0.0001\) in decimal rate form. Thus 5% equals 500 basis points.

| Description | Percentage quotation | Decimal representation |
|---|---:|---:|
| Rate level | 5% | 0.05 |
| One percentage point | 1 percentage point | 0.01 |
| One basis point | 0.01 percentage point | 0.0001 |
| Twenty-five basis points | 0.25 percentage point | 0.0025 |

A rate moving from 4% to 5% increases by 1 percentage point, or 100 basis points. Its relative increase is

\[
\frac{0.05-0.04}{0.04}=0.25=25\%.
\]

These are three descriptions of related but different comparisons. Reporting “the rate rose by 1%” is ambiguous unless the context makes clear whether the change is relative or measured in percentage points.

In code, do not infer that an input `5` means 5%. Require the representation. A decimal input of `5` means 500% under a decimal-rate interface. That can be a valid numerical value, so a magnitude check alone cannot reliably detect the wrong quotation.

## 1.10.6 Scale and Currency Conversion

Suppose a balance is reported as 2.4 million USD while another is 600 thousand USD. In a common unit,

\[
2.4\times10^6+600\times10^3=3{,}000{,}000\ \mathrm{USD}.
\]

Adding the raw numbers 2.4 and 600 produces a meaningless result unless their scales are normalized first. An output labeled “millions” must also divide the result by the appropriate factor.

Currency conversion requires a direction. If \(s=1.10\ \mathrm{USD/EUR}\), then

\[
100\ \mathrm{EUR}\times s=110\ \mathrm{USD}.
\]

To convert USD to EUR under that same hypothetical rate, divide by \(s\). Multiplication by the reciprocal has the same effect. Carrying units makes the direction visible and helps detect an inverted exchange quotation.

This is only an arithmetic example. Actual conversion also needs the rate's timestamp, applicable bid or ask, fees, and rounding policy. A unit label cannot establish that two quotes refer to the same moment or transaction conditions.

## 1.10.7 Rate Periods and Compounding Conventions

The phrase “12% annual rate” is incomplete without a convention. A nominal annual rate of 12% convertible monthly assigns a monthly rate of \(0.12/12=0.01\). Under that model, one year's growth factor is \((1.01)^{12}\), approximately 1.126825.

An effective annual rate of 12% instead specifies a one-year growth factor of 1.12. Dividing this effective annual rate by 12 does not produce an equivalent monthly compound rate. That conversion requires solving

\[
(1+i_m)^{12}=1.12.
\]

Detailed rate conversions belong to Chapter 04. Here the lesson is that matching the word “annual” does not make two quoted rates equivalent. Nominal, effective, simple, and continuously compounded rates encode different calculation rules.

Likewise, 30 days is not automatically one twelfth of a contractual year, and a calendar month is not a fixed number of days. A rate's period and an accrual fraction must be interpreted together. The lab uses an explicitly declared simple annual rate and does not perform compounding conversions.

## 1.10.8 Dates and Day-Count Fractions

A day-count convention maps dates to the fraction used in an accrual formula. This section implements only two conventions:

\[
\alpha_{\mathrm{ACT/360}}=\frac d{360},\qquad
\alpha_{\mathrm{ACT/365F}}=\frac d{365},
\]

where \(d\) is the actual number of calendar days between the dates. ACT/365F means Actual/365 Fixed: the denominator stays 365, including in a leap year.

For the lab, the interval includes the start date and excludes the end date, written \([s,e)\). Date subtraction gives the number of day steps in that interval. Equal dates give zero days. Reversed dates are rejected rather than silently converted into a negative accrual.

From 2024-02-28 to 2024-03-01 there are two days because 2024 includes February 29. Therefore the fractions are \(2/360\) and \(2/365\). This example distinguishes calendar-day counting from a guess based on the month label.

Actual/Actual and the various 30/360 conventions have additional rules. An alias such as “ACT/365” without “Fixed” can also be ambiguous across systems. The lab accepts only the exact two names stated here. It performs no business-day adjustment, holiday-calendar calculation, or coupon-schedule construction. Those choices must come from the applicable specification.

## 1.10.9 Worked Accrual Comparison

Take principal \(P=10{,}000\ \mathrm{USD}\), simple annual rate \(r=6\%=0.06\), and dates 2025-01-01 to 2025-01-31. There are 30 actual days.

Under ACT/360,

\[
I=10{,}000(0.06)\frac{30}{360}=50\ \mathrm{USD}.
\]

Under ACT/365F,

\[
I=10{,}000(0.06)\frac{30}{365}
=\frac{3600}{73}\ \mathrm{USD}
\approx49.315068\ \mathrm{USD}.
\]

Both calculations are internally consistent. The correct convention depends on the declared model or contract. Choosing the denominator that produces the preferred result has no mathematical justification.

These calculations assume constant principal and rate throughout the interval. They produce unrounded modeled interest. A payment rounded to cents requires a separate rounding rule; that rule is not supplied by the day-count convention. The lab retains exact rational results to keep this distinction visible.

## 1.10.10 What Dimensional Analysis Cannot Establish

A dimensionally inconsistent expression is a strong sign of an error. A consistent expression is only a candidate for correctness. For example, both \(P(1+r\alpha)\) and \(P(1+r)^\alpha\) have currency units with dimensionless inputs, but they represent different accumulation assumptions.

Dimensions also cannot detect the difference between 0.05 and 5 when both are stored as dimensionless decimals. Nor can they detect a mislabeled currency, stale exchange rate, incorrect day-count convention, or cash flow assigned to the wrong date.

A useful input record therefore includes the value and its meaning: currency and scale, rate representation, period and compounding convention, dates, day-count name, and any rounding policy needed for the output. Not every function needs all these fields, but every field that changes the intended result must be established somewhere.

The lab checks a deliberately narrow schema. A successful run establishes compliance with that schema and its arithmetic. It does not establish that the supplied labels describe an external data source correctly.

## 1.10.11 Worked Python Lab — Explicit Rate and Accrual Conventions

Save as [code/section_1_10.py](code/section_1_10.py). Use integers, rational strings, or `Fraction` values; floats and Boolean values are rejected. The rate representation is mandatory. Currency must be an uppercase three-letter label, which is a syntax check rather than validation against a currency registry.

```python
"""Exact rate normalization and two declared simple-interest day counts."""
from datetime import date
from fractions import Fraction


def rational(value):
    if type(value) not in (int, str, Fraction):
        raise TypeError("use an integer, rational string, or Fraction")
    return Fraction(value)


def decimal_rate(value, *, representation):
    divisors = {"decimal": 1, "percent": 100, "basis_points": 10000}
    if representation not in divisors:
        raise ValueError("unknown rate representation")
    return rational(value) / divisors[representation]


def year_fraction(start, end, *, convention):
    if type(start) is not date or type(end) is not date:
        raise TypeError("use date objects, not timestamps or strings")
    if end < start:
        raise ValueError("end must not precede start")
    denominators = {"ACT/360": 360, "ACT/365F": 365}
    if convention not in denominators:
        raise ValueError("unsupported day-count convention")
    return Fraction((end - start).days, denominators[convention])


def simple_accrual(*, principal, currency, rate, representation,
                   start, end, convention):
    if (type(currency) is not str or len(currency) != 3
            or any(c not in "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ" for c in currency)):
        raise ValueError("currency must be an uppercase three-letter label")
    p = rational(principal)
    if p < 0:
        raise ValueError("this lab requires nonnegative principal")
    r = decimal_rate(rate, representation=representation)
    alpha = year_fraction(start, end, convention=convention)
    return {"currency": currency, "decimal_rate": r,
            "year_fraction": alpha, "interest": p * r * alpha}


def main():
    print("Equivalent rate quotations:",
          decimal_rate("0.06", representation="decimal"),
          decimal_rate(6, representation="percent"),
          decimal_rate(600, representation="basis_points"))
    for convention in ("ACT/360", "ACT/365F"):
        report = simple_accrual(
            principal=10000, currency="USD", rate=6,
            representation="percent", start=date(2025, 1, 1),
            end=date(2025, 1, 31), convention=convention)
        print(f"{convention}: fraction={report['year_fraction']}, "
              f"interest={report['interest']} {report['currency']}")
    print("Leap interval:", year_fraction(date(2024, 2, 28),
          date(2024, 3, 1), convention="ACT/365F"))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

Expected output:

```text
Equivalent rate quotations: 3/50 3/50 3/50
ACT/360: fraction=1/12, interest=50 USD
ACT/365F: fraction=6/73, interest=3600/73 USD
Leap interval: 2/365
```

Run `python code/section_1_10.py` and `python code/check_section_1_10.py` from the chapter directory. On Windows, `py` can replace `python` if that is your configured launcher. The [validation record](validation/section-1.10.json) records the actual environment used; Windows execution is not claimed. Run reference checks without `-O` because they use assertions.

The lab permits negative quoted rates while requiring nonnegative principal. Negative interest is therefore possible under its simple-interest model. It deliberately imposes no universal upper rate bound: quotation errors must be prevented through explicit representation and source validation, not by assuming all large rates are impossible.

## 1.10.12 Practice Problems

1. A position holds 75 shares at 18 USD per share. Compute its value and show unit cancellation. Explain why adding the two raw inputs is invalid.
2. A rate rises from 3% to 3.5%. Express the change in percentage points, basis points, and relative percentage terms.
3. Combine 1.2 million USD and 350 thousand USD. Report the result in USD and in millions of USD.
4. Compute simple interest on 12,000 USD at 5% for 90 actual days under ACT/360 and ACT/365F. Keep exact values before any rounding.
5. What does the lab return for the fraction from 2024-02-28 to 2024-03-01 under ACT/365F? Why is the denominator not 366?
6. A function receives rate `5` and uses a decimal representation. What rate has it received? Can dimensional analysis alone prove the caller intended 5%?

## 1.10.13 Worked Answers

1. \((75\ \mathrm{shares})(18\ \mathrm{USD/share})=1{,}350\ \mathrm{USD}\). Shares cancel. The sum mixes shares with USD per share, so it has no intended position-value interpretation.
2. The difference is 0.5 percentage point, or 50 basis points. The relative increase is \((0.035-0.03)/0.03=1/6\), approximately 16.6667%. These descriptions use different denominators and scales.
3. \(1{,}200{,}000+350{,}000=1{,}550{,}000\ \mathrm{USD}\), or 1.55 million USD. Normalize the scales before addition.
4. ACT/360 gives \(12{,}000(0.05)(90/360)=150\ \mathrm{USD}\). ACT/365F gives \(54{,}000/365=10{,}800/73\ \mathrm{USD}\), approximately 147.945205 USD. A cents result needs a rounding policy.
5. Two actual days produce \(2/365\). “Fixed” specifies the denominator even in a leap year. The extra calendar day affects the numerator; this convention does not change the denominator to 366.
6. Decimal 5 is 500%. The intended 5% would be decimal 0.05 or percent quotation 5. Both values are dimensionless, so dimensional consistency cannot resolve the caller's intention. Require the representation and verify it against the source.

## 1.10.14 Common Mistakes, Summary, and Sources

Common errors include adding different scales, reversing an exchange quotation, confusing basis points with percentages, applying a monthly count to an annual period rate, and treating a year as 365 days regardless of the declared convention. Another is rounding intermediate results without stating a rule.

Before calculating, label the quantities, normalize compatible units and scales, declare the rate representation, and identify the time and accrual conventions. Check dimensions next, then check that the formula expresses the intended model. Finally, apply any required output rounding separately.

This section implements two fixed-denominator day-count conventions and simple interest only. It does not implement Actual/Actual, 30/360 variants, business-day calendars, currency conversion, compounding conversion, or a production currency registry. Those exclusions define the scope of the executable evidence.
