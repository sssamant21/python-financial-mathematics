## 2.1.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you should be able to identify terms, factors, coefficients, and grouping in an algebraic expression; expand and factor expressions using stated rules; and distinguish equivalent forms from equations that hold only at selected inputs. You should also be able to preserve domain exclusions during simplification and choose a useful form for interpreting a financial model.

Chapter 01 established mathematical language and checking methods. Chapter 02 develops the algebra needed to rearrange formulas, solve equations, and validate financial calculations. We begin with expressions themselves: before isolating an unknown, understand which transformations preserve the quantity being calculated.

The running example is a hypothetical one-period investment value with fees. A second example expands a two-period growth factor. These models supply small, transparent expressions for algebra; their assumptions do not describe every financial product.

## 2.1.2 Read the Structure Before Calculating

An **expression** combines numbers, variables, and operations to represent a value. For example,

\[
P+Pr-F-kP
\]

contains four additive terms: \(P\), \(Pr\), \(-F\), and \(-kP\). Each term includes its sign. In \(Pr\), the factors are \(P\) and \(r\); in \(kP\), they are \(k\) and \(P\).

A coefficient is the multiplicative part attached to a selected variable or factor. The coefficient of \(P\) in \(Pr\) is \(r\) when \(P\) is the variable being collected. A coefficient need not be a fixed numerical literal: its role depends on which symbols are being treated as parameters.

Grouping determines which operation applies to which quantity. In \(P(1+r)\), principal multiplies the entire sum \(1+r\). In \(P+r\), it does not. In \((A-P)/P\), the whole difference is divided by principal; \(A-P/P\) represents a different expression.

Describe an expression in words and then identify its outermost operation. This habit makes later rearrangements easier to inspect and catches translation errors before numbers obscure the structure.

## 2.1.3 Equality of Values and Equivalence of Expressions

Two expressions are **equivalent on a stated domain** if they have equal values for every input in that domain. The identity

\[
P(1+r)=P+Pr
\]

holds for all real \(P,r\). It follows from the distributive law, not from testing a particular balance and rate.

By contrast, \(P+r=P(1+r)\) is not an identity. It holds for some inputs, such as \(P=1\), but fails for others: \(P=100,r=0.05\) gives 100.05 on the left and 105 on the right. A coincidental passing example does not establish equivalence.

Keep three statements separate: two expressions can have the same value at one input; they can be equivalent throughout a domain; or an equation between them can be a condition to solve. This section focuses on the second. Section 2.2 will develop equation solving and rearrangement.

Every equivalence claim should carry the domain on which the operations are defined. Expressions that agree on their common domain need not define the same function on different, larger domains.

## 2.1.4 Use the Algebraic Laws Deliberately

For real quantities, addition and multiplication are commutative and associative. You may reorder additive terms or multiplicative factors and regroup them without changing an exact real value. The distributive law connects the operations:

\[
a(b+c)=ab+ac.
\]

Applying it backward factors a common multiplier:

\[
ab+ac=a(b+c).
\]

Subtraction must retain signs. For example,

\[
P-(F+kP)=P-F-kP.
\]

The minus sign applies to both terms inside the parentheses. Writing \(P-F+kP\) would reverse the effect of the proportional fee.

Division has its own restrictions. Rewriting \(a/b\) as \(a\times(1/b)\) requires \(b\ne0\). You cannot reorder subtraction or division as though they were commutative: \(a-b\) and \(b-a\) generally differ.

These laws refer to exact arithmetic. Floating-point evaluation can depend on grouping because intermediate results are rounded. This section's Python lab uses exact rational values so it can check the stated identities without introducing a tolerance policy. Later numerical chapters revisit the choice of form for finite-precision computation.

## 2.1.5 Expand and Collect Like Terms

Suppose initial principal is \(P\), the effective return for one period is \(r\), the fixed fee is \(F\), and an additional fee is \(kP\), proportional to initial principal. Define final modeled value by

\[
A=P(1+r)-F-kP.
\]

Expanding and collecting the terms involving principal gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
A&=P+Pr-F-kP\\
 &=P(1+r-k)-F.
\end{aligned}
\]

The same value appears in both forms. The first displays gross growth and the two fee deductions. The second displays principal multiplied by a net proportional factor, followed by the fixed deduction.

Like terms must share the same variable structure. Terms \(Pr\) and \(-kP\) can be combined as \(P(r-k)\) while retaining the two coefficients. Terms \(Pr\) and \(Pr^2\) cannot be combined by adding their powers to obtain \(2Pr^3\). For example, \(2r+3r^2\) is not \(5r^3\).

Do not discard units while collecting terms. Here \(r\) and \(k\) are decimal fractions, and \(P,F,A\) share one currency and scale. Subtracting \(k\) directly from a currency amount would not represent the stated fee.

## 2.1.6 Derive Equivalent Net-Return Forms

For positive principal, define net return relative to initial principal as

\[
R_{\mathrm{net}}:=\frac{A-P}{P},\qquad P>0.
\]

Substitute the fee model and simplify one step at a time:

\[
\begin{aligned}
R_{\mathrm{net}}
&=\frac{P(1+r)-F-kP-P}{P}\\
&=\frac{Pr-kP-F}{P}\\
&=r-k-\frac FP.
\end{aligned}
\]

Another equivalent form is

\[
R_{\mathrm{net}}=\frac{P(r-k)-F}{P}.
\]

Each form emphasizes something different. The balance-based quotient connects return to the original definition. The collected numerator separates net currency gain from its denominator. The final form shows the gross rate reduced by the proportional fee rate and the fixed fee as a fraction of principal.

The restriction \(P>0\) is essential to this return definition. At \(P=0\), the balance expression can still be evaluated, but the return quotient divides by zero. An implementation must preserve that distinction rather than infer that every defined final value has a defined return.

## 2.1.7 Interpret the Financial Assumptions

Assume \(P>0\), \(r>-1\), and \(F,k\geq0\). The effective period rate and fee fraction are supplied as decimals for the same one-period model. There are no additional cash flows, taxes, or rounding steps.

The proportional fee is explicitly based on initial principal. If it were based on gross ending value instead, it would be \(kP(1+r)\), and the net return would become

\[
(1+r)(1-k)-1-F/P=r-k-rk-F/P.
\]

The extra term \(-rk\) is a consequence of a changed fee basis, not an alternative simplification of the original model. Even when two results are numerically close, they need not represent equivalent expressions.

The original assumptions do not guarantee nonnegative final value after fees. For example, \(P=10,r=0,F=20,k=0\) yields \(A=-10\) and \(R_{\mathrm{net}}=-2\). The lab reports this exact modeled result. If an application requires \(A\geq0\), add that as a separate constraint; do not silently clamp the output to zero.

Equivalence preserves a model's meaning. It does not supply missing economic assumptions or make the model appropriate for a particular transaction.

## 2.1.8 Worked Comparison of Forms

Take principal 1,000, gross period return 6%, fixed fee 5, and proportional fee fraction 1%. The final modeled value is

\[
A=1000(1.06)-5-0.01(1000)=1045.
\]

The balance-based return is \((1045-1000)/1000=45/1000=0.045\), or 4.5%. The decomposed expression gives

\[
0.06-0.01-5/1000=0.045.
\]

Doubling principal to 2,000 with the same rate and fee parameters gives \(A=2095\) and return \(95/2000=0.0475\), or 4.75%. The fixed-fee fraction has fallen from 0.5% to 0.25%; the proportional fee fraction is unchanged.

These calculations illustrate a symbolic conclusion. For two positive principals \(P_1<P_2\), the net-return difference is

\[
R_{\mathrm{net}}(P_2)-R_{\mathrm{net}}(P_1)
=F\left(\frac1{P_1}-\frac1{P_2}\right)\geq0.
\]

It is strictly positive when \(F>0\), and zero when \(F=0\), provided the other parameters stay fixed. The equivalent decomposed form makes this relationship easier to see than the original balance quotient.

## 2.1.9 Expand a Product Without Losing Cross Terms

A two-period fixed-rate growth expression is

\[
P(1+r)^2=P(1+2r+r^2)=P+2Pr+Pr^2.
\]

The \(r^2\) term comes from multiplying \((1+r)(1+r)\). The two cross terms are both \(r\), so together they give \(2r\). Replacing the square by \(1+r^2\) loses both cross terms; replacing it by \(1+2r\) drops \(r^2\).

With \(P=100,r=0.10\), the factored and expanded expressions both give 121. The expression \(P(1+2r)\) gives 120. Its difference from the exact growth model is \(Pr^2=1\). It can be studied as an approximation under additional accuracy requirements, but it is not an equivalent form.

The factorization \((1+r)^2-1=r(2+r)\) is also useful. It displays that the two-period growth increment is zero at \(r=0\). This observation comes from the expression itself, without solving a new equation numerically.

## 2.1.10 Cancel Factors and Preserve Exclusions

From the factorization above,

\[
\frac{(1+r)^2-1}{r}
=\frac{r(2+r)}r=2+r,\qquad r\ne0.
\]

Cancellation removes a common nonzero factor. It does not allow setting the canceled denominator to zero. The original quotient is undefined at \(r=0\), while \(2+r\) is defined there. Defining the latter at zero extends the expression's domain; it does not evaluate the original quotient at a previously excluded input.

Cancel factors, not selected pieces of an addition. For example,

\[
\frac{Pr-F}{P}=r-\frac FP,
\]

not \(r-F\). The factor \(P\) is present in \(Pr\) but is not a factor of the entire numerator unless \(F\) is expressed accordingly. With \(P=100,r=0.10,F=5\), the correct quotient is 0.05, while \(r-F=-4.9\).

Keeping exclusions visible becomes especially important when simplifying rational equations in Section 2.3. A simplified expression can be easier to evaluate while still requiring the original domain contract.

## 2.1.11 Worked Python Lab — Compare Exact Forms

Save as [code/section_2_01.py](code/section_2_01.py). The lab requires exact integer, rational-string, or `Fraction` inputs and rejects floats and Boolean values. It evaluates three net-return forms, two growth forms, and the cancellation identity with its exclusion intact.

```python
"""Exact equivalent forms with explicit mathematical and model domains."""
from fractions import Fraction


def rational(value):
    if type(value) not in (int, str, Fraction):
        raise TypeError("use an integer, rational string, or Fraction")
    return Fraction(value)


def net_return_forms(*, principal, period_rate, fixed_fee, fee_rate):
    p, r, fixed, k = map(rational, (principal, period_rate, fixed_fee, fee_rate))
    if p <= 0 or r <= -1 or fixed < 0 or k < 0:
        raise ValueError("require P > 0, r > -1, F >= 0, k >= 0")
    ending_value = p * (1 + r) - fixed - k * p
    forms = {
        "balance_quotient": (ending_value - p) / p,
        "collected_numerator": (p * (r - k) - fixed) / p,
        "rate_decomposition": r - k - fixed / p,
    }
    return {"ending_value": ending_value, "forms": forms,
            "agree": len(set(forms.values())) == 1}


def two_period_forms(*, principal, period_rate):
    p, r = map(rational, (principal, period_rate))
    if p < 0 or r <= -1:
        raise ValueError("require nonnegative principal and rate > -1")
    factored = p * (1 + r) ** 2
    expanded = p + 2 * p * r + p * r * r
    return {"factored": factored, "expanded": expanded,
            "agree": factored == expanded}


def cancellation_forms(rate):
    r = rational(rate)
    if r == 0:
        raise ValueError("original quotient excludes rate = 0")
    original = ((1 + r) ** 2 - 1) / r
    simplified = 2 + r
    return {"original": original, "simplified": simplified,
            "agree": original == simplified}


def main():
    for principal in (1000, 2000):
        report = net_return_forms(principal=principal, period_rate="0.06",
                                  fixed_fee=5, fee_rate="0.01")
        print(f"Principal {principal}: value={report['ending_value']}, "
              f"net return={report['forms']['rate_decomposition']}, "
              f"agree={report['agree']}")
    growth = two_period_forms(principal=100, period_rate="0.10")
    print(f"Growth: factored={growth['factored']}, "
          f"expanded={growth['expanded']}, agree={growth['agree']}")
    quotient = cancellation_forms("0.10")
    print(f"Cancellation at 0.10: {quotient['original']} = "
          f"{quotient['simplified']}")
    try:
        cancellation_forms(0)
    except ValueError:
        print("Cancellation at zero: excluded by original domain")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

Expected output:

```text
Principal 1000: value=1045, net return=9/200, agree=True
Principal 2000: value=2095, net return=19/400, agree=True
Growth: factored=121, expanded=121, agree=True
Cancellation at 0.10: 21/10 = 21/10
Cancellation at zero: excluded by original domain
```

## 2.1.12 Practice Problems

1. Expand \(3P(1+r)-2P-F\), collect terms in principal, and identify the coefficient of \(P\) when \(r\) is treated as a parameter.
2. Under the original fee model, calculate final value and net return for \(P=800,r=0.05,F=4,k=0.01\). Verify both the balance quotient and rate decomposition.
3. At those same inputs, suppose the proportional fee is instead charged on gross ending value. Derive the changed net-return expression and calculate the difference from Problem 2.
4. Expand \((1+a)(1+b)\). At \(a=0.02,b=0.03\), compare its value with \(1+a+b\). Is dropping the product term an equivalent transformation?
5. Simplify \((x^2-9)/(x-3)\), retaining the original domain. Can the original expression be evaluated at \(x=3\)? Verify the identity at \(x=4\).
6. A developer rewrites \((Pr-F)/P\) as \(r-F\). Give an admissible counterexample and explain the cancellation mistake. State the principal restriction for the correct expression.

## 2.1.13 Worked Answers

1. Expansion gives \(3P+3Pr-2P-F=P+3Pr-F=P(1+3r)-F\). The coefficient of \(P\) is \(1+3r\). Treating \(r\) as a parameter allows it to appear inside that coefficient.
2. Gross value is 840, proportional fee is 8, and final value is 828. Net return is \((828-800)/800=28/800=7/200=0.035\), or 3.5%. Decomposition gives \(0.05-0.01-4/800=0.035\).
3. The proportional fee is \(0.01(840)=8.4\); final value is 827.6. Return is \(0.05-0.01-0.05(0.01)-4/800=69/2000=0.0345\). It is 0.0005 lower than Problem 2, a difference of 5 basis points. The changed fee basis accounts for the cross term.
4. Expansion gives \(1+a+b+ab\). At the stated rates it is 1.0506, while the expression without the product term is 1.05. The difference is \(ab=0.0006\). Omitting it is not an identity.
5. Factor the numerator as \((x-3)(x+3)\). The quotient equals \(x+3\) for \(x\ne3\). At 3 the original expression is undefined; defining the simplified expression there extends its domain. At 4 both expressions give 7.
6. Choose \(P=100,r=0.10,F=5\). The correct value is 0.05; the proposed rewrite gives −4.9. Only the first numerator term has the visible factor \(P\); division of the whole difference yields \(r-F/P\). Algebraically require \(P\ne0\); the financial return model here uses the stronger domain \(P>0\).

## 2.1.14 Common Mistakes, Summary, and Sources

Common mistakes include combining unlike terms, distributing a negative sign to only one term, dropping cross terms from a product, canceling across a sum, and forgetting an original denominator exclusion. A subtler error is treating a changed fee basis as an equivalent algebraic form.

Read the expression's structure, state its domain, apply one justified rule at a time, and choose the form that makes the intended interpretation clear. Use a counterexample to reject a proposed identity, but use a symbolic argument to establish it throughout the stated domain. Keep exact algebra separate from finite-precision behavior and external financial model validation.
