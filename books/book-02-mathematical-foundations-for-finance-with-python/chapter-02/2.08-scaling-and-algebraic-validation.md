Financial formulas are often rearranged, multiplied by large amounts, or expressed in different units. A transformed equation may be mathematically equivalent to the original yet look very different numerically. This section distinguishes **solution-preserving algebra**, **dimensional consistency**, and **computational validation**. All financial examples are synthetic.

## 2.8.1 Learning objectives

By the end, you should be able to:

- Prove when multiplying an equation preserves its solution set.
- Identify operations that lose solutions or introduce invalid candidates.
- Distinguish currency, quantity, rates, and dimensionless factors.
- Verify a proposed solution in the original equation.
- Interpret absolute and scaled residuals without confusing them with solution error.
- Write exact, deterministic Python checks for basic algebraic transformations.

Prerequisites: Sections 2.1–2.7, especially equation rearrangement, rational arithmetic, domain restrictions, and simultaneous equations.

## 2.8.2 Equivalence is about solution sets

Let an equation be written as $f(x)=g(x)$. Two equations are *equivalent on a specified domain* if they admit exactly the same values of $x$ from that domain.

Adding the same expression to both sides preserves equality wherever that expression is defined. Multiplying both sides by a **fixed nonzero constant** also preserves equality:

$$f(x)=g(x)\iff kf(x)=kg(x),\qquad k\ne0.$$

The reverse implication follows by division by $k$. If $k=0$, the new statement is $0=0$ for every input, so information has been lost.

Multiplication by a variable expression needs more care. For example,

$$x=2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x(x-1)=2(x-1).$$

The transformed equation is satisfied by both $x=2$ and $x=1$. The additional root arises because $x-1$ can be zero. Conversely, dividing by $x-1$ may discard $x=1$ if it was a valid original solution. Record all denominator exclusions before cancellation.

Squaring is another one-way operation without additional restrictions. $x=3$ implies $x^2=9$, but $x^2=9$ also admits $x=-3$. Verify candidates in the *original* equation, not only the transformed one.

## 2.8.3 Scaling a linear equation

For $ax+by=c$, multiply every coefficient, including the right-hand side, by $k\ne0$:

$$kax+kby=kc.$$

Both equations define the same line. For example,

$$2x+3y=13$$

and

$$0.002x+0.003y=0.013$$

have identical solution sets. At $(x,y)=(2,3)$, both residuals are zero. At $(0,0)$, the first residual is $-13$ while the second is $-0.013$; the candidate has not become more accurate merely because the raw residual shrank.

For two simultaneous equations, each row can be multiplied by a different nonzero factor without changing their common solution set. However, the determinant of the coefficient matrix changes by the *product* of those factors. Its magnitude alone is not a scale-independent test of numerical reliability.

## 2.8.4 Financial units and model scale

Consider a synthetic one-period sales model:

$$R=pq-F,$$

where $R$ is net revenue in currency units, $p$ is currency units per item, $q$ is number of items, and $F$ is a fixed fee in currency units. Thus $pq$, $F$, and $R$ have matching units.

For $p=12$, $q=100$, and $F=50$, the modeled net revenue is $1150$ currency units. A claimed revenue of $1200$ gives residual

$$12(100)-50-1200=-50\text{ currency units}.$$

If all currency amounts are represented in thousands of currency units, divide $R$, $p$, and $F$ by $1000$ while leaving item quantity $q$ unchanged. The same model then reads

$$1.15=0.012(100)-0.05.$$

A change of units is not a change in economics. But dividing the quantity by $1000$ as well would be a different transformation and would require corresponding changes elsewhere.

A percentage is dimensionless: a rate of 5% is $0.05$, not $5$. Rates, dates, periods, and compounding conventions must be specified before a financial equality can be meaningfully checked.

## 2.8.5 Residuals: necessary checks, not complete guarantees

For $ax+by=c$, define the signed residual

$$r=ax+by-c.$$

An exact solution has $r=0$. A proposed solution with nonzero $r$ fails the equation. For multiple equations, check each residual separately; adding residuals can conceal cancellation.

Raw residual magnitude depends on equation scaling. A simple scale-independent indicator is

$$\eta=\frac{|ax+by-c|}{|ax|+|by|+|c|},$$

when the denominator is nonzero. When all three terms are zero, define $\eta=0$. This is a *relative residual indicator*, not a proof that the computed variables are close to the correct ones.

Indeed, for $\varepsilon x=\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon\ne0$, the exact solution is $x=1$. The candidate $x=0$ has raw residual $-\varepsilon$, which can be arbitrarily small even though its solution error is exactly $1$. Normalizing the residual gives $1$ for this candidate. For more complicated systems, even a small normalized residual need not imply a small solution error: sensitivity to perturbations and conditioning are treated in Chapter 12.

In exact rational arithmetic, zero is exact. In floating-point arithmetic, tolerances should be chosen with scale, representation error, and the intended model in mind. Never replace a mathematical argument with an unexplained global epsilon.

## 2.8.6 Rearrangement and domain validation

Consider a target revenue equation $R=pq-F$. If $p\ne0$, then

$$q=\frac{R+F}{p}.$$

If $p=0$, this division is invalid. The original equation instead becomes $R=-F$: it admits every $q$ if that equality holds, or no $q$ otherwise. A nonnegative-integer sales policy imposes an additional domain restriction even when the algebraic solution is unique.

Validation therefore has three distinct questions:

1. **Algebra:** Was each transformation reversible under its assumptions?
2. **Domain:** Does the candidate meet the required restrictions and units?
3. **Original model:** Does substitution satisfy the original equation?

Passing the third check does not prove the assumptions of the model are economically realistic. It verifies the candidate against the stated model.

## 2.8.7 Python lab: exact checks and scale invariance

The standard-library implementation is [code/section_2_08.py](code/section_2_08.py). It uses `fractions.Fraction` so that strings such as `"0.002"` are interpreted exactly; binary floating-point values and booleans are deliberately rejected. The `scale` function refuses a zero multiplier.

```python
from fractions import Fraction

row = (Fraction(2), Fraction(3), Fraction(13))
point = (Fraction(2), Fraction(3))
r = row[0]*point[0] + row[1]*point[1] - row[2]
assert r == 0

factor = Fraction("0.001")
scaled = tuple(factor * term for term in row)
assert scaled[0]*point[0] + scaled[1]*point[1] - scaled[2] == 0
```


## 2.8.8 Worked validation checklist

Suppose an analyst proposes $q=100$ for $R=1150$, $p=12$, and $F=50$.

| Check | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Units | (currency/item) × items − currency | Currency |
| Original substitution | $12(100)-50$ | $1150$ |
| Signed residual | $1150-1150$ | $0$ |
| Nonnegative quantity | $100\ge0$ | Pass |
| Integer quantity | $100\in\mathbb Z$ | Pass |

This verifies the stated deterministic equation and policy. It does not validate demand forecasts, realized sales, or the assumed fee.

## 2.8.9 Common failure modes

- **Scaling only one side:** $2x=10$ is not equivalent to $0.002x=10$.
- **Multiplying by zero:** the result $0=0$ cannot recover the original restriction.
- **Cancelling an expression that may vanish:** record exclusions before division.
- **Checking a transformed equation only:** introduced roots may survive that check.
- **Comparing raw residuals across different units:** normalize or convert units first.
- **Interpreting a tiny residual as a tiny variable error:** conditioning matters.
- **Ignoring a policy domain:** a negative quantity may satisfy the equation but fail the financial requirement.

## 2.8.10 Exercises

1. Prove that multiplying $5x-10=0$ by $-7$ preserves its solution set. Find the solution and check both equations.
2. Show why multiplying $x=4$ by $x-2$ introduces a new solution. List both roots of the transformed equation.
3. In the model $R=pq-F$, use $p=8$, $F=40$, $R=360$ to solve for $q$. State its units and check the original equation.
4. Evaluate the raw and normalized residuals of $(x,y)=(0,0)$ in $2x+3y=13$, then repeat after scaling by $1/1000$. Explain the difference.
5. Solve $0q=R+F$ for the cases $R=-F$ and $R\ne-F$, with $q$ restricted to nonnegative integers.
6. Construct an example where a candidate has raw residual less than $10^{-8}$ but its variable error is at least $1$. Explain why a small raw residual is insufficient.

### Answers and explanations

1. $5x-10=0$ gives $x=2$. Multiplication yields $-35x+70=0$, also solved by $2$. The multiplier is nonzero, so division recovers the original equation.
2. $x(x-2)=4(x-2)$ rearranges to $(x-4)(x-2)=0$, giving $x=4$ or $x=2$. Only $x=4$ solves the original.
3. $q=(360+40)/8=50$ items. Check: $8(50)-40=360$ currency units.
4. Original: $r=-13$, $\eta=13/13=1$. Scaled: $r=-0.013$, $\eta=0.013/0.013=1$. Raw magnitude changes; the normalized indicator does not.
5. If $R=-F$, every nonnegative integer $q$ satisfies the equation; otherwise no $q$ does.
6. Let $10^{-10}x=10^{-10}$ and propose $x=0$. Residual is $-10^{-10}$ while the exact solution is $1$, an error of $1$.
