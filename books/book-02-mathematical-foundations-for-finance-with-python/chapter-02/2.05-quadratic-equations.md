## 2.5.1 Learning Objectives

After this section, you should be able to put a quadratic equation into standard form, solve suitable examples by factoring or completing the square, use the discriminant and quadratic formula, and distinguish mathematical roots from financially admissible values. You should also be able to check exact rational roots and interpret approximate representations of irrational roots without changing the equation's classification.

Section 2.4 introduced powers and roots. Here those operations solve equations in which the selected unknown appears to the second power. A quadratic can have two distinct real roots, one repeated real root, or no real roots; a solver should preserve those distinctions.

The examples use a synthetic break-even model and a two-period growth equation. Their purpose is algebraic reasoning, not investment selection or a claim that a fitted demand model describes a real market.

## 2.5.2 Recognize Standard Form

A quadratic equation in one unknown has standard form

\[
ax^2+bx+c=0,\qquad a\ne0.
\]

The coefficients are fixed while solving for \(x\). Missing terms have coefficient zero: \(x^2=9\) becomes \(x^2+0x-9=0\). Move every term to the same side and combine like terms before identifying the coefficients.

For example, \(2x^2+3x=5\) has \(a=2,b=3,c=-5\). The constant is negative because five was subtracted from both sides. Reading \(c=5\) would solve a different equation.

If \(a=0\), the equation is not quadratic. It becomes linear when \(b\ne0\), an identity when \(b=c=0\), or inconsistent when \(b=0,c\ne0\). Section 2.2 handles those cases. The lab requires a nonzero quadratic coefficient explicitly rather than dividing by zero in the quadratic formula.

Linearity and degree refer to the chosen unknown. The formula \(A=P(1+r)^2\) is quadratic in rate for fixed nonzero principal, but linear in principal for fixed rate.

## 2.5.3 Solve by Factoring When the Factors Are Visible

If a product of real factors is zero, at least one factor is zero. For

\[
x^2-5x+6=(x-2)(x-3)=0,
\]

the solutions are \(x=2\) and \(x=3\). Substitution into the original polynomial gives zero in each case.

The zero-product property requires a product equal to zero. From \((x-2)(x-3)=6\), you cannot conclude that either factor equals six or zero. Bring the equation to an appropriate zero form before applying the property.

If \(x^2-4x+4=(x-2)^2=0\), the only distinct real solution is 2. It is called a repeated root, with multiplicity two, because the factor occurs twice. A reported tuple containing one value does not mean the polynomial was linear.

Factoring is efficient when the structure is clear. Some quadratics do not factor over the rational numbers, so a systematic method must also support irrational real roots. Lack of obvious integer factors is not evidence that no real roots exist.

## 2.5.4 Complete the Square

Completing the square rewrites a quadratic as a shifted square plus a constant. For example,

\[
x^2-6x+5=0
\iff x^2-6x=-5
\iff (x-3)^2=4.
\]

Both signs must be considered, so \(x-3=\pm2\) and the roots are 1 and 5. The added value was \((6/2)^2=9\), applied to both sides.

The same form can clarify a financial expression. The synthetic profit function used below is

\[
\pi(q)=-0.1q^2+10q-160
=-0.1(q-50)^2+90.
\]

The second form exposes the center at quantity 50 and the value 90 there. Solving \(\pi(q)=0\) gives \((q-50)^2=900\), hence quantities 20 and 80. This is the same equation written to make its structure visible.

Completing the square does not remove domain restrictions. If quantity must be nonnegative, integer-valued, capped, or associated with nonnegative price, apply those conditions to the roots afterward.

## 2.5.5 Derive and Use the Quadratic Formula

For nonzero \(a\), completing the square in the general equation gives

\[
(2ax+b)^2=b^2-4ac.
\]

Define the discriminant \(D=b^2-4ac\). When \(D\geq0\), take both square-root signs and isolate the unknown:

\[
x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt D}{2a}.
\]

The entire numerator is divided by \(2a\). In code, omitting denominator parentheses changes the operation. A negative \(b\) also needs its sign carried correctly through \(-b\).

For \(2x^2+3x-5=0\), \(D=9+40=49\), so

\[
x=\frac{-3\pm7}{4}.
\]

The roots are 1 and \(-5/2\). Both satisfy the original equation over the reals. Whether both are relevant to an application depends on the meaning and domain of \(x\).

Multiplying all coefficients by the same nonzero constant preserves the solution set. It changes the discriminant's numerical scale but not its sign. Do not confuse a small discriminant with an absent one without considering how the equation is represented.

## 2.5.6 Classify Roots with the Discriminant

| Discriminant | Distinct real roots | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| \(D>0\) | Two | Both signs in the formula give different values |
| \(D=0\) | One | Repeated root \(-b/(2a)\) |
| \(D<0\) | Zero | No real square root of the discriminant |

For \(x^2-2=0\), the discriminant is 8 and the roots are \(\pm\sqrt2\), both irrational. For \(x^2+1=0\), the discriminant is −4 and there are no real roots. Complex roots can be studied in a broader number system, but this section's solver deliberately reports only real roots.

With rational coefficients, compute the discriminant exactly before converting anything to an approximation. This preserves the mathematical distinction between positive, zero, and negative values for the supplied coefficients.

An exact sign does not establish that the coefficients themselves are measured without uncertainty. A nearly repeated root can be sensitive to small coefficient changes. That is a model and numerical-analysis issue, separate from exact classification of the given rational equation.

## 2.5.7 Check Roots and Relations Between Them

Always substitute candidate roots into the original polynomial. For an exact rational root \(u\), check \(au^2+bu+c=0\) exactly.

When the two roots are \(u,v\), factoring \(a(x-u)(x-v)\) and comparing coefficients gives

\[
u+v=-b/a,\qquad uv=c/a.
\]

These sum and product relationships offer additional checks. For roots 1 and \(-5/2\), the sum is \(-3/2\) and product is \(-5/2\), matching the coefficients of \(2x^2+3x-5\).

A small approximate residual needs interpretation. The equation multiplied by a very small constant has correspondingly small residuals even for a poor candidate. For numerical checks, compare the residual with a scale such as \(|a||x|^2+|b||x|+|c|\), and state the accuracy policy.

The lab keeps rational roots exact. For irrational roots it retains an exact center-and-spread description and optionally computes Decimal approximations. Those approximate outputs must be checked with a declared precision and tolerance rather than labeled exact because they contain many digits.

## 2.5.8 Formulate a Quadratic Break-Even Model

Suppose continuous quantity \(q\geq0\) has a hypothetical unit price

\[
p(q)=12-0.1q,
\]

and total cost \(C(q)=160+2q\). Price is in currency per unit, so the slope coefficient has the units required to convert quantity into a price change. Revenue is quantity times unit price:

\[
R(q)=q(12-0.1q).
\]

Profit is

\[
\pi(q)=R(q)-C(q)=-0.1q^2+10q-160.
\]

A break-even quantity satisfies \(\pi(q)=0\). Multiplying by −10 gives

\[
q^2-100q+1600=(q-20)(q-80)=0.
\]

The roots are 20 and 80. At 20, price is 10, revenue is 200, and cost is 200. At 80, price is 4, revenue is 320, and cost is 320.

The model also requires nonnegative price, giving \(q\leq120\), so both roots lie in its domain. A separate capacity cap of 60 would exclude 80. The polynomial root remains mathematically correct; the added constraint changes admissibility. This model permits continuously divisible quantity and imposes no transaction increments or rounding rule.

## 2.5.9 Apply a Financial Rate Domain to Two Algebraic Roots

For starting value \(P>0\) and ending value \(A\geq0\) after two fixed periods,

\[
P(1+r)^2=A
\iff Pr^2+2Pr+(P-A)=0.
\]

With \(P=100,A=121\), divide by principal to obtain

\[
r^2+2r-0.21=0.
\]

The roots are 0.10 and −2.10. Both satisfy the polynomial and reconstruct the same endpoint under unrestricted squaring. The positive-growth-factor model, however, requires \(r>-1\). Only 0.10 is admissible; the other root gives growth factor −1.1.

If the target ending value is zero, the repeated root is \(r=-1\). It satisfies the polynomial but is excluded by the strict positive-factor domain. A solver must not replace that excluded root with an allowed rate or silently change the domain.

This example connects the quadratic formula with Section 2.4's root selection. It assumes no intermediate flows and a constant effective period rate. Solving the equation is not evidence that such a rate is available or that every realized period had the same return.

## 2.5.10 Represent Exact and Approximate Roots Explicitly

For rational coefficients and nonnegative discriminant, write

\[
x=h\pm\sqrt{s},\qquad
h=-\frac b{2a},\quad s=\frac D{4a^2}.
\]

Both \(h\) and \(s\) are exact rational values. The lab returns this description. If \(s\) has a rational square root, the roots are returned as exact `Fraction` values. Otherwise `exact_roots=None` means that the exact roots are represented by the center and radical, not that roots are missing.

A nonnegative rational in lowest terms has a rational square root precisely when its numerator and denominator are perfect squares. The implementation tests those integers using `math.isqrt` and verifies their squares exactly.

The optional Decimal evaluator uses a local precision from 16 through 100 significant digits. Decimal square roots of irrational numbers are still approximations. The center-minus-spread calculation can lose significant digits when nearly equal values are subtracted; increasing precision can help particular cases but is not a universal stability guarantee.

This is a transparent teaching implementation for small coefficients. Production root algorithms, cancellation-resistant rearrangements, scaling, and error analysis receive further treatment in the numerical chapters. The exact classification and symbolic description remain separate from the precision of an approximate root.

## 2.5.11 Worked Python Lab — Classify and Represent Real Roots

Save as [code/section_2_05.py](code/section_2_05.py). Coefficients must be integers, rational strings, or `Fraction` values; floats and Boolean values are rejected. The kernel requires \(a\ne0\). It returns distinct roots, so a repeated root appears once.

```python
"""Exact quadratic classification and optional Decimal root approximations."""
from decimal import Decimal, localcontext
from fractions import Fraction
from math import isqrt


def rational(value):
    if type(value) not in (int, str, Fraction):
        raise TypeError("use an integer, rational string, or Fraction")
    return Fraction(value)


def rational_sqrt(value):
    if value < 0:
        raise ValueError("square root requires a nonnegative rational")
    numerator, denominator = isqrt(value.numerator), isqrt(value.denominator)
    if numerator * numerator == value.numerator and denominator * denominator == value.denominator:
        return Fraction(numerator, denominator)
    return None


def solve_quadratic(*, a, b, c):
    a, b, c = map(rational, (a, b, c))
    if a == 0:
        raise ValueError("a must be nonzero; use linear classification otherwise")
    discriminant = b * b - 4 * a * c
    if discriminant < 0:
        return {"kind": "no_real", "discriminant": discriminant,
                "center": None, "spread_squared": None, "exact_roots": ()}
    center = -b / (2 * a)
    spread_squared = discriminant / (4 * a * a)
    spread = rational_sqrt(spread_squared)
    roots = None if spread is None else tuple(sorted({center - spread, center + spread}))
    return {"kind": "repeated" if discriminant == 0 else "two_real",
            "discriminant": discriminant, "center": center,
            "spread_squared": spread_squared, "exact_roots": roots}


def approximate_roots(*, a, b, c, precision=40):
    if type(precision) is not int or not 16 <= precision <= 100:
        raise ValueError("precision must be an integer from 16 to 100")
    result = solve_quadratic(a=a, b=b, c=c)
    def decimal_value(value):
        return Decimal(value.numerator) / Decimal(value.denominator)
    with localcontext() as context:
        context.prec = precision
        if result["exact_roots"] is not None:
            return tuple(decimal_value(root) for root in result["exact_roots"])
        center = decimal_value(result["center"])
        spread = decimal_value(result["spread_squared"]).sqrt()
        return (center - spread, center + spread)


def main():
    factored = solve_quadratic(a=1, b=-5, c=6)
    repeated = solve_quadratic(a=1, b=-4, c=4)
    absent = solve_quadratic(a=1, b=0, c=1)
    print("Factored roots:", ", ".join(map(str, factored["exact_roots"])))
    print("Repeated root:", ", ".join(map(str, repeated["exact_roots"])))
    print("Negative discriminant:", absent["kind"])
    irrational = approximate_roots(a=1, b=0, c=-2)
    print("Irrational root approximations:", ", ".join(f"{x:.6f}" for x in irrational))
    profit = solve_quadratic(a="-0.1", b=10, c=-160)
    print("Break-even quantities:", ", ".join(map(str, profit["exact_roots"])))
    growth = solve_quadratic(a=1, b=2, c="-0.21")
    print("Growth rate candidates:", ", ".join(map(str, growth["exact_roots"])))
    allowed = [rate for rate in growth["exact_roots"] if rate > -1]
    print("Admissible growth rates:", ", ".join(map(str, allowed)))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

```text
Repeated root: 2
Negative discriminant: no_real
Irrational root approximations: -1.414214, 1.414214
Break-even quantities: 20, 80
Growth rate candidates: -21/10, 1/10
Admissible growth rates: 1/10
```

## 2.5.14 Common Mistakes, Summary, and Sources

Common mistakes include misreading a coefficient's sign, applying the zero-product property to a nonzero right side, forgetting one root, treating repeated roots as a linear equation, and calling a negative discriminant “no solutions” without specifying the real number system. Another is accepting both financial roots without checking quantity, capacity, price, or rate restrictions.

Put the equation in standard form, verify that the quadratic coefficient is nonzero, classify the discriminant, and choose a solving method. Substitute roots into the original equation, then apply the financial domain. Preserve exact descriptions of irrational roots separately from their approximate numerical displays.
