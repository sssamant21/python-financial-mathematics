# 2.7 — Simultaneous Equations

**Status:** Draft — executable examples validated; canonical editorial review pending.

[Chapter index](README.md) · [Previous: Inequalities and Absolute Values](2.06-inequalities-and-absolute-values.md) · [Master TOC](../MASTER-TOC.md)

A budget alone rarely determines a financial allocation. If two accounts must receive a total of 1,000 currency units, many allocations satisfy that requirement. A second requirement, such as a specified modeled interest amount over one period, can determine the allocation, contradict the budget, or repeat information already supplied by it. Solving simultaneous equations means finding values that satisfy every equation together.

This section develops two linear equations in two real unknowns. The examples use synthetic, deterministic amounts and exact rational inputs. They teach equation solving and feasibility, without requiring market data or an optimization model. Larger systems, matrices, rank, and numerical conditioning receive fuller treatment in Chapter 12.

## 2.7.1 Learning Objectives

After completing this section, you should be able to:

- Translate two compatible financial requirements into equations with named variables and units.
- Solve a two-variable linear system by substitution and elimination.
- Distinguish a unique solution, an inconsistent system, a solution line, and an unconstrained plane.
- Handle zero coefficients without dividing by zero or discarding an equation.
- Separate an algebraic solution from financial domain restrictions.
- Verify both original equations and preserve exact inputs in a small Python implementation.

Prerequisites are linear rearrangement from Section 2.2, rational arithmetic from Section 2.3, and inequality domains from Section 2.6. Before using a solver, practice identifying whether the unknowns are money amounts, quantities of instruments, or dimensionless weights. A correct algorithm cannot repair a mismatched model specification.

## 2.7.2 What a System Requires

Write a general system as

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_1x+b_1y&=c_1,\\
a_2x+b_2y&=c_2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The six coefficients are fixed inputs; the ordered pair $(x,y)$ is unknown. Each equation defines a set of permissible pairs. The system's solution set is their intersection. Satisfying the first equation alone is insufficient, even if its interpretation is especially important, such as a budget constraint.

For example, consider

$$x+y=10,\qquad 2x-y=2.$$

The pair $(4,6)$ works because $4+6=10$ and $2(4)-6=2$. The pair $(5,5)$ satisfies the first equation but fails the second. Swapping the correct pair to $(6,4)$ also fails: variable labels and order matter.

When an equation has at least one nonzero variable coefficient, its graph is a line. Two such lines can meet once, be distinct and parallel, or coincide. However, the equation $0x+0y=0$ describes the entire plane, while $0x+0y=7$ describes no point. These cases require explicit treatment in software; they cannot all be interpreted as ordinary lines.

## 2.7.3 Substitution: Reduce to One Unknown

From $x+y=10$, obtain $y=10-x$. Substitute this expression into the other equation:

$$2x-(10-x)=2.$$

Thus $3x=12$, so $x=4$ and $y=10-4=6$. Finally, evaluate the two original left sides, rather than checking only the substituted equation.

Substitution works because the first equation permits replacing $y$ by an equal expression wherever that equation applies. Recovering the second unknown is part of the solution, not an optional reporting step. An intermediate value $x=4$ is not yet an ordered-pair answer.

Choose a convenient equation and variable. If a coefficient is $1$ or $-1$, isolating that variable usually avoids unnecessary fractions. If a proposed rearrangement needs division by a coefficient, establish that the coefficient is nonzero first. For $0x+2y=8$, solve for $y$, not for $x$. If substitution ends with $0=0$, investigate a family of solutions; if it ends with $0=3$, report inconsistency.

The words “investigate a family” matter: an identity in the reduced equation does not mean both original variables are arbitrary. A remaining equation can still constrain them to one line.

## 2.7.4 Elimination: Preserve the Shared Solution Set

For the same system, add the two equations:

$$
(x+y)+(2x-y)=10+2,
$$

which gives $3x=12$. Recover $y$ from an original equation. If the coefficients do not already cancel, multiply one or both equations by suitable nonzero constants before adding or subtracting.

For instance,

$$2x+3y=19,\qquad 4x-y=17.$$

Subtract twice the first equation from the second:

$$-7y=-21.$$

Therefore $y=3$ and $x=5$. Checks are $2(5)+3(3)=19$ and $4(5)-3=17$.

The safe operation replaces one equation by that equation plus a multiple of the other, while retaining the other equation. Keeping only the combined equation loses information. In this example, $y=3$ by itself allows any $x$, although the original system permits only $x=5$.

Multiplying an equation by zero is also unsafe as a replacement operation: it turns every equation into $0=0$ and erases its restriction. Multiplication by a nonzero scalar preserves the equation's solutions. This distinction will become useful when checking implementations by rescaling their inputs.

## 2.7.5 Derive the Unique-Solution Formula

Eliminate $y$ from the general system by multiplying the first equation by $b_2$ and the second by $b_1$, then subtracting:

$$(a_1b_2-a_2b_1)x=c_1b_2-c_2b_1.$$

Define the two-by-two determinant

$$D=a_1b_2-a_2b_1.$$

If $D\ne0$, division is valid and

$$
x=\frac{c_1b_2-c_2b_1}{D},\qquad
y=\frac{a_1c_2-a_2c_1}{D}.
$$

The second formula follows by eliminating $x$. The recovered pair satisfies both equations, and elimination shows that any solution must have those same two coordinates. Existence and uniqueness follow together.

A zero coefficient is not automatically a problem. The system $2y=8$, $3x=9$ has $D=-6$ and the unique solution $(3,4)$. What matters for these formulas is the combined expression $D$, not whether every individual coefficient is nonzero.

If $D=0$, these formulas must not be evaluated. A zero denominator signals that classification needs another branch; it does not distinguish no solution from infinitely many solutions. A determinant alone is therefore an incomplete solver.

## 2.7.6 Classify Singular and Degenerate Cases

First examine any row with both variable coefficients zero. If its constant is nonzero, the entire system is inconsistent. If its constant is zero, the row adds no restriction and may be ignored when describing the remaining solutions.

If both rows add no restriction, every real pair is a solution. Otherwise select a row $(a,b,c)$ with at least one nonzero coefficient. With $D=0$, consistency additionally requires

$$ac_2=a_2c,\qquad bc_2=b_2c,$$

where $(a_2,b_2,c_2)$ here denotes the *other* row. These multiplication checks avoid ratios such as $a/a_2$, which may be undefined. If either equality fails, there is no solution. If both hold, the other equation is redundant and the selected row describes the whole solution line.

Compare:

| Equations | Classification | Solution description |
|---|---|---|
| $x+y=10$, $2x+2y=20$ | Infinite line | $x=t$, $y=10-t$ |
| $x+y=10$, $2x+2y=21$ | None | Contradictory totals |
| $0=0$, $2y=8$ | Infinite line | $x=t$, $y=4$ |
| $0=0$, $0=0$ | All pairs | $x=s$, $y=t$ |
| $0=1$, $x+y=10$ | None | First equation impossible |

The parameters range over all real numbers unless a separate domain restricts them. “Infinite” must retain a mathematical description of the set; returning one convenient pair would conceal the unresolved freedom.

## 2.7.7 Describe a Line Without Losing Solutions

A convenient representation is

$$(x,y)=(x_0,y_0)+t(d_x,d_y),\qquad t\in\mathbb R.$$

For a nonzero row $ax+by=c$, choose

$$
(x_0,y_0)=
\begin{cases}
(0,c/b),&b\ne0,\\
(c/a,0),&b=0,
\end{cases}
\qquad(d_x,d_y)=(b,-a).
$$

The base point satisfies the row, and

$$a(b)+b(-a)=0,$$

so moving along the direction does not change its left side. The direction is nonzero because the row is nonzero. Every point on the line is reached: when $b\ne0$, its first coordinate determines $t=x/b$; when $b=0$, the free second coordinate determines $t=-y/a$.

For $x+y=10$, this construction gives $(0,10)+t(1,-1)$. For $2y=8$, it gives $(0,4)+t(2,0)$. The second representation still covers every real $x$; the parameter does not need to equal a coordinate. Different valid parameterizations may describe the same set, so tests should examine membership rather than demand one arbitrary direction normalization.

## 2.7.8 Financial Example: Budget and Interest Target

Let $x$ and $y$ be currency amounts placed in two hypothetical accounts for the same single period. Their modeled interest rates are 4% and 6%. Assume no fees, taxes, defaults, intermediate flows, or compounding within the period. Require a starting allocation of 1,000 and modeled interest of 52:

$$x+y=1000,\qquad 0.04x+0.06y=52.$$

The right side of the second equation is interest, not ending wealth. Ending wealth would be $1052$ under these assumptions. Substitute $y=1000-x$:

$$0.04x+0.06(1000-x)=52,$$

so $-0.02x=-8$, giving $x=400$ and $y=600$. Independent checks are

$$400+600=1000,\qquad 0.04(400)+0.06(600)=16+36=52.$$

Both amounts are nonnegative, so the pair also satisfies a no-borrowing allocation policy.

For a total budget $B>0$, rates $r_1\ne r_2$, and interest target $I$, substitution gives

$$x=\frac{I-r_2B}{r_1-r_2},\qquad y=B-x.$$

With nonnegative allocations, $I/B$ is a weighted average of the two rates. Therefore the necessary and sufficient feasibility condition is

$$\min(r_1,r_2)B\le I\le\max(r_1,r_2)B.$$

The endpoint targets allocate everything to one account. This feasibility statement also holds for negative modeled rates; it follows from the linear model and nonnegative weights, without assuming that rates are positive.

## 2.7.9 Algebraic Existence versus Financial Feasibility

Raise the interest target to 70 while retaining the budget and rates. The unique algebraic solution becomes $x=-500$, $y=1500$. Both equations hold, but the nonnegative allocation policy fails. The two equations are consistent over the real numbers; the financially permitted solution set is empty. These are distinct conclusions and should be reported separately.

If both rates equal 5%, the interest equation becomes

$$0.05x+0.05y=I.$$

A target of 50 repeats the budget information, so every $(x,1000-x)$ solves the equations. Nonnegativity restricts this line to $0\le x\le1000$. A target of 52 is inconsistent even before the policy is applied.

A zero budget is another boundary case. With nonnegative amounts, $x+y=0$ forces $x=y=0$. If equal rates and a zero target make the unrestricted system a line, the financial feasible set is nevertheless a single point. The lab preserves this distinction explicitly.

These examples establish feasibility only. Choosing a preferred point on a feasible segment would require another objective or policy. Equation solving cannot identify a uniquely “best” allocation when the model leaves many possibilities.

## 2.7.10 Units, Labels, and Residual Checks

If $x,y$ represent currency amounts, the budget equation is $x+y=B$. If instead $u,v$ represent instrument quantities with prices $p_1,p_2$, the budget is $p_1u+p_2v=B$. Substituting quantities into an amount-based equation changes the problem. Likewise, using percentage inputs $4$ and $6$ where decimal rates $0.04$ and $0.06$ are expected multiplies the interest coefficients by 100.

For a proposed pair, compute the original residuals

$$e_1=a_1x+b_1y-c_1,\qquad e_2=a_2x+b_2y-c_2.$$

Both must be zero in exact arithmetic. Keep their units separate: in the allocation model they represent a budget discrepancy and an interest discrepancy. A single summed residual is unsafe because two nonzero errors could cancel.

Exact rational arithmetic makes zero comparisons meaningful for this small lab. It does not establish that modeled rates or financial assumptions are correct. With measured floating-point inputs, singularity and residual tolerances require additional analysis. A small nonzero determinant can accompany large changes in solutions when inputs change. Rescaling equations can also change the determinant's magnitude without changing their solution set, so a universal raw determinant cutoff is inappropriate. Section 2.8 begins scaling checks; Chapter 12 develops conditioning.

## 2.7.11 Python Lab: Exact Classification and Allocation

Save the following as [code/section_2_07.py](code/section_2_07.py). It accepts integers, rational strings, and `Fraction` objects; booleans and floats are rejected. Decimal strings such as `".04"` express the intended rational value directly. Each row has exactly three entries `(a, b, c)` representing $ax+by=c$.

`solve_system` reports `unique`, `none`, `line`, or `all_pairs`. A line retains its base and direction. `allocation` adds a nonnegative-budget requirement and distinguishes a unique feasible pair, an empty set, and a segment. The equal-rate segment is reported through the free first allocation and the rule $y=B-x$; no general inequality solver is implied.

```python
"""Exact two-equation systems and synthetic one-period allocation."""
from fractions import Fraction


def rational(value):
    if type(value) not in (int, str, Fraction):
        raise TypeError("use int, rational string, or Fraction")
    return Fraction(value)


def row(values):
    values = tuple(values)
    if len(values) != 3:
        raise ValueError("a row must contain a, b, c")
    return tuple(rational(value) for value in values)


def residuals(first, second, point):
    first, second = row(first), row(second)
    point = tuple(point)
    if len(point) != 2:
        raise ValueError("a point must contain x, y")
    x, y = map(rational, point)
    return tuple(a*x + b*y - c for a, b, c in (first, second))


def solve_system(first, second):
    a, b, c = row(first)
    d, e, f = row(second)
    determinant = a*e - d*b
    if determinant:
        point = ((c*e - f*b)/determinant,
                 (a*f - d*c)/determinant)
        return {"kind": "unique", "point": point}
    if (a == b == 0 and c != 0) or (d == e == 0 and f != 0):
        return {"kind": "none"}
    if a == b == d == e == 0:
        return {"kind": "all_pairs"}
    if a == b == 0:
        a, b, c, d, e, f = d, e, f, a, b, c
    if a*f != d*c or b*f != e*c:
        return {"kind": "none"}
    zero = Fraction(0)
    base = (zero, c/b) if b else (c/a, zero)
    return {"kind": "line", "base": base, "direction": (b, -a)}


def allocation(*, budget, rate_one, rate_two, interest):
    budget, r1, r2, interest = map(
        rational, (budget, rate_one, rate_two, interest))
    if budget < 0:
        raise ValueError("nonnegative allocation requires budget >= 0")
    first, second = (1, 1, budget), (r1, r2, interest)
    algebraic = solve_system(first, second)
    if algebraic["kind"] == "unique":
        point = algebraic["point"]
        feasible = "unique" if all(value >= 0 for value in point) else "empty"
        return {"algebraic": algebraic, "feasible": feasible,
                "residuals": residuals(first, second, point)}
    if algebraic["kind"] == "none":
        return {"algebraic": algebraic, "feasible": "empty"}
    # The nonzero budget row means the remaining case is a line.
    if budget == 0:
        return {"algebraic": algebraic, "feasible": "unique",
                "point": (Fraction(0), Fraction(0))}
    return {"algebraic": algebraic, "feasible": "segment",
            "x_interval": (Fraction(0), budget), "y_rule": "budget - x"}


def show(point):
    return "(" + ", ".join(str(value) for value in point) + ")"


def main():
    unique = solve_system((1, 1, 10), (2, -1, 2))
    print("Unique pair:", show(unique["point"]))
    line = solve_system((1, 1, 10), (2, 2, 20))
    print("Line: base", show(line["base"]),
          "direction", show(line["direction"]))
    print("Contradictory:", solve_system((1, 1, 10), (2, 2, 21))["kind"])
    print("Zero equations:", solve_system((0, 0, 0), (0, 0, 0))["kind"])
    for target in (52, 70):
        result = allocation(budget=1000, rate_one=".04",
                            rate_two=".06", interest=target)
        print(f"Interest {target}: {show(result['algebraic']['point'])}; "
              f"feasible={result['feasible']}; "
              f"residuals={show(result['residuals'])}")
    equal = allocation(budget=1000, rate_one=".05",
                       rate_two=".05", interest=50)
    print("Equal rates: feasible=" + equal["feasible"] +
          "; x in " + show(equal["x_interval"]) + "; y = budget - x")
    zero = allocation(budget=0, rate_one=".05", rate_two=".05", interest=0)
    print("Zero budget: algebraic=" + zero["algebraic"]["kind"] +
          "; feasible=" + zero["feasible"] + "; point=" + show(zero["point"]))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

## 2.7.12 Exercises

1. **Substitution.** Solve $x+y=18$, $3x-y=10$. Check both equations.
2. **Elimination and units.** Let $u,v$ be instrument quantities. Solve $20u+50v=700$ and $2u+v=34$. The first equation is a currency budget; the second is an abstract exposure requirement in compatible exposure units. Are fractional quantities needed?
3. **Singular systems.** Classify each pair: (a) $3x-6y=9$, $x-2y=3$; (b) $3x-6y=9$, $x-2y=4$; (c) $0=0$, $0=0$; (d) $0=2$, $x+y=1$. Parameterize any solution line.
4. **Feasible allocation.** Allocate 2,000 between modeled rates 3% and 7% for a single period, targeting interest of 100. Find the amounts and the full feasible range of interest targets under nonnegativity. What happens at target 150?
5. **Equal rates and a policy.** Both modeled rates are 4%, the budget is 500, and interest is 20. Describe the unrestricted solution set, then impose $x,y\ge0$ and $x\le200$. What if the interest target changes to 21?
6. **A zero row and invariance.** Solve $0x+2y=8$ with $0=0$. Explain why $x$ remains free. Multiply the first equation by $-3$, then swap the equation order. Should the solution set change? Give a point that fails the system and its residuals in the original row order.

## 2.7.13 Worked Answers

1. Substitute $y=18-x$ into $3x-y=10$: $4x-18=10$, so $x=7$ and $y=11$. The checks are $7+11=18$ and $21-11=10$.
2. From the exposure equation, $v=34-2u$. Substitution gives $20u+1700-100u=700$, hence $u=12.5$ and $v=9$. The budget is $250+450=700$; exposure is $25+9=34$. Fractional units of the first instrument are required. If an instrument must be held in whole units, the unique real solution fails that additional domain. Rounding it does not preserve both equations.
3. (a) The first equation is three times the second, so the solution line is $x=3+2t$, $y=t$, for real $t$. (b) Multiplying the second equation by three would require the same left side to equal 12 rather than 9; no solution. (c) Every real pair solves the system, requiring two free parameters. (d) No solution because $0=2$ is impossible regardless of the other equation.
4. With $y=2000-x$, $0.03x+0.07(2000-x)=100$ gives $x=1000$, $y=1000$. Budget and interest checks give 2,000 and $30+70=100$. Feasible interest is $[60,140]$, including both endpoints. A target of 150 gives $x=-250$, $y=2250$: algebraically unique, financially infeasible under nonnegativity.
5. Interest of 20 repeats $0.04(x+y)=20$. The unrestricted line is $(x,y)=(t,500-t)$ for real $t$. Nonnegativity gives $t\in[0,500]$; the added cap gives $t\in[0,200]$. The target of 21 contradicts the budget, so no solution exists even before policy restrictions.
6. The solution is $y=4$, $x$ arbitrary. One parameterization is $(0,4)+t(2,0)$. Nonzero row scaling and swapping preserve exactly the original restrictions. The point $(3,5)$ fails: original residuals are $(2,0)$. The transformed first nontrivial residual would be $-6$, illustrating that the numerical size of a residual changes with equation scaling even when its zero/nonzero conclusion is preserved.

## 2.7.14 Common Mistakes, References, and Next Step

Common mistakes include checking only the budget, dividing by a zero determinant, treating two proportional left sides as automatically consistent, and choosing one point from an infinite family without reporting the family. Another mistake is interpreting a negative allocation as solver failure: the solver may have correctly exposed an infeasible policy. Preserve the distinction between equations, their real solution set, and any additional financial domain.

The working sequence is: define unknowns and units, write both original equations, solve or classify, retain free parameters when needed, impose the domain, and check original residuals. Exact arithmetic makes this sequence transparent for small rational systems, while leaving numerical and model-risk questions for their appropriate later treatment.
