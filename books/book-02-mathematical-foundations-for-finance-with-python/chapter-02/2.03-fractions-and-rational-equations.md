## 2.3.1 Learning Objectives

After this section, you should be able to combine rational expressions using valid fraction rules, identify all original denominator exclusions, clear denominators without losing those exclusions, and reject candidates that do not satisfy the original equation. You should also be able to classify a simple financial ratio target and distinguish an algebraic solution from an admissible positive principal.

Sections 2.1–2.2 developed equivalent expressions and linear rearrangements. An unknown in a denominator introduces another responsibility: determine where the original expressions exist before transforming them. A simplified expression or cleared equation can have a larger domain than the problem you started with.

Our financial example asks which principal produces a specified effective fee fraction under a fixed-plus-proportional fee model. The lab solves that model through a restricted family of rational equations whose cleared form is linear. It is not a general symbolic equation solver.

## 2.3.2 Read a Fraction as a Whole Quotient

A rational expression is a quotient of polynomials, with a denominator required to be nonzero. Examples include

\[
\frac{F+kP}{P},\qquad \frac{2x+3}{x-1},\qquad \frac{x^2-9}{x-3}.
\]

The entire numerator is divided by the entire denominator. Thus \((F+kP)/P\) means \(F/P+k\), not \(F+k\). Its original mathematical domain excludes \(P=0\); the financial model will use the stronger condition \(P>0\).

An expression is not yet an equation. Evaluating a fee fraction at a given principal is a calculation. Equating that fraction to a specified target introduces an equation whose solutions must be found and checked.

The denominator restriction remains meaningful when the numerator is also zero. The quotient \(0/0\) is undefined, not zero and not one. A numerator equal to zero gives a zero quotient only when the denominator is nonzero.

## 2.3.3 Multiply and Divide with Domain Conditions

For nonzero denominators,

\[
\frac ab\frac cd=\frac{ac}{bd},\qquad b\ne0,\ d\ne0.
\]

To divide by \(c/d\), the divisor must also be nonzero:

\[
\frac{a/b}{c/d}=\frac{ad}{bc},
\qquad b\ne0,\ d\ne0,\ c\ne0.
\]

The condition \(d\ne0\) comes from the original divisor even when \(d\) moves to the numerator of the simplified expression. Simplification does not erase that original exclusion.

For example, with positive principal and \(r>-1\),

\[
\frac{F/P+k}{1+r}
=\frac{F+kP}{P(1+r)}.
\]

Both \(P\) and \(1+r\) must be nonzero. The financial assumptions make them positive, but the algebraic rule itself needs only nonzero denominators.

Check units as well as arithmetic. A fee divided by principal is dimensionless. A ratio involving a rate or a currency amount needs a definition before it acquires a financial interpretation; fraction manipulation cannot supply that definition.

## 2.3.4 Add and Subtract Through a Common Denominator

When denominators agree, combine numerators:

\[
\frac ab+\frac cb=\frac{a+c}{b},\qquad b\ne0.
\]

For different nonzero denominators,

\[
\frac ab+\frac cd=\frac{ad+bc}{bd}.
\]

For example, \(1/3+1/4=7/12\), not \(2/7\). Adding denominators does not preserve the original quantities.

With symbolic denominators, find a common denominator and retain every original exclusion. For

\[
\frac1x+\frac1{x+2},
\]

exclude \(x=0,-2\). Combining gives

\[
\frac{x+2+x}{x(x+2)}=\frac{2x+2}{x(x+2)}.
\]

A product of denominators is a valid common denominator, though it may contain unnecessary repeated factors. If denominators already share factors, a least common denominator can shorten the calculation. For instance, denominators \(x\) and \(x(x+2)\) need only \(x(x+2)\), with the same exclusions.

For subtraction, group the complete second numerator before removing parentheses. The expression \(a/b-(c+d)/b\) becomes \((a-c-d)/b\), not \((a-c+d)/b\).

## 2.3.5 Simplify Factors Without Enlarging the Original Domain

Factor before canceling:

\[
\frac{x^2-9}{x-3}
=\frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3}=x+3,
\qquad x\ne3.
\]

The simplified formula agrees with the quotient at every permitted input. It also has a value at 3, but that value is not an evaluation of the original quotient there. Defining such a value would extend the domain.

Canceling selected pieces of a sum is invalid. In \((F+kP)/P\), division distributes across the numerator, giving \(F/P+k\). It does not remove principal from a term where it was not a factor of the complete term.

For a rational equation, preserve exclusions before any cancellation. If an apparent solution later equals an excluded input, reject it even when the simplified expression appears harmless. The problem is defined by the original expression, not by whichever form is most convenient at the final step.

## 2.3.6 Clear Denominators on the Allowed Domain

A reliable procedure is to identify the exclusions, multiply both sides by a common denominator, solve the resulting equation, and then test every candidate against the exclusions and original equation.

Consider

\[
\frac{2x+3}{x-1}=5,\qquad x\ne1.
\]

On this domain, multiplying by \(x-1\) is reversible:

\[
2x+3=5(x-1)
\iff 2x+3=5x-5
\iff x=\frac83.
\]

The candidate is not excluded. Substitution gives numerator \(25/3\), denominator \(5/3\), and quotient 5.

An unknown denominator can have either sign. For an equation, multiplication by a nonzero denominator preserves equality regardless of that sign. Inequalities require additional sign analysis; do not transfer equation-clearing rules to inequalities without it. Section 2.6 develops that topic.

Clearing denominators can produce a polynomial equation of higher degree. This section handles examples reducible to linear equations; quadratic methods arrive in Section 2.5.

## 2.3.7 Understand an Excluded Candidate

Consider

\[
\frac{x+1}{x-1}=\frac2{x-1},\qquad x\ne1.
\]

Clearing the denominator yields \(x+1=2\), so the transformed equation has candidate \(x=1\). But both original sides are undefined there. The original equation has no solution.

Subtracting the right side first gives the equivalent allowed-domain equation

\[
\frac{x-1}{x-1}=0,\qquad x\ne1.
\]

Its left side equals 1 at every allowed input, making the contradiction visible. The candidate at the hole belongs only to the unrestricted cleared equation.

It is common to call this an extraneous candidate or extraneous solution from the clearing step. It was never a solution of the original rational equation. There is no original-equation residual to calculate at that point because the expression is undefined. Report the domain rejection instead of substituting zero for an undefined residual.

## 2.3.8 Formulate an Effective Fee Target

Let a positive principal \(P\) incur total fee \(F+kP\), with \(F,k\geq0\). Define effective fee fraction

\[
e(P)=\frac{F+kP}{P}=k+\frac FP.
\]

Ask which principal gives a target fraction \(q\geq0\):

\[
\frac{F+kP}{P}=q,\qquad P>0.
\]

Here \(F\) and principal share one currency, while \(k\) and \(q\) are dimensionless decimal fractions. A 2% target is \(q=0.02\), not 2.

Multiplying by permitted positive principal gives

\[
F+kP=qP
\iff(q-k)P=F.
\]

If \(q\ne k\), the cleared candidate is \(P=F/(q-k)\). It must still be nonzero and positive. For \(F=5,k=0.01,q=0.02\), principal is 500. Total fee is 10, and \(10/500=0.02\), verifying the original ratio.

This is a target equality. It does not ask for all principals whose fee fraction is below the target, and it does not automatically impose a minimum transaction increment or rounding rule.

## 2.3.9 Classify All Fee-Target Branches

The parameters determine both the rational solution set and the financial admissibility:

| Parameters | Original rational equation over \(P\ne0\) | Positive-principal result |
|---|---|---|
| \(F>0,q>k\) | One positive solution | Admissible |
| \(F>0,q=k\) | No solution | None |
| \(F>0,q<k\) | One negative solution | Inadmissible |
| \(F=0,q=k\) | Every nonzero real principal | Every positive principal |
| \(F=0,q\ne k\) | No solution; cleared candidate is zero | None |

When \(F>0\), the effective fee fraction \(k+F/P\) exceeds \(k\) at every positive finite principal. Thus target \(q=k\) cannot be attained exactly, even though the difference can become small for large principal. A numerical closeness test would answer a different question.

When \(F=0\), the effective fraction is the constant \(k\) on its domain. If the target differs, clearing produces zero principal, which is excluded. This branch demonstrates why an algebraic candidate is not enough.

These classifications describe the declared fee formula. Real contractual fee tiers, rebates, rounding, or limits would require a new specification rather than an unexplained adjustment to the solution.

## 2.3.10 A Restricted Rational Solver and Its Evidence

The lab solves equations of the form

\[
\frac{ax+b}{cx+d}=t.
\]

On the original domain \(cx+d\ne0\), clearing gives

\[
(a-tc)x=td-b.
\]

Use Section 2.2's three-way linear classification, then test any candidate in the original denominator. If \(c=d=0\), the original expression is undefined everywhere and its solution set is empty, even if the cleared equation resembles an identity.

When the cleared equation is an identity and the domain is nonempty, every input in that original domain solves it. `kind="all"` therefore means all permitted inputs, not permission to reintroduce a pole. A unique candidate rejected at a pole is preserved for diagnosis while `solution` remains null.

The financial wrapper applies \(P>0\) separately. A negative rational solution can be perfectly valid for the equation over nonzero real inputs yet inadmissible as a principal. Exact fractions allow the code to classify these cases without a tolerance that hides a zero denominator.

## 2.3.11 Worked Python Lab — Keep the Pole and Check the Candidate

Save as [code/section_2_03.py](code/section_2_03.py). Inputs use integers, rational strings, or `Fraction` values. Floats and Boolean values are rejected. `excluded` records a finite pole when present; `domain_empty=True` handles an identically zero denominator separately.

```python
"""Restricted exact rational equations and positive-principal fee targets."""
from fractions import Fraction


def rational(value):
    if type(value) not in (int, str, Fraction):
        raise TypeError("use an integer, rational string, or Fraction")
    return Fraction(value)


def solve_ratio(*, a, b, c, d, target):
    a, b, c, d, t = map(rational, (a, b, c, d, target))
    result = {"candidate": None, "solution": None, "residual": None,
              "excluded": () if c == 0 else (-d / c,),
              "domain_empty": c == 0 and d == 0}
    if result["domain_empty"]:
        return {**result, "kind": "none", "reason": "undefined_for_all_inputs"}
    coefficient, rhs = a - t * c, t * d - b
    if coefficient == 0:
        kind = "all" if rhs == 0 else "none"
        return {**result, "kind": kind, "reason": "cleared_" + kind}
    x = rhs / coefficient
    result["candidate"] = x
    denominator = c * x + d
    if denominator == 0:
        return {**result, "kind": "none", "reason": "candidate_at_excluded_pole"}
    residual = (a * x + b) / denominator - t
    return {**result, "kind": "unique", "solution": x,
            "residual": residual, "reason": "checked_original_equation"}


def principal_for_fee_target(*, fixed_fee, fee_rate, target_fraction):
    fixed, k, q = map(rational, (fixed_fee, fee_rate, target_fraction))
    if fixed < 0 or k < 0 or q < 0:
        raise ValueError("fee parameters and target fraction must be nonnegative")
    result = solve_ratio(a=k, b=fixed, c=1, d=0, target=q)
    p = result["solution"]
    admissible = result["kind"] == "all" or (p is not None and p > 0)
    return {**result, "admissible": admissible,
            "financial_domain": "positive real principal"}


def main():
    valid = solve_ratio(a=2, b=3, c=1, d=-1, target=5)
    hole = solve_ratio(a=1, b=-1, c=1, d=-1, target=0)
    print(f"Valid ratio root: {valid['solution']}, residual={valid['residual']}")
    print(f"Excluded candidate: {hole['candidate']}, kind={hole['kind']}")
    fee = principal_for_fee_target(fixed_fee=5, fee_rate="0.01", target_fraction="0.02")
    same = principal_for_fee_target(fixed_fee=0, fee_rate="0.01", target_fraction="0.01")
    zero = principal_for_fee_target(fixed_fee=0, fee_rate="0.01", target_fraction="0.02")
    negative = principal_for_fee_target(fixed_fee=5, fee_rate="0.01", target_fraction="0.005")
    print(f"Fee target principal: {fee['solution']}, admissible={fee['admissible']}")
    print(f"Zero fixed fee, matching target: {same['kind']}")
    print(f"Zero fixed fee, other target: {zero['kind']}, candidate={zero['candidate']}")
    print(f"Negative ratio root: {negative['solution']}, admissible={negative['admissible']}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

```text

Excluded candidate: 1, kind=none
Fee target principal: 500, admissible=True
Zero fixed fee, matching target: all
Zero fixed fee, other target: none, candidate=0
Negative ratio root: -1000, admissible=False
```

## 2.3.12 Practice Problems

1. Combine \(1/x+2/(x+3)\), stating all exclusions. Evaluate the original sum and combined expression at \(x=3\).
2. Simplify \((x^2-16)/(x-4)\) with its original domain. Solve the equation \((x^2-16)/(x-4)=8\). Explain what happens to the cleared candidate.
3. Solve \((3x+2)/(x-2)=4\), retain the exclusion, and substitute into the original ratio.
4. Solve \((x+2)/(x-1)=3/(x-1)\). Explain why the transformed linear equation does not supply an original solution.
5. Under the fee-target model, use \(F=6,k=0.01,q=0.025\). Find principal and total fee. Classify the same target problem when \(q=0.01\) and when \(q=0.005\).
6. Let \(F=0,k=0.02\). Classify positive-principal solutions for targets \(q=0.02\) and \(q=0.03\). Explain why the zero candidate in the second case is inadmissible even though the cleared equation holds there.

## 2.3.13 Worked Answers

1. Exclude \(x=0,-3\). A common denominator gives \((x+3+2x)/(x(x+3))=(3x+3)/(x(x+3))\). At 3 the original sum is \(1/3+2/6=2/3\), and the combined expression is \(12/18=2/3\).
2. Factoring gives \(x+4\) for \(x\ne4\). The simplified equation gives candidate 4, which the original quotient excludes, so the original equation has no solution. Clearing directly gives \((x-4)^2=0\), with the same rejected candidate.
3. Exclude 2. Clearing gives \(3x+2=4x-8\), so \(x=10\). The original ratio is \(32/8=4\), so the candidate is accepted.
4. Exclude 1. Clearing gives \(x+2=3\), hence candidate 1. Both original sides are undefined there, so there is no solution. On the original domain the difference of the two sides is identically 1.
5. Principal is \(6/(0.025-0.01)=400\). Total fee is \(6+0.01(400)=10\), and \(10/400=0.025\). At target 0.01 there is no solution because a positive fixed fee keeps the ratio above the proportional fraction for positive principal, and the cleared equation is inconsistent. At target 0.005 the rational root is −1200; it is excluded by the financial domain \(P>0\).
6. At target 0.02 every positive principal works. At target 0.03 the cleared equation yields zero principal, but the original ratio is undefined there and equals 0.02 at every nonzero input. The rational equation therefore has no solution, not a valid zero-principal answer.

## 2.3.14 Common Mistakes, Summary, and Sources

Common mistakes include adding denominators, losing a subtraction sign, canceling terms instead of factors, forgetting a divisor must be nonzero, and accepting a cleared candidate at an excluded input. Another is interpreting `all` without retaining the original domain, or treating an inadmissible negative principal as a numerical calculation error.

Record exclusions first, combine or clear fractions with justified operations, solve the transformed equation, and check the original expressions. Apply any stronger financial restrictions afterward. An undefined original expression has no valid residual to replace with zero.
