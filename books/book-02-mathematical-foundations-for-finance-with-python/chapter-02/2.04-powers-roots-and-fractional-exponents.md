## 2.4.1 Learning Objectives

After this section, you should be able to interpret positive, zero, and negative integer exponents; apply power rules on stated domains; distinguish a principal root from all solutions of a power equation; and interpret rational exponents for positive bases. You should also be able to recover a constant equivalent period rate from positive starting and ending values and check the result by substitution.

Earlier sections solved linear and restricted rational equations. An unknown inside a power needs another operation: taking a root. The root's domain and branch are part of the mathematics, just as denominator exclusions were in Section 2.3.

The financial examples use fixed-length periods, positive balances, and no intervening contributions, withdrawals, or fees. This section introduces the algebraic rate inference; Chapter 04 develops compounding conventions and conversions more fully.

## 2.4.2 Positive Integer Powers

For a positive integer \(n\), \(a^n\) means the product of \(n\) copies of \(a\). Thus \(a^3=a\times a\times a\). The base is the whole grouped expression immediately under the exponent: \((1+r)^3\) multiplies three copies of \(1+r\), not three copies of \(r\) followed by adding one.

Signs depend on grouping. For example, \((-2)^4=16\), while \(-2^4=-(2^4)=-16\) under the usual precedence convention. An even power of a negative real number is positive; an odd power is negative.

In a fixed-rate growth model,

\[
A_n=P(1+r)^n,
\]

principal is multiplied by one growth factor for each period. This is different from \((P(1+r))^n\), which also raises principal to the power \(n\). The period count and effective rate must refer to matching periods.

An exponent counts repeated multiplication; it does not distribute over addition. Generally, \((a+b)^n\ne a^n+b^n\). The cross terms from Section 2.1 explain the difference already for \(n=2\).

## 2.4.3 Zero and Negative Integer Exponents

For \(a\ne0\), define \(a^0=1\). This makes the exponent rule \(a^{m}/a^{m}=a^{m-m}\) consistent with a quotient equal to one. For positive integer \(n\),

\[
a^{-n}=\frac1{a^n},\qquad a\ne0.
\]

A negative exponent indicates a reciprocal, not a negative result. For example, \(2^{-3}=1/8\), not −8. At growth factor \(1.05=21/20\), the two-period discount factor is

\[
(1.05)^{-2}=\left(\frac{20}{21}\right)^2=\frac{400}{441}.
\]

Zero raised to a positive integer is zero. Zero raised to a negative integer would require division by zero and is excluded. The expression \(0^0\) is convention-dependent across contexts; this section's exact-power interface excludes it explicitly. Python itself returns 1 for `0 ** 0`, so the interface must enforce its own stated policy.

Discount factors are dimensionless. Multiplying a future currency amount by an appropriate factor preserves currency units. Whether that factor is financially appropriate depends on the model and timing assumptions, not just the exponent arithmetic.

## 2.4.4 Power Rules and Their Domains

For positive real bases, the following rules apply to rational exponents, including integer exponents:

\[
a^u a^v=a^{u+v},\qquad
\frac{a^u}{a^v}=a^{u-v},\qquad
(a^u)^v=a^{uv}.
\]

For positive \(a,b\),

\[
(ab)^u=a^u b^u,\qquad
(a/b)^u=a^u/b^u.
\]

The positive-base restriction gives a consistent real-valued setting for all the rational powers used here. Broader integer-power identities can include negative bases, provided zero is excluded whenever a reciprocal occurs. Do not extend every rule to arbitrary negative bases and fractional exponents without checking definitions.

As a small example, \(a^3a^{-1}=a^2\) for nonzero \(a\). But \(a^3+a^{-1}\) is a sum of terms, so it does not simplify by adding exponents. Likewise, \((a^3)^2=a^6\), while \(a^{3^2}=a^9\). Parentheses identify which operation is being composed.

These are exact mathematical identities. Floating-point evaluation may differ in intermediate rounding or supported range, so a symbolic identity alone is not a claim about identical machine results for every input.

## 2.4.5 Principal Roots and Power-Equation Solutions

An \(n\)th root of \(b\) is a number \(x\) satisfying \(x^n=b\), where \(n\) is a positive integer. Over the reals, parity matters:

| Index | Radicand | Real roots of \(x^n=b\) |
|---|---|---|
| Even | Positive | One positive and one negative root |
| Even | Zero | Zero |
| Even | Negative | None |
| Odd | Any real value | Exactly one real root |

For a nonnegative radicand and even index, radical notation denotes the **principal nonnegative root**. Thus \(\sqrt{9}=3\), while the equation \(x^2=9\) has solutions \(x=3\) and \(x=-3\).

The identity is \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\), not \(x\) for every real \(x\). At \(x=-5\), the left side is 5. Losing the absolute value silently discards a sign condition.

For odd index, negative inputs have a unique negative real root, such as \(\sqrt[3]{-8}=-2\). The lab implements that real-root branch explicitly instead of relying on a generic fractional power of a negative float.

## 2.4.6 Rational Exponents for Positive Bases

For \(a>0\), integer \(m\), and positive integer \(n\),

\[
a^{m/n}=(\sqrt[n]{a})^m.
\]

Reduce the exponent as a rational number when useful. For example,

\[
16^{3/4}=(\sqrt[4]{16})^3=2^3=8,
\]

and

\[
27^{-2/3}=\frac1{(\sqrt[3]{27})^2}=\frac19.
\]

A rational exponent does not guarantee a rational answer. The exact value \(2^{1/2}=\sqrt2\) is irrational. An implementation based on `Fraction` cannot represent it as an exact rational number simply because the input exponent is rational.

Distinguish the index of a root from the number of periods in a financial model. They may have the same numerical value when solving \(g^n=A/P\), but the relationship comes from the model. A time interval must be expressed in units consistent with the period rate before it becomes an exponent.

## 2.4.7 Negative Bases Need Explicit Definitions

Integer powers of negative bases are unambiguous. Fractional powers require care. In real radical notation, \((-8)^{1/3}\) can be interpreted as the real cube root −2. A language's generic power operator applied to a negative base and a noninteger exponent need not choose that real branch.

Python's `(-8) ** (1 / 3)` produces a complex result rather than the intended negative real cube root. The floating expression `1 / 3` also represents an approximation to one third, not a symbolic odd-denominator exponent.

Our root function accepts a root **index** as an integer and handles a negative radicand only when that index is odd. This preserves the intended real operation. It rejects even roots of negative values rather than converting to complex arithmetic.

For most fixed-growth financial calculations, use growth factor \(g=1+r>0\). This avoids the negative-base ambiguity. It also excludes a period return of −100% or lower from the positive-balance model used here. Those exclusions should be stated rather than inferred from whichever power operation happens to execute.

## 2.4.8 Solve a Fixed-Growth Equation by Taking a Root

Suppose starting value \(P>0\), ending value \(A>0\), and period count \(n\geq1\) are known. Under a constant effective period rate and no intermediate flows,

\[
A=P(1+r)^n.
\]

Divide by positive principal and set \(g=1+r>0\):

\[
g^n=A/P.
\]

There is one positive real growth factor,

\[
g=(A/P)^{1/n},\qquad r=(A/P)^{1/n}-1.
\]

For even \(n\), the unrestricted power equation also has a negative real factor, but the model excludes it. For example, \(g^2=1.21\) has factors \(\pm1.1\); only \(g=1.1\) gives the required positive growth factor. The negative factor would imply \(r=-2.1\), outside \(r>-1\).

At \(n=0\), the model gives \(A=P\) independently of rate. Equal values do not determine a unique rate, and unequal values are inconsistent. The rate-inference function therefore requires a positive period count rather than attempting the undefined exponent \(1/0\).

## 2.4.9 Worked Equivalent-Rate Examples

Starting at 1,000 and ending at 1,210 after two periods gives ratio 1.21. Its positive square root is 1.1, so the equivalent period rate is 0.10, or 10%. Substitution gives \(1000(1.1)^2=1210\).

The total return is 21%, but dividing that total by two gives 10.5%, not the compounded equivalent rate. Two periods at 10.5% would produce a different ending value.

Starting at 1,000 and ending at 810 over two periods gives ratio 0.81, positive factor 0.9, and rate −0.10. A negative rate greater than −1 is consistent with positive balances. Starting at 1,000 and ending at 1,331 over three periods gives ratio 1.331, factor 1.1, and rate 10%.

These are constant **equivalent** rates for the specified endpoints. They do not establish that every actual period had the same return. If rates varied, their growth factors could produce the same product. Intermediate deposits, withdrawals, or fees would also change the endpoint equation, so do not infer a rate from balances alone when those flows are present.

The ratio \(A/P\) is dimensionless. Taking an \(n\)th root of that ratio preserves a dimensionless growth factor; taking an arbitrary fractional power of an unnormalized currency amount would not express this inference.

## 2.4.10 Exact Arithmetic, Approximation, and Supported Range

The lab uses two explicit numeric interfaces. Integer powers accept exact integer, rational-string, or `Fraction` bases and return exact rational results. Root and rate-inference functions accept built-in integers and floats, convert them to finite floats, and return approximate real values.

The root index is a built-in integer from 1 through 120, excluding Boolean values. This is a small teaching limit, not a mathematical upper bound on roots. Even roots of negative values, unsupported input types, and nonfinite inputs are rejected.

A positive finite mathematical ratio can still fall outside a float representation after division. The inference function rejects an overflowed, underflowed, or nonpositive computed ratio. If subtracting one from a tiny positive factor rounds the rate to exactly −1, it rejects that representation too rather than returning a rate outside the declared model.

For supported examples, check by raising the factor to the index and reconstructing the ending value. Use an explicit approximate comparison, as in Section 1.13, rather than demanding exact float equality or hiding discrepancies by display rounding. The checks here use a relative tolerance of \(10^{-12}\) and absolute tolerance \(10^{-12}\) on small synthetic examples; that is not a universal contractual accuracy policy.

## 2.4.11 Worked Python Lab — Exact Powers and Approximate Real Roots

Save as [code/section_2_04.py](code/section_2_04.py). The real-root function returns the principal nonnegative root for nonnegative inputs, or the unique negative root for a negative input with odd index. It does not return both signs of an even-power equation.

```python
"""Exact integer powers, approximate real roots, and equivalent period rates."""
from fractions import Fraction
import math


def integer_power(base, exponent):
    if type(base) not in (int, str, Fraction):
        raise TypeError("exact base requires int, rational string, or Fraction")
    if type(exponent) is not int:
        raise TypeError("exponent must be an integer, excluding bool")
    value = Fraction(base)
    if value == 0 and exponent <= 0:
        raise ValueError("zero base requires a positive exponent in this interface")
    return value ** exponent


def finite_float(value):
    if type(value) not in (int, float):
        raise TypeError("use a built-in int or float, excluding bool")
    try:
        number = float(value)
    except OverflowError as exc:
        raise ValueError("input exceeds the float interface") from exc
    if not math.isfinite(number):
        raise ValueError("input must be finite")
    return number


def root_index(index):
    if type(index) is not int or not 1 <= index <= 120:
        raise ValueError("root index must be an integer from 1 to 120")
    return index


def real_root(value, index):
    x, n = finite_float(value), root_index(index)
    if x < 0 and n % 2 == 0:
        raise ValueError("even root of a negative value is outside the real domain")
    magnitude = abs(x) ** (1.0 / n)
    return -magnitude if x < 0 else magnitude


def infer_period_rate(*, initial_value, final_value, periods):
    initial, final = finite_float(initial_value), finite_float(final_value)
    n = root_index(periods)
    if initial <= 0 or final <= 0:
        raise ValueError("rate inference requires positive endpoint values")
    ratio = final / initial
    if not math.isfinite(ratio) or ratio <= 0:
        raise ValueError("endpoint ratio is outside the supported float range")
    factor = real_root(ratio, n)
    rate = factor - 1.0
    if rate <= -1:
        raise ValueError("period rate cannot be represented inside the declared domain")
    return {"growth_factor": factor, "period_rate": rate, "target_ratio": ratio}


def main():
    print("Exact discount factor:", integer_power("1.05", -2))
    print(f"Principal square root of 9: {real_root(9, 2):.6f}")
    print(f"Real cube root of -8: {real_root(-8, 3):.6f}")
    print(f"16 to the power 3/4: {real_root(16, 4) ** 3:.6f}")
    for ending, periods in [(1210, 2), (810, 2), (1331, 3)]:
        result = infer_period_rate(initial_value=1000, final_value=ending, periods=periods)
        print(f"Ending {ending} over {periods} periods: "
              f"rate={result['period_rate']:.6f}, factor={result['growth_factor']:.6f}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

```text
16 to the power 3/4: 8.000000

Ending 810 over 2 periods: rate=-0.100000, factor=0.900000
Ending 1331 over 3 periods: rate=0.100000, factor=1.100000
```

## 2.4.12 Practice Problems

2. Simplify \(a^5a^{-2}\) and \((a^2)^3\) for \(a>0\). Explain why \(a^5+a^{-2}\) cannot be simplified by adding exponents.
3. Give \(\sqrt{49}\), all real solutions of \(x^2=49\), and \(\sqrt{(-7)^2}\). Explain the role of the principal root.
4. Evaluate \(81^{3/4}\), \(32^{-2/5}\), and the real cube root of −27. Is the real fourth root of −16 defined?
5. Starting value is 500 and ending value is 605 after two periods with no intermediate flows. Infer the constant equivalent period rate and verify it. Explain why dividing the total return by two gives a different answer.

## 2.4.13 Worked Answers

1. \((3/2)^{-2}=4/9\), \(7^0=1\), and \(0^3=0\). A negative exponent at zero requires an undefined reciprocal. The lab also excludes \(0^0\) as an explicit domain policy, while Python's `0 ** 0` evaluates to 1. Do not infer a mathematical convention solely from a language default.
2. Product exponents add, giving \(a^3\). A power of a power multiplies exponents, giving \(a^6\). The sum has different operation structure and remains a sum; for example, at \(a=2\) it is \(32+1/4\), not \(2^3\).
3. The principal square root is 7. The power equation has solutions ±7. The radical \(\sqrt{(-7)^2}\) equals 7 because \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\). The radical notation chooses the nonnegative root; solving the equation requires considering both signs.
4. Since the positive fourth root of 81 is 3, \(81^{3/4}=27\). The fifth root of 32 is 2, so \(32^{-2/5}=1/4\). The real cube root of −27 is −3. There is no real fourth root of −16 because every real fourth power is nonnegative.
5. Ratio is \(605/500=1.21\). Positive square root gives factor 1.1 and rate 10%. Reconstruction gives \(500(1.1)^2=605\). The total return is 21%; dividing by two gives 10.5%, which does not preserve the compound-growth equation.
6. Ratio is 0.729 and its positive cube root is 0.9, giving rate −10%. At zero periods, equal endpoints hold for every allowed period rate, so no unique rate is determined. Unequal endpoints are inconsistent with the zero-period model. The inference interface rejects zero periods rather than returning a fabricated unique answer.

## 2.4.14 Common Mistakes, Summary, and Sources

Common mistakes include treating a negative exponent as a negative sign, dropping parentheses around a base, distributing a power over addition, confusing the principal square root with all solutions of a square equation, and assuming a rational exponent always yields a rational result. Another is asking a generic fractional-power operator to choose a real root branch for a negative input.

State the base and exponent domains, identify the intended root, and apply power rules only where the expressions are defined. In financial rate inference, normalize endpoints to a dimensionless ratio, require a matching positive period count, select the positive growth factor, and reconstruct the original equation. Keep approximate arithmetic checks separate from contractual accuracy claims.
