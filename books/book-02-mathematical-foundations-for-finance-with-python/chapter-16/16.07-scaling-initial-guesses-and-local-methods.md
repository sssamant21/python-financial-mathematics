## Learning objectives

Distinguish parameter scaling from objective scaling, derive transformed gradients and Hessians, assess initial-guess sensitivity, recognize local optima, design deterministic multistart experiments, and report reproducible calibration diagnostics.

## 1. Why scaling matters

Financial calibration often mixes parameters with different units and magnitudes: decimal interest rates, volatility, years, currency amounts, and dimensionless correlation. A solver operating directly on raw coordinates can move too far in one direction and too little in another.

Let \(x=c+Sz\), where \(S\) is an invertible diagonal scaling matrix and \(c\) is a reference vector. Define the transformed objective

\[
\phi(z)=\frac{f(c+Sz)}{\gamma},\qquad \gamma>0.
\]

The parameter transform changes coordinates; dividing by positive \(\gamma\) changes objective units but not minimizers. The derivatives are

\[
\boxed{\nabla_z\phi(z)=\gamma^{-1}S^\mathsf T\nabla_x f(c+Sz)}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{H_z=\gamma^{-1}S^\mathsf TH_xS}.
\]

For an affine invertible transform, stationary points correspond one-to-one. A nonlinear transform may introduce additional complications or restrict the domain.

## 2. Worked example: badly scaled quadratic

Consider

\[
f(x,y)=\tfrac12[10^6(x-0.02)^2+(y-3)^2].
\]

Its minimizer is \((0.02,3)\), but the Hessian \(\operatorname{diag}(10^6,1)\) has condition number \(10^6\). Fixed-step gradient descent must use \(\alpha<2\times10^{-6}\), leaving the \(y\)-direction slow.

Let \(u=1000(x-0.02)\) and \(v=y-3\). Then

\[
f=\tfrac12(u^2+v^2),
\]

with transformed Hessian \(I\) and condition number \(1\). This change of variables preserves the optimizer while making the geometry more favorable.

## 3. Objective scaling versus parameter scaling

Multiplying an objective by a positive constant does not change its minimizers, but it scales the gradient and Hessian. Fixed-step gradient descent therefore needs a corresponding learning-rate adjustment. Exact full Newton directions are invariant under positive scalar objective scaling when the gradient and Hessian are both scaled consistently.

Parameter scaling changes the numerical geometry and can improve conditioning. It must be accompanied by correctly transformed derivatives and an explicit conversion back to financial units.

## 4. Initial guesses and basins of attraction

A **local solver** uses a starting point \(x_0\) and iterative information near its current iterate. For a nonconvex objective, different starts can lead to different stationary points, local minima, or failures. The set of initial points that converge to a particular solution under a specified algorithm is its basin of attraction.

The result depends on the objective, algorithm, stopping rules, scaling, and start. A successful solver status does not establish global optimality.

## 5. Worked example: two local minima

Consider

\[
q(x)=(x^2-1)^2+0.2x.
\]

Its derivative is

\[
q'(x)=4x(x^2-1)+0.2,
\]

and its second derivative is \(q''(x)=12x^2-4\). This tilted double-well objective has two local minima separated by a local maximum. Starts near opposite wells may converge to different minima. The left-hand well is lower because the positive linear tilt favors negative \(x\).

Do not assume a grid or finite multistart sample proves global optimality; it provides comparative evidence only.

## 6. Financial example: rate and spread calibration

Suppose a model is calibrated in two parameters: a decimal rate \(r\) near \(0.02\) and a spread \(s\) measured in basis points near \(150\). A raw Euclidean step treats a unit change in \(r\) and a unit change in \(s\) equally, although they represent very different economic changes.

Define dimensionless coordinates

\[
z_r=\frac{r-0.02}{0.01},\qquad
z_s=\frac{s-150}{100}.
\]

One unit in \(z_r\) corresponds to 100 basis points of rate, while one unit in \(z_s\) corresponds to 100 basis points of spread. These reference scales are illustrative and must be documented; the correct choice depends on sensitivities, permissible ranges, and data uncertainty.

## 7. Deterministic multistart protocol

A reproducible local-calibration comparison should:

1. Freeze the input observations, objective definition, parameter transforms, and solver configuration.
2. Define a documented, deterministic set of initial guesses.
3. Run each start independently, preserving status and termination reason.
4. Convert solutions back to physical parameter units.
5. Recompute objective and residuals independently.
6. Compare candidate solutions only if domain and numerical checks pass.
7. Report the best **observed** result, never an unsupported global-optimum claim.

If starts are randomly sampled, record the generator, seed, and sampling distribution. Deterministic grids are easier to audit but can miss narrow basins.

## 8. Diagnostics and failure modes

Record raw and scaled parameter values, initial guesses, final loss, gradient norm, iteration count, finite-value checks, and objective history. Watch for non-identifiability, flat directions, boundary violations, overflow, inconsistent derivative units, and convergence to economically implausible parameters.

A small change in loss alone can reflect numerical stagnation. Compare gradients and residuals, and consider independent finite-difference derivative checks.

## 10. Twenty worked exercises

**1.** Define parameter scaling.  
*Answer:* Changing the coordinates used to represent decision variables, such as \(x=c+Sz\).

**2.** Define objective scaling.  
*Answer:* Multiplying or dividing the objective by a positive constant.

**3.** Does positive objective scaling change minimizers?  
*Answer:* No.

**4.** Does positive objective scaling change the raw gradient?  
*Answer:* Yes, by the same positive factor.

**5.** Give the transformed gradient for \(x=c+Sz\).  
*Answer:* \(S^\mathsf T\nabla_x f/\gamma\).

**6.** Give the transformed Hessian.  
*Answer:* \(S^\mathsf TH_xS/\gamma\).

**7.** Find the minimizer of the badly scaled quadratic.  
*Answer:* \((0.02,3)\).

**8.** Find its raw Hessian.  
*Answer:* \(\operatorname{diag}(10^6,1)\).

**9.** Find its condition number.  
*Answer:* \(10^6\).

**10.** Define \(u=1000(x-0.02)\). What is \(x\)?  
*Answer:* \(x=0.02+u/1000\).

**11.** Find the transformed Hessian.  
*Answer:* \(I\).

**12.** Find its condition number.  
*Answer:* \(1\).

**13.** State the stable fixed-step range for the raw quadratic.  
*Answer:* \(0<\alpha<2\times10^{-6}\).

**14.** State the stable fixed-step range for the transformed quadratic.  
*Answer:* \(0<\alpha<2\).

**15.** Differentiate \((x^2-1)^2+0.2x\).  
*Answer:* \(4x(x^2-1)+0.2\).

**16.** Find its second derivative.  
*Answer:* \(12x^2-4\).

**17.** Why can different starts produce different answers?  
*Answer:* A nonconvex objective can have multiple basins of attraction.

**18.** Does multistart prove global optimality?  
*Answer:* No.

**19.** Why report raw and scaled parameters?

### Stationarity and multistart reference limits

Independent high-precision roots of the stationary cubic corroborate the two local minima reached by the four selected starts. A pool containing only a real failed start returns `best_observed=None`; failed runs are excluded from a mixed pool. However, `gradient_tolerance` means stationarity within tolerance, not a minimum certificate: the middle stationary point has negative second derivative and can satisfy that status when used as the start. Best observed remains a comparison of successful runs, with `global_optimality_certified=False`. [Independent root and failure-pool evidence](../validation/chapter-16-multistart-reference-followup.md).


### Scaling and step-size reference checks

For the stated quadratic, the raw-coordinate Hessian is `diag(1e6, 1)`. The inverse coordinate derivative `diag(1/1000, 1)` transforms it to the identity Hessian, giving Euclidean spectral condition numbers `1e6` and `1` in those respective coordinates. These are coordinate-dependent quantities; a numerical step size is not transferable across parameter units without transformation. Selected second differences check the implemented curvatures, and the existing scalar descent solver tests individual modes at steps `1/a`, `2/a` and `3/a`. This is not a new two-dimensional optimization API. [Evidence and scope](../validation/chapter-16-scaling-reference-followup.md).
