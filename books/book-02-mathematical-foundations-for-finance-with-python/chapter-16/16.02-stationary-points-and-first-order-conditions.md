## Learning objectives

Find stationary points of differentiable objectives, derive first-order necessary conditions for interior optima, solve scalar and vector gradient equations, distinguish stationary points from minima and maxima, and interpret residual-based calibration in financial units.

## 1. First-order necessary conditions

For differentiable \(f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R\), a **stationary point** \(x^*\) satisfies

\[
\boxed{\nabla f(x^*)=0}.
\]

If \(x^*\) is an interior local minimizer or maximizer and \(f\) is differentiable there, then the gradient must vanish. This is a **necessary**, not generally sufficient, condition. A zero gradient can also occur at a saddle point or a flat point that is neither a local maximum nor minimum.

A first-order condition alone does not prove global optimality. A convex differentiable objective is an important exception: a stationary point on a convex open domain is a global minimizer.

## 2. One-dimensional example

For

\[
f(x)=(x-3)^2+5,
\]

the derivative is \(f'(x)=2(x-3)\). The first-order condition \(f'(x)=0\) yields \(x^*=3\). Since \(f(x)\ge5\) for every real \(x\), this is a global minimum with \(f(3)=5\).

For \(g(x)=-(x-3)^2+5\), the same stationary point is a global maximum. The first-order condition locates the candidate, but the objective's shape determines its type.

## 3. Stationary does not imply optimal

Consider \(h(x)=x^3\). Its derivative is \(h'(x)=3x^2\), so \(x=0\) is stationary. Yet \(h(x)\) is strictly increasing across zero and has no local extremum there.

In two variables, \(s(x,y)=x^2-y^2\) has gradient \((2x,-2y)\). Its only stationary point is \((0,0)\), but values rise along the \(x\)-axis and fall along the \(y\)-axis: it is a saddle.

## 4. Multivariable first-order conditions

For

\[
Q(x,y)=2(x-1)^2+3(y+2)^2,
\]

the gradient is

\[
\nabla Q(x,y)=
\begin{bmatrix}
4(x-1)\\
6(y+2)
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Setting both components to zero gives \(x=1,y=-2\). The objective is nonnegative and vanishes at this point, proving it is a global minimum.

**Important:** solving one partial derivative at a time is valid only when the complete simultaneous system is satisfied. Coupled variables generally require solving a system.

## 5. Coupled quadratic calibration

Consider

\[
L(x,y)=(x+y-3)^2+(x-y-1)^2.
\]

Expanding gives

\[
L=2x^2+2y^2-8x-4y+10.
\]

Its gradient is

\[
\nabla L=
\begin{bmatrix}4x-8\\4y-4\end{bmatrix},
\]

and the first-order conditions yield \((x^*,y^*)=(2,1)\). Both residuals vanish, so \(L(2,1)=0\). The Hessian is \(\operatorname{diag}(4,4)\), confirming strict convexity and a unique global minimum.

This example illustrates a general least-squares calibration: a stationary solution is determined by the interaction between model sensitivities and residuals, not simply by setting each residual to zero when there are more observations than parameters.

## 6. Financial rate calibration

Reuse the synthetic pricing model from Section 16.1:

\[
M(r)=1000(1+2r),\qquad
L(r)=[M(r)-1100]^2=(2000r-100)^2.
\]

Differentiate:

\[
\boxed{L'(r)=4000(2000r-100)}.
\]

The first-order condition \(L'(r)=0\) implies

\[
\boxed{r^*=0.05}.
\]

Because \(L(r)\ge0\) and \(L(0.05)=0\), this is a global minimizer. The derivative measures change in squared currency residual per unit decimal rate; it should not be confused with a market DV01.

## 7. Least-squares gradient and normal equations

Let \(r(\theta)=A\theta-b\) be a residual vector for a linear model, and define

\[
L(\theta)=\tfrac12\|A\theta-b\|_2^2.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{\nabla L(\theta)=A^\mathsf T(A\theta-b)}.
\]

Stationarity gives the **normal equations**

\[
\boxed{A^\mathsf TA\theta=A^\mathsf Tb}.
\]

If \(A\) has full column rank, the minimizer is unique. If \(A\) is rank deficient, there can be infinitely many minimizers. Numerically, solving the normal equations directly can amplify conditioning problems; QR or SVD methods are generally preferable for real calibration systems.

## 8. Domain and boundary caveat

An interior differentiable optimum must be stationary, but a boundary optimum need not satisfy \(\nabla f=0\). For example, minimize \(f(x)=x\) subject to \(x\ge0\). The minimum is \(x=0\), yet \(f'(0)=1\). This is a constrained problem; later chapters cover boundary and KKT conditions.

Likewise, nonsmooth objectives such as \(f(x)=|x|\) have minima where an ordinary derivative does not exist. First-order derivative equations must not be applied blindly.

## 9. Numerical interpretation

In computational calibration, a small gradient is a diagnostic, not a complete certificate. Gradient magnitudes depend on input scaling and objective units. A small gradient may occur near a flat saddle, and finite-difference gradients can be misleading when steps are poorly chosen. Always report the objective, residuals, domain validity, curvature evidence where available, and numerical tolerances.

**1.** State the differentiable interior first-order necessary condition.  
*Answer:* \(\nabla f(x^*)=0\).

**2.** Is this condition sufficient for a local minimum in general?  
*Answer:* No.

**3.** Differentiate \((x-3)^2+5\).  
*Answer:* \(2(x-3)\).

**4.** Find its stationary point.  
*Answer:* \(x=3\).

**5.** Find its minimum value.  
*Answer:* \(5\).

**6.** Classify \(x=3\) for \(-(x-3)^2+5\).  
*Answer:* Global maximum.

**7.** Differentiate \(x^3\).  
*Answer:* \(3x^2\).

**8.** Is zero a local minimum of \(x^3\)?  
*Answer:* No.

**9.** Find the gradient of \(x^2-y^2\).  
*Answer:* \((2x,-2y)\).

**10.** Classify its stationary point at the origin.  
*Answer:* Saddle.

**11.** Find \(\nabla Q\) for \(Q=2(x-1)^2+3(y+2)^2\).  
*Answer:* \((4(x-1),6(y+2))\).

**12.** Solve \(\nabla Q=0\).  
*Answer:* \((1,-2)\).

**13.** Find \(Q(1,-2)\).  
*Answer:* \(0\).

**14.** Find the stationary point of \((x+y-3)^2+(x-y-1)^2\).  
*Answer:* \((2,1)\).

**15.** Evaluate that least-squares loss at \((2,1)\).  
*Answer:* \(0\).
