## 1. From gradient descent to curvature-aware steps

Gradient descent uses the direction \(d=-\nabla f(x)\). Newton's method instead approximates the objective locally with a second-order Taylor model:

\[
m_x(d)=f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\mathsf Td+\tfrac12d^\mathsf TH(x)d.
\]

Setting its derivative with respect to \(d\) to zero gives

\[
\boxed{H(x)d_N=-\nabla f(x)},\qquad x_{k+1}=x_k+\alpha_kd_N.
\]

When \(H\) is positive definite, the Newton direction is a descent direction because \(\nabla f^\mathsf Td_N=-d_N^\mathsf THd_N<0\) for nonzero gradient. Solve the linear system rather than explicitly forming the inverse.

## 2. One-dimensional Newton method

For a scalar objective with \(f''(x)\ne0\),

\[
\boxed{x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{f'(x_k)}{f''(x_k)}}.
\]

Consider \(f(x)=\tfrac12a(x-b)^2\), \(a>0\). Since \(f'(x)=a(x-b)\) and \(f''(x)=a\), a full Newton step gives \(x_{k+1}=b\), the exact minimizer, from any starting point.

This one-step result is special to exact quadratics and should not be generalized to nonlinear objectives.

## 3. Financial calibration example

Use the normalized rate-calibration objective

\[
\ell(r)=(r-0.05)^2.
\]

Its derivatives are \(\ell'(r)=2(r-0.05)\) and \(\ell''(r)=2\). A full Newton step from \(r_0=0\) gives

\[
r_1=0-\frac{-0.1}{2}=\boxed{0.05}.
\]

For the unnormalized loss \(L(r)=4{,}000{,}000(r-0.05)^2\), the gradient and Hessian both scale by \(4{,}000{,}000\), so the exact Newton step is unchanged. This contrasts with fixed-step gradient descent, where objective scaling changes the stable learning-rate interval.

## 4. Newton's method in multiple dimensions

For

\[
f(x,y)=\tfrac12\left[4(x-1)^2+6(y+2)^2\right],
\]

we have

\[
\nabla f=\begin{bmatrix}4(x-1)\\6(y+2)\end{bmatrix},\quad
H=\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&6\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Solving \(Hd=-\nabla f\) yields \(d=(1-x,-2-y)\). One full Newton step reaches \((1,-2)\).

For a general symmetric positive-definite Hessian, a Cholesky-based solve is appropriate. For singular, nearly singular, or indefinite matrices, blindly solving and accepting the full Newton step can fail.

## 5. When Newton steps are unsafe

An indefinite Hessian can produce an ascent direction. For \(f(x)=-x^2\), Newton's step points to \(x=0\), a **maximum**, not a minimum. A singular Hessian may prevent a unique Newton direction. A poorly conditioned Hessian can amplify rounding errors.

Practical safeguards include:
- Check positive definiteness or otherwise establish descent.
- Use damping or regularization, such as \(H+\lambda I\) with an appropriate \(\lambda>0\).
- Apply a backtracking line search or trust-region method.
- Reject nonfinite function values, derivatives, and trial points.
- Track objective decrease and termination reason, not just the step size.

## 6. Local convergence

Under standard smoothness, nonsingularity, and suitable starting-point assumptions near a strict local minimizer, full-step Newton iterations can converge **quadratically**. This is a local result, not a global convergence guarantee. In practice, globalization strategies are used to improve behavior far from the solution.

## 7. Quasi-Newton idea

Quasi-Newton methods avoid computing the exact Hessian at every iteration. Instead, they build an approximation \(B_k\) to the Hessian, or \(M_k\) to its inverse, from differences in iterates and gradients.

Define

\[
s_k=x_{k+1}-x_k,\qquad y_k=\nabla f(x_{k+1})-\nabla f(x_k).
\]

The secant relation is \(B_{k+1}s_k=y_k\). A standard BFGS inverse-Hessian update is

\[
\boxed{M_{k+1}=(I-\rho s_ky_k^\mathsf T)M_k(I-\rho y_ks_k^\mathsf T)+\rho s_ks_k^\mathsf T},
\quad \rho=(y_k^\mathsf Ts_k)^{-1}.
\]

The update requires \(y_k^\mathsf Ts_k>0\) to preserve positive definiteness when \(M_k\) is positive definite. Line-search conditions such as Wolfe conditions are often used to support this curvature condition. Skipping or damping an unsafe update is preferable to dividing by a nonpositive or near-zero curvature scalar.

## 8. BFGS versus L-BFGS

Full BFGS stores an \(n\times n\) approximation, which costs \(O(n^2)\) memory. Limited-memory BFGS (L-BFGS) stores a small history of vector pairs, making it useful for larger problems. Neither method removes the need for objective scaling, reliable gradients, valid domains, or independent solution checks.

## 9. Financial calibration considerations

In nonlinear yield-curve or volatility calibration, gradients and curvature can be noisy, badly scaled, or non-identifiable. Newton-like methods may converge rapidly near a well-behaved solution but can move into economically invalid parameter regions unless constraints or transformations are specified. For a least-squares objective, Gauss–Newton approximates curvature using the residual Jacobian; it is related but not identical to full Newton when residuals are nonzero.

A small final gradient is evidence of stationarity, not proof of economic correctness or global optimality. Validate residuals, sensitivities, input data, model assumptions, and out-of-sample behavior.

## 11. Twenty worked exercises

**1.** State the Newton direction equation.  
*Answer:* \(Hd=-\nabla f\).

**2.** State the scalar Newton update.  
*Answer:* \(x_{k+1}=x_k-f'(x_k)/f''(x_k)\).

**3.** Why avoid explicit Hessian inversion?  
*Answer:* Solving the linear system is generally more stable and efficient.

**4.** When is the Newton direction guaranteed to be descent?  
*Answer:* For a positive-definite Hessian and nonzero gradient.

**5.** Find the Newton step for \(f(x)=\tfrac12a(x-b)^2\).  
*Answer:* \(d=b-x\).

**6.** How many full Newton steps does this quadratic require?  
*Answer:* One.

**7.** Find the normalized calibration gradient at \(r=0\).  
*Answer:* \(-0.1\).

**8.** Find its Hessian.  
*Answer:* \(2\).

**9.** Find the full Newton update from \(r=0\).  
*Answer:* \(0.05\).

**10.** Does multiplying an objective by a positive constant change an exact Newton direction?  
*Answer:* No, when both gradient and Hessian scale by the same constant.

**11.** Give the Hessian of the two-variable quadratic example.  
*Answer:* \(\operatorname{diag}(4,6)\).

**12.** Give its minimizer.  
*Answer:* \((1,-2)\).

**13.** What can go wrong with an indefinite Hessian?  
*Answer:* Newton's direction may fail to decrease the objective.

**14.** What can go wrong with a singular Hessian?  
*Answer:* The Newton system may have no unique solution.

**15.** What is damping?  
*Answer:* Taking a reduced step or modifying curvature to improve stability.

**16.** Define the quasi-Newton displacement \(s_k\).  
*Answer:* \(x_{k+1}-x_k\).

**17.** Define the gradient difference \(y_k\).  
*Answer:* \(\nabla f(x_{k+1})-\nabla f(x_k)\).

**18.** State the BFGS curvature condition.  
*Answer:* \(y_k^\mathsf Ts_k>0\).

**19.** Why use L-BFGS?  
*Answer:* To reduce memory usage for large-dimensional optimization.

## Scalar secant numerical contract — validation follow-up

The Python lab implements a scalar secant curvature, not a BFGS matrix update. Each callback value must be a finite numeric value excluding bool. The helper forms finite represented differences `s=x_new-x_old` and `y=g_new-g_old`, compares their exact represented product strictly against the nonnegative absolute threshold, and requires a finite positive `y/s`. The default threshold `1e-14` can intentionally reject small positive pairs; a result underflowing to zero is rejected. The product has objective units when gradients and coordinates are defined consistently, so objective scaling requires attention to the absolute threshold. Exact product comparison does not remove rounding/cancellation in the differences or validate derivative accuracy.
