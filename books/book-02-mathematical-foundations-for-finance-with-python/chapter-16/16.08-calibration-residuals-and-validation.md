## Objectives

Distinguish optimization convergence from model fit, compute signed and weighted residuals, assess identifiability, separate calibration and holdout evidence, and define reproducible financial-model acceptance criteria.

## 1. Residuals and objective functions

For observations \(y_i\), model outputs \(m_i(\theta)\), and a parameter vector \(\theta\), define the signed residual

\[
e_i(\theta)=m_i(\theta)-y_i.
\]

Residuals retain units of the observed quantity: dollars for prices, basis points for spreads, or decimal rates for yields. A weighted least-squares objective is

\[
L(\theta)=\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^n w_i e_i(\theta)^2,\qquad w_i\ge0.
\]

If measurement uncertainty \(\sigma_i>0\) is known and appropriate, weights \(w_i=1/\sigma_i^2\) yield dimensionless standardized squared residuals. Arbitrary weights must not be described as statistical uncertainty weights.

A small weighted loss can hide a large error on a lightly weighted observation. Always inspect raw residuals and their distribution.

## 2. Metrics and interpretation

For \(n\) observations, define

\[
\operatorname{MAE}=\frac1n\sum_i|e_i|,\qquad
\operatorname{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_i e_i^2},\qquad
\operatorname{Bias}=\frac1n\sum_i e_i.
\]

Also report maximum absolute error \(\max_i|e_i|\). MAE, RMSE, bias, and maximum error have the same units as the observations. RMSE is more sensitive to large errors than MAE. Bias near zero does not imply good fit because positive and negative errors may cancel.

For weighted metrics, clearly state whether weights are normalized and whether the reported quantity is a weighted mean or a raw sum of squares.

## 3. Synthetic financial calibration

Suppose a synthetic model predicts a price using

\[
m_i(r)=1000(1+t_i r),
\]

where \(r\) is a decimal annual rate and \(t_i\) is a year fraction. Use training observations

\[
(t_i,y_i)\in\{(1,1050),(2,1100),(3,1150)\}.
\]

Every observation is generated by \(r^*=0.05\), so all training residuals vanish at \(r^*\). The squared-loss derivative is

\[
L'(r)=\sum_i1000t_i[1000(1+t_ir)-y_i],
\]

and the curvature is

\[
L''(r)=\sum_i(1000t_i)^2>0.
\]

Thus the unique minimizer is \(r^*=0.05\). The example is synthetic and does not constitute a market pricing model.

## 4. Holdout validation

Calibration error and holdout error answer different questions. A holdout observation must not be used to choose parameters, weights, stopping rules, or model specifications if it is to remain an independent evaluation. For example, a holdout pair \((t,y)=(4,1201)\) has a price residual of \(-1\) dollar at \(r=0.05\), despite zero training residuals.

A good in-sample fit does not guarantee predictive validity, stability under market changes, or economic plausibility. For time-dependent financial observations, avoid look-ahead leakage and use a time-aware split where appropriate.

## 5. Gradient and Jacobian checks

Let \(J_{ij}=\partial e_i/\partial\theta_j\). For ordinary least squares,

\[
\nabla L=J^\mathsf Te,\qquad
H_L=J^\mathsf TJ+\sum_i e_iH_{e_i}.
\]

The Gauss–Newton approximation retains \(J^\mathsf TJ\). In a linear residual model, the residual Hessians vanish and Gauss–Newton curvature is exact.

A small gradient can coexist with a poor fit, especially when residuals cancel in the gradient or when the Jacobian has weak sensitivity. Record both gradient norm and residual metrics.

## 6. Identifiability and conditioning

A calibration parameter is locally identifiable only to the extent that the observations respond distinguishably to it. Rank-deficient or nearly rank-deficient Jacobians produce nonunique or unstable parameter estimates.

For example, \(m_i(a,b)=a+b\) cannot separately identify \(a\) and \(b\) from any number of observations of that same sum. Multiple parameter pairs yield the same predictions.

Inspect Jacobian rank or singular values, parameter sensitivity, perturbation stability, and whether fitted parameters are economically meaningful.

## 7. Acceptance criteria and solver status

A defensible calibration gate should distinguish:

- **Numerical:** finite outputs, solver termination reason, derivative consistency, gradient norm, and conditioning.
- **Fit quality:** MAE, RMSE, maximum absolute error, bias, and per-instrument residuals.
- **Model validity:** permissible parameter domain, units, monotonicity or arbitrage constraints where applicable, and independent pricing checks.
- **Generalization:** holdout errors and stability under reasonable input perturbations.
- **Reproducibility:** data version, code revision, weights, initial guesses, tolerances, and environment.

Thresholds must be chosen for the actual financial use case; no universal dollar-error or gradient tolerance is appropriate.

## 8. Common failure patterns

**False convergence:** the solver stops due to a tiny step despite large residuals. Check objective and gradient independently.

**Residual cancellation:** mean bias is near zero but RMSE remains large. Inspect signed and absolute residuals.

**Overweighting:** an instrument with a high weight dominates the fit. Document weighting assumptions.

**Non-identifiability:** different parameter vectors produce equivalent outputs. Check Jacobian rank and report parameter uncertainty.

**Leakage:** a holdout instrument influences calibration choices. Freeze validation splits before tuning.

**Unit mismatch:** prices, yields, and basis-point errors are mixed without normalization. Convert explicitly before aggregating.

## 11. Applied assessment

Fit the synthetic training observations analytically, derive the gradient and Hessian, and calculate all residual metrics. Evaluate the untouched holdout instrument and explain the discrepancy. Demonstrate non-identifiability in the \(a+b\) example. Propose use-case-specific acceptance thresholds without asserting that a low gradient alone certifies the model.

**Acceptance:** correct residual signs and units, transparent weighting, independent holdout evaluation, Jacobian-rank reasoning, and explicit evidence gaps.
