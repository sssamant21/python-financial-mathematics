## Learning objectives

Express a financial decision or calibration problem as a mathematical objective, distinguish decision variables from fixed inputs and model parameters, distinguish minimizing cost from maximizing value, identify feasible domains and hidden constraints, and compute a simple analytical optimum with Python reference code.

## 1. What is optimization?

Optimization chooses values of **decision variables** to make an **objective function** as small or as large as possible.

\[
\boxed{\min_{x\in D} f(x)\qquad\text{or}\qquad\max_{x\in D}g(x)}
\]

Here \(x\in\mathbb R^n\) is the decision vector, \(D\) is its allowed domain, and \(f\) or \(g\) maps decisions to a scalar performance measure. This chapter focuses on unconstrained methods; a mathematically unconstrained problem has \(D=\mathbb R^n\), but actual financial decisions often have explicit or implicit restrictions. Chapter 17 develops constrained optimization.

Minimizing \(f\) is equivalent to maximizing \(-f\). However, changing a sign does not change domain restrictions or make a nonconvex problem convex.

## 2. Decision variables versus inputs

Consider the objective

\[
f(x;\theta,z).
\]

- \(x\): **decision variables** selected by the optimizer.
- \(\theta\): fixed **model parameters**, such as a target rate or penalty coefficient.
- \(z\): fixed **observed inputs**, such as market quotes or cash-flow data.
- \(f\): scalar **objective**, such as cost, residual sum of squares, or negative utility.

For calibration, a model parameter can become a **decision variable** during fitting. Its role depends on the problem formulation, not on its everyday name.

## 3. What makes a useful objective?

A good objective is measurable, has consistent units, corresponds to the actual decision goal, and makes trade-offs explicit. Common finance examples include:

- Minimize squared model-to-market pricing residuals.
- Minimize a specified risk measure.
- Maximize modeled expected return or utility.
- Minimize transaction or funding costs.
- Minimize deviations from a target allocation.

These goals are not interchangeable. A small calibration error does not guarantee good out-of-sample pricing, and a low-variance allocation does not necessarily maximize expected return.

## 4. Worked example: a one-parameter calibration

Suppose a simplified pricing model predicts

\[
M(r)=1000(1+2r)
\]

for a two-year illustrative linear-rate model. The observed target is \(1100\) currency units. Calibrate \(r\) by minimizing squared residual:

\[
\boxed{L(r)=[1000(1+2r)-1100]^2}.
\]

Simplify:

\[
L(r)=(2000r-100)^2
=4{,}000{,}000(r-0.05)^2.
\]

Differentiate:

\[
L'(r)=8{,}000{,}000(r-0.05),\qquad
L''(r)=8{,}000{,}000>0.
\]

The unique global minimizer is \(r^*=0.05\), with \(L(r^*)=0\). This synthetic linear-rate model is deliberately not the continuously compounded valuation model used in Chapter 15.

## 5. Two-variable objective

Suppose two independent calibration parameters \(x\) and \(y\) are measured relative to targets \(a\) and \(b\):

\[
\boxed{Q(x,y)=2(x-a)^2+3(y-b)^2}.
\]

Then

\[
\nabla Q=\begin{bmatrix}4(x-a)\\6(y-b)\end{bmatrix},\qquad
H_Q=\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&6\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Since the Hessian is positive definite, \(Q\) is strictly convex and has the unique global minimizer \((a,b)\). The coefficients \(2\) and \(3\) are objective weights; their values should reflect documented scaling or uncertainty assumptions rather than arbitrary numerical convenience.

## 6. Weighted calibration and units

For model outputs \(m_i(\theta)\), targets \(y_i\), and positive weights \(w_i\), a common objective is

\[
\boxed{L(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i[m_i(\theta)-y_i]^2}.
\]

A weight may represent inverse measurement variance when the statistical assumptions support that interpretation. Without appropriate scaling, large-dollar instruments can dominate smaller-dollar instruments even if their relative pricing errors are similar. Weights must be chosen and disclosed before interpreting the fitted parameter.

A residual sum of squares is **nonnegative**, but need not be convex in \(\theta\) when the pricing model is nonlinear. The objective's curvature, identifiability, and number of minima must be assessed rather than assumed.

## 7. Local versus global optima

A **local minimum** is no worse than nearby feasible points. A **global minimum** is no worse than every feasible point in the domain. A stationary point with zero gradient can be a minimum, maximum, or saddle; it can also be a degenerate stationary point requiring additional analysis.

For a differentiable convex objective on a convex open domain, a stationary point is a global minimizer. Strict convexity guarantees at most one minimizer, but existence still requires suitable conditions.

## 8. Domains and hidden constraints

Although Chapter 16 introduces unconstrained optimization, a financial rate may be bounded by model assumptions, maturity must be nonnegative, and portfolio weights may have budget or long-only requirements. Never quietly drop these constraints. If an objective is only defined on a restricted domain, describe it explicitly and avoid calling the problem unconstrained over all real numbers.

For the calibration example, we can mathematically minimize \(L(r)\) over all \(r\in\mathbb R\); the optimum happens to lie in a financially plausible range. A different target could produce an economically invalid optimum unless the model domain is restricted.

## 10. Twenty worked exercises

**1.** What is a decision variable?  
*Answer:* A quantity selected to optimize an objective.

**2.** What is an objective function?  
*Answer:* A scalar function that measures the decision goal.

**3.** How can maximization be written as minimization?  
*Answer:* Maximize \(g\) by minimizing \(-g\).

**4.** What is a global minimizer?  
*Answer:* A point whose objective is no greater than at any other allowed point.

**5.** What is a local minimizer?  
*Answer:* A point whose objective is no greater than at sufficiently nearby allowed points.

**6.** Identify the decision variable in \(L(r)\).  
*Answer:* The rate \(r\).

**7.** Identify the target in the calibration example.  
*Answer:* \(1100\) currency units.

**8.** Write the model prediction.  
*Answer:* \(M(r)=1000(1+2r)\).

**9.** Write the residual.  
*Answer:* \(2000r-100\).

**10.** Write the squared-error objective.  
*Answer:* \((2000r-100)^2\).

**11.** Expand it about its minimizer.  
*Answer:* \(4{,}000{,}000(r-0.05)^2\).

**12.** Differentiate once.  
*Answer:* \(8{,}000{,}000(r-0.05)\).

**13.** Differentiate twice.  
*Answer:* \(8{,}000{,}000\).

**14.** Find the minimizing rate.  
*Answer:* \(0.05\).

**15.** Find the minimum loss.  
*Answer:* \(0\).

**16.** What is \(L(0.04)\)?  
*Answer:* \(400\).

**17.** What is \(L(0.06)\)?  
*Answer:* \(400\).

**18.** Find the minimizer of \(2(x-a)^2+3(y-b)^2\).  
*Answer:* \((a,b)\).

**19.** Is every sum-of-squares calibration objective convex in its parameters?  
*Answer:* No; nonlinear model residuals can yield nonconvex objectives.
