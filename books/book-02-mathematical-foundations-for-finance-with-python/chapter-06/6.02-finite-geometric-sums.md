## Learning objectives

Recognize geometric sums, derive their finite closed form, handle the ratio-one case, reindex powers, apply sums to recurring payments, and assess numerical accuracy when the ratio is close to one.

## 1. Geometric sequences and sums

A geometric sequence multiplies each term by a constant ratio \(q\). Its first \(n\) terms, starting with \(a\), are \(a,aq,\ldots,aq^{n-1}\). Their sum is

\[
S_n=a\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}q^k.
\]

The number of terms is \(n\), not \(n+1\). When \(n=0\), the sum is empty and equals zero.

## 2. Deriving the finite geometric-sum formula

Let

\[
G_n=1+q+q^2+\cdots+q^{n-1}.
\]

Multiply by \(q\):

\[
qG_n=q+q^2+\cdots+q^n.
\]

Subtracting yields \((1-q)G_n=1-q^n\). Therefore, for \(q\ne1\),

\[
\boxed{G_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}q^k=\frac{1-q^n}{1-q}
=\frac{q^n-1}{q-1}.}
\]

When \(q=1\), every term is one, so

\[
\boxed{G_n=n.}
\]

For \(n=0\), both cases return zero. The formula is valid for any real \(q\) (including negative and zero), with the special case \(q=1\).

## 3. Reindexing and scaled sums

A geometric sum starting at exponent \(m\) satisfies

\[
\sum_{k=m}^{m+n-1}aq^k=aq^m G_n
\]

when the powers are well-defined. For financial rates \(q=1+i>0\), there is no issue with negative exponents.

For powers running backward,

\[
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}q^{n-1-k}=G_n.
\]

This is the form that appears when equal end-of-period deposits accumulate to a common horizon.

## 4. Future value of equal end-of-period contributions

Let \(B_0\) be the opening balance, \(i\) the effective periodic rate, \(q=1+i>0\), and \(C\) a constant signed contribution at each period's **end**. The recurrence is

\[
B_n=qB_{n-1}+C.
\]

Expanding gives

\[
B_n=q^nB_0+C(1+q+\cdots+q^{n-1}).
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{B_n=q^nB_0+C\frac{q^n-1}{q-1}}\quad(q\ne1),
\]

and

\[
\boxed{B_n=B_0+nC}\quad(q=1).
\]

For beginning-of-period contributions, each payment earns one additional period of growth:

\[
\boxed{B_n=q^nB_0+CqG_n.}
\]

These identities assume constant periodic rates and equal signed cash flows.

## 5. Worked example: recurring deposits

Start with \(B_0=1000\), contribute \(C=100\) at each period's end, and earn \(i=0.02\) per period for \(n=3\) periods.

\[
G_3=1+1.02+1.02^2=3.0604.
\]

Therefore,

\[
B_3=1000(1.02)^3+100(3.0604)
=1061.208+306.04
=\boxed{1367.248}.
\]

Direct iteration yields \(B_1=1120\), \(B_2=1242.4\), and \(B_3=1367.248\).

With beginning-of-period contributions, the contribution portion is \(100(1.02)(3.0604)=312.1608\), giving \(B_3=1373.3688\).

## 6. Zero-rate boundary

When \(i=0\), \(q=1\), the quotient \((q^n-1)/(q-1)\) is undefined even though the underlying sum is well-defined. The correct result is \(G_n=n\). The expression also approaches \(n\) continuously as \(q\to1\), but the \(q=1\) case must be handled explicitly in code.

## 7. Numerical reliability near a unit ratio

When \(q\) is close to one, evaluating \((q^n-1)/(q-1)\) directly can lose precision through subtraction of nearly equal numbers. With \(q=1+i\), use

\[
\boxed{G_n=\frac{\operatorname{expm1}(n\operatorname{log1p}(i))}{i}}
\]

for nonzero \(i>-1\), where \(\operatorname{log1p}(i)\) computes \(\ln(1+i)\) and \(\operatorname{expm1}(x)\) computes \(e^x-1\) accurately for small \(x\).

For \(i=0\), return \(n\). For extreme rates and horizons, floating-point overflow remains possible. The Python lab validates inputs and detects nonfinite outputs.

## 8. Financial interpretation and limitations

Finite geometric sums model level periodic payments under a **constant** effective rate. They are the foundation for annuity calculations, savings plans, and simple loan recurrences. They do not by themselves account for fees, variable rates, taxes, payment dates, or stochastic returns. A negative signed contribution represents a withdrawal; no-overdraft feasibility must be checked separately.
