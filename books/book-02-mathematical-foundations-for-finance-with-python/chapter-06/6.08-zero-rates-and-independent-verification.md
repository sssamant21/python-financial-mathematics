## Learning objectives

Evaluate finite financial series at zero interest rates, distinguish valid zero-rate limits from divergent perpetuities, verify closed forms with independently enumerated cash flows, and design checks that detect timing and numerical mistakes.

## 1. Why zero rates require care

Many financial formulas contain a rate in the denominator:

\[
FV_{\mathrm{ord}}=C\frac{(1+r)^n-1}{r},\qquad
PV_{\mathrm{ord}}=C\frac{1-(1+r)^{-n}}{r}.
\]

Direct substitution \(r=0\) produces \(0/0\). This does **not** mean the finite cash-flow values are undefined. It means the quotient formula must be replaced by its limit or by the original finite sum.

At \(r=0\), each of the \(n\) level payments is worth \(C\) at every valuation date:

\[
\boxed{PV_{\mathrm{ord}}=FV_{\mathrm{ord}}=nC}.
\]

An annuity due also has value \(nC\) at zero rate, since payment timing no longer changes value.

## 2. Derive the zero-rate limit

Using the binomial expansion for integer \(n\ge1\),

\[
(1+r)^n=1+nr+O(r^2).
\]

Therefore,

\[
\lim_{r\to0}\frac{(1+r)^n-1}{r}=n.
\]

Similarly,

\[
(1+r)^{-n}=1-nr+O(r^2),
\]

so

\[
\lim_{r\to0}\frac{1-(1+r)^{-n}}{r}=n.
\]

The finite sum provides a second derivation without limits.

## 3. Growing streams at zero discount rate

Let \(C_1\) be the first end-of-period payment and \(g>-1\) the payment growth rate. At \(r=0\),

\[
PV_n=FV_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}C_1(1+g)^k.
\]

For \(g\ne0\),

\[
\boxed{PV_n=FV_n=C_1\frac{(1+g)^n-1}{g}}.
\]

For \(g=0\), the value is \(nC_1\). In general, when \(r=g\) (even when both are nonzero), the growing-stream present value is \(nC_1/(1+r)\), as established in Section 6.5.

## 4. Infinite streams are different

For a **nonzero level perpetuity** with payments \(C\) at times \(1,2,\ldots\), setting \(r=0\) gives

\[
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}C,
\]

whose partial sums \(nC\) have no finite limit. Thus \(C/r\) has **no finite zero-rate extension** for a nonzero perpetual stream. Do not transfer the finite-annuity zero-rate limit to a perpetuity.

A growing perpetuity may converge at a zero discount rate if \(-1<g<0\), because its payments decay geometrically. The correct condition for nonzero first payment remains \(r>g>-1\). For example, with \(r=0\) and \(g=-0.1\), its value is \(C_1/0.1\). This contrasts with a level perpetuity at zero discount rate.

## 5. Independent verification strategy

A reliable implementation should calculate the same result through **different mathematical paths**:

1. **Closed form:** use the derived geometric or annuity expression.
2. **Direct cash-flow sum:** enumerate payment times and discount or compound each payment.
3. **Reconciliation identity:** verify \(FV=PV(1+r)^n\) for finite streams using consistent timing.
4. **Boundary checks:** compare exact zero-rate values with results at small positive and negative rates.
5. **Timing checks:** confirm that beginning-of-period and end-of-period values differ by a factor \(1+r\).

A direct sum and a closed form can share a conceptual modeling error. Verification must also review the payment schedule, units, sign convention, and valuation date.

## 6. Worked example: zero-rate annuity

For \(C=250\) and \(n=8\), at \(r=0\),

\[
PV=FV=8(250)=\boxed{2000}.
\]

This is true for ordinary and due annuities. At a small nonzero rate, the values should approach 2000 continuously as the rate approaches zero.

## 7. Worked example: growing stream at zero discount

Let \(C_1=100\), \(g=0.02\), \(n=3\), and \(r=0\). The payments are \(100,102,104.04\), so

\[
PV=FV=100+102+104.04=\boxed{306.04}.
\]

The geometric formula gives the same value:

\[
100\frac{1.02^3-1}{0.02}=306.04.
\]

## 8. Numerical implementation

For nonzero rates near zero, direct subtraction of almost equal powers can lose significant digits. Use

\[
\frac{\operatorname{expm1}(n\operatorname{log1p}(r))}{r}
\]

for the ordinary-annuity future-value factor and

\[
\frac{-\operatorname{expm1}(-n\operatorname{log1p}(r))}{r}
\]

for its present-value factor. At exactly zero, return \(n\). Independent direct sums should use \`math.fsum\` where practical.

Absolute tolerances matter near a zero-valued result; relative tolerances alone are unreliable there. Validate that inputs and outputs are finite and that the effective discount rate exceeds \(-1\) for finite-stream formulas.
