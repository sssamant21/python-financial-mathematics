## Learning objectives

Distinguish truncation error from floating-point error, derive exact geometric tails, use absolute and relative tolerances, choose a finite horizon from an error budget, and verify approximations against independent calculations.

## 1. Infinite models and finite computations

A computer cannot add infinitely many payments. Instead, it evaluates the first \(N\) terms of a convergent series and estimates the omitted tail.

For a convergent series,

\[
S_\infty=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_k,\qquad
S_N=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}a_k,
\]

the **signed truncation error** is

\[
R_N=S_\infty-S_N.
\]

The **absolute error** is \(|R_N|\). If \(S_\infty\ne0\), the relative error is \(|R_N|/|S_\infty|\).

This error is distinct from floating-point rounding error: even an exact evaluation of \(S_N\) leaves out the tail.

## 2. Exact geometric tail

For \(a_k=aq^k\) with \(|q|<1\),

\[
S_\infty=\frac{a}{1-q},\qquad
S_N=a\frac{1-q^N}{1-q}.
\]

Subtracting yields the exact remainder

\[
\boxed{R_N=\frac{aq^N}{1-q}}.
\]

Consequently,

\[
\boxed{|R_N|=\frac{|a||q|^N}{|1-q|}}.
\]

When \(a\ne0\), the relative error of the first \(N\) terms is simply

\[
\boxed{\frac{|R_N|}{|S_\infty|}=|q|^N}.
\]

For \(q<0\), the signed remainder alternates, so use its absolute magnitude for a tolerance check.

## 3. Choosing a horizon for a relative tolerance

Suppose \(0<|q|<1\) and a relative tolerance \(0<\varepsilon<1\) is required. The condition is

\[
|q|^N\le\varepsilon.
\]

Taking logarithms gives

\[
N\ln|q|\le\ln\varepsilon.
\]

Because \(\ln|q|<0\), dividing reverses the inequality:

\[
\boxed{N\ge\frac{\ln\varepsilon}{\ln|q|}}.
\]

The smallest integer horizon is the ceiling of the right-hand side, subject to a numerical verification. When \(q=0\), one term suffices for nonzero \(a\); when \(a=0\), the series is identically zero.

## 4. Choosing a horizon for an absolute tolerance

For \(|a|>0\), \(0<|q|<1\), and an absolute budget \(\delta>0\),

\[
\frac{|a||q|^N}{|1-q|}\le\delta.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
|q|^N\le\frac{\delta|1-q|}{|a|}.
\]

If the right side is at least one, \(N=0\) already meets the tolerance. Otherwise,

\[
\boxed{N\ge
\frac{\ln(\delta|1-q|/|a|)}{\ln|q|}}.
\]

A practical implementation should verify the returned horizon and adjust for floating-point rounding near the threshold.

## 5. Financial application: level perpetuity tail

For a positive periodic discount rate \(r\) and level end-of-period payment \(C\),

\[
PV_\infty=\frac{C}{r},\qquad
PV_N=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{C}{(1+r)^k}.
\]

The exact omitted time-zero tail is

\[
\boxed{T_N=\frac{C}{r}(1+r)^{-N}}.
\]

For nonzero \(C\), the relative error is

\[
\boxed{\frac{|T_N|}{|PV_\infty|}=(1+r)^{-N}}.
\]

For a 5% effective rate and a 1% relative error budget, the minimum horizon satisfies

\[
N\ge\frac{\ln(0.01)}{-\ln(1.05)}\approx94.39.
\]

Thus at least **95 payments** are required to keep the omitted present value within 1% of the full perpetuity value.

## 6. Financial application: growing perpetuity tail

For \(r>g>-1\), nonzero first payment \(C_1\), and ratio

\[
q=\frac{1+g}{1+r}\in(0,1),
\]

the infinite growing-perpetuity present value is \(C_1/(r-g)\). After the first \(N\) payments,

\[
\boxed{T_N=\frac{C_1}{r-g}q^N}.
\]

Its relative truncation error is exactly \(q^N\). If growth is close to discounting, \(q\) is near one and a large horizon may be necessary.

## 7. Numerical example and independent check

Let \(a=1\), \(q=0.8\), and \(N=4\). The infinite sum is 5, the four-term sum is 2.952, and

\[
R_4=5-2.952=\boxed{2.048}.
\]

The relative error is \(2.048/5=0.4096=0.8^4\). A visually plausible partial sum can still be far from its limit.

A separate check can compute \(S_N\) by explicit term-by-term summation and compare \(S_N+R_N\) to the closed-form infinite value.

## 8. Error budgets and numerical safeguards

A production-quality valuation should specify a tolerance in the **units of the result** (for example, currency) and, when appropriate, a relative tolerance. Relative error is undefined when the reference value is zero; use an absolute criterion in that case.

Avoid blindly trusting a formula when its terms are nearly equal. Use \`math.log1p\` and \`math.expm1\` for small rates, and \`math.fsum\` for long sums. An analytic tail bound controls truncation error, not model error, parameter uncertainty, or all floating-point error.

A discounting model may be mathematically convergent but economically unrealistic. Error budgets cannot fix incorrect payment timing, an implausible discount rate, or a non-sustainable perpetual stream.

### Worked solutions

1. Signed: \(S_\infty-S_N\); absolute: its magnitude; relative: absolute error divided by \(|S_\infty|\) when nonzero.
2. \(R_N=aq^N/(1-q)\), for \(|q|<1\).
3. \(5\).
4. \(S_4=2.952\), \(R_4=2.048\).
5. \(0.8^4=0.4096\), or 40.96%.
6. \(N_{\min}=\lceil\ln\varepsilon/\ln|q|\rceil\) for \(0<|q|<1\), with a final tolerance check.
7. \(95\) periods.
8. Truncation error is the mathematical tail omitted from an assumed series; model error arises when the assumptions do not represent the actual financial process.
