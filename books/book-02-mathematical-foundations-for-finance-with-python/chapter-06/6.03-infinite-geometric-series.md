## Learning objectives

Define an infinite series through partial sums; prove the geometric convergence criterion; derive its limit and finite remainder; distinguish mathematical convergence from practical financial assumptions; and verify discounted cash-flow calculations in Python.

## 1. Infinite series are limits of finite sums

For a real ratio \(q\), define the partial sum

\[
S_N=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^k.
\]

The infinite geometric series \(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^k\) **converges** if the limit of its partial sums exists and is finite:

\[
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^k=\lim_{N\to\infty}S_N.
\]

The infinity symbol is not a last index to substitute. It describes a limiting process.

## 2. Convergence criterion

From Section 6.2, for \(q\ne1\),

\[
S_N=\frac{1-q^N}{1-q}.
\]

When \(|q|<1\), the powers \(q^N\) approach zero. Therefore,

\[
\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^k=\frac{1}{1-q}\quad\text{if }|q|<1.}
\]

For \(q=1\), the partial sums equal \(N\), so they diverge. For \(q=-1\), the partial sums alternate between one and zero and do not converge. For \(|q|>1\), the terms fail to approach zero, so the series diverges.

Thus **\(|q|<1\) is necessary and sufficient** for convergence of the geometric series with initial term one.

For any constant \(a\),

\[
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a q^k=\frac{a}{1-q}
\quad (|q|<1).
\]

If \(a=0\), the series is identically zero regardless of \(q\); this is a trivial exception to the usual nonzero-first-term convergence statement.

## 3. Remainder after a finite number of terms

For \(|q|<1\), after taking terms \(k=0,\ldots,N-1\),

\[
R_N=\sum_{k=N}^{\infty}q^k
=\boxed{\frac{q^N}{1-q}}.
\]

The absolute truncation error is

\[
\boxed{|R_N|=\frac{|q|^N}{|1-q|}}.
\]

For a scaled series \(a\sum q^k\), multiply the error by \(|a|\). This exact remainder is more informative than merely observing that partial sums appear stable.

When \(0<q<1\), the first \(N\) terms understate the positive infinite sum; for negative \(q\), the remainder alternates in sign.

## 4. Worked example

Let \(q=0.8\). The infinite sum is

\[
S_\infty=\frac{1}{1-0.8}=5.
\]

The first four terms sum to

\[
S_4=1+0.8+0.64+0.512=2.952.
\]

The remaining tail is

\[
R_4=\frac{0.8^4}{1-0.8}=2.048.
\]

Indeed, \(2.952+2.048=5\). Four terms are not yet a close approximation when the ratio is near one.

## 5. Finance: a perpetuity as a discounted series

Suppose a constant payment \(C\) arrives **at the end of each period**, beginning at period 1. Let the effective discount rate per period be \(r>0\). The present value is

\[
PV=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{C}{(1+r)^k}.
\]

Set \(q=(1+r)^{-1}\), so \(0<q<1\). Then

\[
PV=C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q^k
=C\frac{q}{1-q}
=\boxed{\frac{C}{r}}.
\]

This is a mathematical valuation under fixed-payment, fixed-discount-rate, infinite-horizon assumptions. It is not a guarantee that a real institution can pay forever.

For \(C=100\) and \(r=0.05\), the present value is \(2000\).

## 6. Beginning-of-period perpetuity

If the first payment occurs **immediately** at time zero, the present value becomes

\[
PV_{\mathrm{due}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{C}{(1+r)^k}
=\boxed{\frac{C(1+r)}{r}}.
\]

For \(C=100,r=0.05\), this is \(2100\). The timing difference is exactly the immediate payment \(C\).

## 7. Discounted tail and finite-horizon approximation

For an end-of-period perpetuity, the present value of payments **after** period \(N\) is

\[
\boxed{T_N=\frac{C}{r}(1+r)^{-N}}.
\]

The present value of the first \(N\) payments is

\[
PV_N=\frac{C}{r}\left(1-(1+r)^{-N}\right).
\]

Consequently \(PV=PV_N+T_N\). This identity supplies an independent check of a truncated cash-flow valuation.

For \(C=100,r=0.05,N=10\), the total value is \(2000\); the tail is approximately \(1227.8265\), and the first ten payments are approximately \(772.1735\).

## 8. When the formula must not be used

- If \(r=0\) and \(C>0\), undiscounted equal payments diverge; \(C/r\) is undefined.
- If \(-1<r<0\), the discount factor exceeds one, so the fixed-payment series does not converge for nonzero \(C\).
- Variable payment growth, changing discount rates, finite survival probabilities, default, inflation, and credit risk require additional models.
- A finite-looking numerical output does not establish convergence without the mathematical condition.

## 10. Exercises

**1.** Define convergence of an infinite series using partial sums.

**2.** Determine whether \(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(0.6)^k\) converges and compute its sum.

**3.** Determine whether \(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^k\) converges.

**4.** Compute the first four terms of \(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(0.8)^k\) and its exact remainder.

**5.** Compute the present value of a 100-per-period end-of-period perpetuity at a 5% effective rate.

**6.** Compute the corresponding beginning-of-period perpetuity.

**7.** Derive the discounted tail after \(N\) end-of-period payments.

**8.** Explain why a zero discount rate does not produce a finite value for an infinite stream of positive level payments.

### Worked solutions

1. The series converges if the limit of its finite partial sums exists and is finite.
2. Yes, because \(|0.6|<1\); sum \(1/(1-0.6)=2.5\).
3. No; partial sums alternate between one and zero.
4. \(S_4=2.952\), \(R_4=0.8^4/0.2=2.048\).
5. \(100/0.05=2000\).
6. \(100(1.05)/0.05=2100\).
7. \(T_N=\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}C(1+r)^{-k}=(C/r)(1+r)^{-N}\).
8. Each payment remains undiscounted, so partial sums increase without bound.
