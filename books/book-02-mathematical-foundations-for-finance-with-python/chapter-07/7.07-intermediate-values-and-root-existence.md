## Learning objectives

## 1. The Intermediate Value Theorem

**Theorem.** Suppose \(f\) is continuous on the closed interval \([a,b]\). For any number \(y\) between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\), there exists at least one \(c\in[a,b]\) such that \(f(c)=y\).

In particular, if \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\) have opposite signs, then some \(c\in(a,b)\) satisfies

\[
\boxed{f(c)=0.}
\]

This is the **root-existence corollary** of the IVT. It is an existence theorem: it does not identify the root, count all roots, or guarantee that there is only one.

A zero endpoint value is already a root; a strict sign change is sufficient for an interior root but not necessary for root existence.

## 2. Why continuity is essential

Consider \(f(x)=x^2-2\) on \([1,2]\). Since this polynomial is continuous, \(f(1)=-1\) and \(f(2)=2\), the IVT guarantees a root between 1 and 2. In fact the root is \(\sqrt2\).

Without continuity the conclusion can fail. Define

\[
g(x)=\begin{cases}-1,&x<0,\\1,&x\ge0.\end{cases}
\]

On \([-1,1]\), the endpoint values have opposite signs, but \(g(x)\) is never zero. The jump at zero invalidates the IVT hypothesis.

The converse also fails: \(h(x)=x^2\) has a root at zero even though its values at \(-1\) and \(1\) have the same sign. A sign-change test can miss even-multiplicity roots.

## 3. Existence is not uniqueness

For \(f(x)=x^3-x\) on \([-2,2]\), the endpoint values have opposite signs, but the function has three roots: \(-1,0,1\).

A separate property such as **strict monotonicity** ensures uniqueness: a strictly increasing or strictly decreasing function cannot take the value zero at two different inputs. If a continuous strictly monotone function changes sign across a bracket, exactly one root lies inside.

## 4. Bisection: turning a theorem into an algorithm

Given a continuous function and a bracket \([a,b]\) with \(f(a)f(b)<0\):

1. Compute the midpoint \(m=a+(b-a)/2\).
2. If \(f(m)=0\), stop.
3. Otherwise keep the half whose endpoints still have opposite signs.
4. Repeat until the bracket is sufficiently narrow or a justified residual criterion is met.

After \(n\) successful halvings, the bracket width is

\[
\boxed{b_n-a_n=\frac{b_0-a_0}{2^n}}.
\]

The midpoint is within half the final bracket width of any root contained in that bracket. Thus, to guarantee midpoint absolute error at most \(\varepsilon>0\), it suffices to choose \(n\) so that

\[
\frac{b_0-a_0}{2^{n+1}}\le\varepsilon.
\]

The sign change is preserved mathematically under continuity; floating-point arithmetic introduces additional practical limits.

## 5. Worked example: square-root approximation

Solve \(x^2-2=0\) on \([1,2]\). The initial signs are negative and positive.

| Iteration | Midpoint | \(m^2-2\) | Updated bracket |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | 1.5 | 0.25 | [1, 1.5] |
| 2 | 1.25 | -0.4375 | [1.25, 1.5] |
| 3 | 1.375 | -0.109375 | [1.375, 1.5] |
| 4 | 1.4375 | 0.06640625 | [1.375, 1.4375] |

The interval contracts while retaining the root. Continued bisection approximates \(\sqrt2\).

## 6. Financial application: a break-even contribution

Suppose a stylized project has a fixed cost of 1,000, a variable cost of 12 per unit, and revenue of 20 per unit. Profit at sales volume \(q\) is

\[
\Pi(q)=20q-(1000+12q)=8q-1000.
\]

Since \(\Pi(100)=-200\) and \(\Pi(200)=600\), continuity guarantees a break-even point between 100 and 200 units. Solving directly gives \(q=125\). The example uses a continuous volume approximation; actual unit sales may be integer-valued.

## 7. Financial application: yield to maturity

For a fixed-income security with positive dated cash flows \(C_k>0\) at integer periods \(k=1,\ldots,n\), consider the price equation

\[
P=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{C_k}{(1+y)^k},\qquad y>-1.
\]

Define the residual

\[
F(y)=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{C_k}{(1+y)^k}-P.
\]

For \(P>0\), \(F\) is continuous on \(y>-1\). It is also strictly decreasing because each positive discounted payment decreases as the yield increases. Thus any sign-changing bracket contains **exactly one** root.

As \(y\to-1^+\), the present value diverges to \(+\infty\), while as \(y\to+\infty\), the present value tends to zero. For positive price and at least one positive cash flow, a unique yield exists on \((-1,\infty)\).

**Worked example.** A two-period instrument pays 50 after year 1 and 1,050 after year 2. If its price is 1,000, then

\[
F(y)=\frac{50}{1+y}+\frac{1050}{(1+y)^2}-1000.
\]

At \(y=0\), \(F(0)=100\); at \(y=0.1\), \(F(0.1)\approx-86.77686\). The root lies in \((0,0.1)\). Substituting \(y=0.05\) gives \(F(0.05)=0\), so the yield is exactly 5% per period.

These formulas assume a simple periodic-yield convention with positive cash flows and no embedded options, taxes, accrued interest, or market conventions.

## 8. Practical numerical safeguards

- Verify continuity analytically on the chosen interval; sign changes alone are not enough.
- Check finite endpoint values and return an endpoint immediately when it is a root.
- Reject invalid brackets rather than inventing a root.
- Use a midpoint formula that reduces overflow risk, while guarding against indistinguishable floating-point endpoints.
- Stop when the bracket width meets a specified absolute tolerance; a small residual alone does not always imply a small root error.
- Set a maximum iteration count and return a convergence status or raise an explicit error.
- Record the final bracket, residual, iteration count, and tolerance in operational use.

## 9. Exercises

**1.** State the IVT and its continuity hypothesis.

**2.** Show that \(x^2-2\) has a root in \((1,2)\).

**3.** Why does the step function \(g(x)=-1\) for \(x<0\) and \(1\) otherwise not satisfy the IVT conclusion on \([-1,1]\)?

**4.** Does a sign-changing bracket guarantee a unique root?

**5.** Can a continuous function have a root without changing sign?

**6.** Starting with a bracket of width 8, what is its width after five bisection halvings?

**7.** How many halvings guarantee midpoint error at most \(10^{-3}\) from an initial bracket of width 1?

**8.** Find break-even units for revenue 20 per unit, variable cost 12, and fixed cost 1,000.

**9.** Write the yield residual for payments of 50 and 1,050 in years 1 and 2 and a price of 1,000.

**10.** Explain why the positive-cash-flow yield residual is strictly decreasing for \(y>-1\).

### Worked solutions

1. A continuous function on a closed interval takes every intermediate value between its endpoint values.
2. The function is continuous, with values \(-1\) and \(2\) at the endpoints; therefore it crosses zero.
3. It jumps at zero and is not continuous.
4. No; a continuous function can have multiple roots in the bracket.
5. Yes: \(x^2\) has a root at zero but is nonnegative on both sides.
6. \(8/2^5=0.25\).
7. Find \(n\) with \(1/2^{n+1}\le10^{-3}\); \(n=9\) suffices.
8. \(q=1000/(20-12)=125\).
9. \(F(y)=50/(1+y)+1050/(1+y)^2-1000\).
10. Increasing the yield decreases every positive discounted payment; therefore their sum, minus the fixed price, strictly decreases.
