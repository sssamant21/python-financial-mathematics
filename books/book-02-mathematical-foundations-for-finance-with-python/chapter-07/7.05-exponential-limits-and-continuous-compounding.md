## Learning objectives

Derive continuous compounding from increasingly frequent discrete compounding; distinguish nominal rates, effective annual rates, and continuously compounded rates; use exponential limits correctly; evaluate accumulation and discount factors with numerically stable Python; and explain the assumptions behind the model.

## 1. From periodic to continuous compounding

Suppose a principal \(P\) earns a nominal annual rate \(j\), compounded \(m\) times per year. After \(t\) years, where \(mt\) is an integer, its value is

\[
A_m(t)=P\left(1+\frac jm\right)^{mt}.
\]

For fixed real \(j\) and \(t\ge0\), and sufficiently large \(m\) so that \(1+j/m>0\), take logarithms:

\[
\log\frac{A_m(t)}P=mt\log\left(1+\frac jm\right).
\]

Set \(u=j/m\). The key small-change limit is

\[
\lim_{u\to0}\frac{\log(1+u)}u=1.
\]

Consequently,

\[
\lim_{m\to\infty}mt\log\left(1+\frac jm\right)=jt,
\]

and exponentiation gives

\[
\boxed{\lim_{m\to\infty}A_m(t)=Pe^{jt}}.
\]

This is **continuous compounding**. The limiting formula is meaningful for real \(t\), even when the discrete model originally specifies only whole compounding intervals.

## 2. The exponential limit

The familiar identity

\[
\boxed{e^x=\lim_{m\to\infty}\left(1+\frac xm\right)^m}
\]

holds for every fixed real \(x\) with sufficiently large \(m\) to keep the base positive. Raising this expression to the power \(t\) yields the accumulation factor \(e^{xt}\).

The case \(x=0\) gives 1. For negative \(x\), the continuous factor remains positive and decreases as \(t\) grows. A negative continuously compounded rate is mathematically valid but must be justified economically.

## 3. Effective annual and continuous rates

If \(\delta\) is a continuously compounded annual rate, the corresponding effective annual rate is

\[
\boxed{i=e^\delta-1}.
\]

Conversely, for an effective annual rate \(i>-1\),

\[
\boxed{\delta=\log(1+i)}.
\]

For nominal annual rate \(j\) compounded \(m\) times annually,

\[
i_m=\left(1+\frac jm\right)^m-1,\qquad
\delta_m=m\log\left(1+\frac jm\right).
\]

As \(m\to\infty\), \(i_m\to e^j-1\) and \(\delta_m\to j\). The symbols \(j\), \(i\), and \(\delta\) are not interchangeable unless the compounding convention is explicitly specified.

## 4. Discount factors

A payment \(C\) due at time \(t\ge0\) has continuously compounded present value

\[
\boxed{PV=Ce^{-\delta t}}.
\]

The associated accumulation factor is \(e^{\delta t}\). Their product is exactly 1 in real arithmetic:

\[
e^{\delta t}e^{-\delta t}=1.
\]

For a collection of dated payments \(C_k\) at times \(t_k\),

\[
PV=\sum_k C_ke^{-\delta t_k}.
\]

This deterministic expression assumes a common constant continuously compounded rate. Term structures and stochastic discounting are outside this section's scope.

## 5. Worked example

Let \(P=1000\), \(j=0.06\), and \(t=2\). Then

\[
A_m(2)=1000\left(1+\frac{0.06}{m}\right)^{2m}
\]

approaches

\[
A_\infty(2)=1000e^{0.12}\approx1127.49685.
\]

For \(m=1\), the discrete value is \(1000(1.06)^2=1123.60\). As \(m\) increases, the discrete values approach the continuous limit.

The effective annual rate corresponding to \(\delta=0.06\) is \(e^{0.06}-1\approx0.06183655\), or about 6.183655%.

## 6. Numerical stability

For small \(x\), computing \(\exp(x)-1\) directly can lose precision through subtraction of nearly equal numbers. Use \`math.expm1(x)\`.

Similarly, for small \(u\), use \`math.log1p(u)\` instead of \`math.log(1+u)\`.

Compute discrete accumulation as

\[
A_m(t)=P\exp\left(mt\,\log1p(j/m)\right)
\]

rather than forming a huge power directly. This remains subject to floating-point overflow when the true magnitude exceeds representable range.

To compare discrete and continuous factors, use the relative error

\[
E_m=\frac{|(1+j/m)^{mt}-e^{jt}|}{e^{jt}},
\]

when the continuous factor is finite and nonzero. Numerical agreement for large \(m\) supports the derivation but is not a proof of the limit.

## 7. Financial interpretation and model limits

Continuous compounding is a convenient mathematical convention. It does not imply that a bank actually posts interest continuously, nor does it guarantee a return. Comparisons require aligned rate bases, currency, timing, day-count conventions, fees, and credit assumptions.

For variable deterministic instantaneous rates \(\delta(s)\), the general accumulation factor becomes \(\exp(\int_0^t\delta(s)\,ds)\). Integration is developed later in this book.
