## Learning objectives

Test continuity at a point and on an interval; distinguish removable, jump, infinite, and oscillatory discontinuities; understand one-sided continuity at endpoints; and assess the consequences of discontinuities in financial models and numerical algorithms.

## 1. Definition of continuity

A function \(f\) is **continuous at \(a\)** if all three conditions hold:

1. \(f(a)\) is defined.
2. The finite two-sided limit \(\lim_{x\to a}f(x)\) exists.
3. \(\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)\).

For an interior point of the domain, continuity requires the function value to agree with both one-sided limits. At a domain endpoint, use the appropriate one-sided limit relative to the domain.

For example, \(f(x)=2x+1\) is continuous at \(a=3\): \(f(3)=7\) and \(\lim_{x\to3}(2x+1)=7\).

## 2. Removable discontinuity

Consider

\[
f(x)=\frac{x^2-9}{x-3},\qquad x\ne3.
\]

For \(x\ne3\), factoring gives \(f(x)=x+3\). Therefore,

\[
\lim_{x\to3}f(x)=6,
\]

but \(f(3)\) is undefined. Defining \(f(3)=6\) fills the hole and produces a continuous extension.

A removable discontinuity can also occur when the function is defined at \(a\) but its value differs from the limit. Changing only that value to the limiting value repairs continuity.

## 3. Jump discontinuity

Let

\[
J(x)=
\begin{cases}
2,&x<5,\\
7,&x\ge5.
\end{cases}
\]

Then \(\lim_{x\to5^-}J(x)=2\) and \(\lim_{x\to5^+}J(x)=7\). Because the one-sided limits differ, no two-sided limit exists at \(5\). Assigning a different value at \(x=5\) cannot remove this jump.

## 4. Infinite discontinuity

For \(f(x)=1/(x-2)\), values become unbounded as \(x\) approaches \(2\). Specifically,

\[
\lim_{x\to2^-}f(x)=-\infty,\qquad
\lim_{x\to2^+}f(x)=+\infty.
\]

These symbols describe unbounded behavior; they are not finite real limits. The vertical line \(x=2\) is a vertical asymptote.

## 5. Oscillatory discontinuity

Define \(f(x)=\sin(1/x)\) for \(x\ne0\), with any chosen value at \(x=0\). As \(x\to0\), the function oscillates between \(-1\) and \(1\) without approaching one value. Neither finite one-sided limit exists.

A numerical table with only a few sample points can miss this behavior. Mathematical proof must consider all sufficiently nearby inputs.

## 6. Continuity on intervals

A function is continuous on an open interval when it is continuous at every point inside it. On a closed interval \([a,b]\), continuity requires right-continuity at \(a\), left-continuity at \(b\), and ordinary two-sided continuity at interior points.

Polynomials are continuous everywhere on the real line. Rational functions are continuous wherever their denominators are nonzero. Sums, products, and compositions of continuous functions remain continuous where the operations are defined; quotients additionally require nonzero denominators.

## 7. Financial example: a fee threshold

Consider a hypothetical transaction fee

\[
F(A)=
\begin{cases}
5,&0\le A<1000,\\
10,&A\ge1000.
\end{cases}
\]

The fee jumps at \(A=1000\), so it is discontinuous there. A numerical solver or optimizer that assumes continuity can behave unpredictably near the threshold. In particular, interpolation across the jump may predict a fee that the schedule never actually charges.

A different fee schedule can be continuous but not differentiable. For example, \(G(A)=0.01\max(A-1000,0)\) is continuous at \(1000\), but has different slopes on either side. Continuity does not imply differentiability.

## 8. Financial example: a removable zero-rate singularity

For a fixed positive integer \(n\), define

\[
A_n(r)=\frac{(1+r)^n-1}{r},\quad r\ne0.
\]

The expression has a removable singularity at \(r=0\), since

\[
\lim_{r\to0}A_n(r)=n.
\]

Defining \(A_n(0)=n\) yields a continuous annuity-factor function for \(r>-1\). This extension applies to a **finite** stream; it does not assign a finite value to a nonzero level perpetuity at zero interest.

For reliable computation near zero, use \`math.expm1(n * math.log1p(r)) / r\` and handle exact zero separately.

## 9. Verification method

To classify a suspected discontinuity at \(a\):

1. Check whether \(f(a)\) exists.
2. Derive or bound left-hand and right-hand behavior.
3. Determine whether the two sides converge to the same finite value.
4. Compare the common limit, if any, with \(f(a)\).
5. State the type of discontinuity and whether a point-value change can repair it.
6. Independently sample nearby values, while treating numerical results as supporting evidence rather than proof.
