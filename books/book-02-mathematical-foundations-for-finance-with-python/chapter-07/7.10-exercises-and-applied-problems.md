## Chapter review and learning outcomes

This assessment consolidates Sections 7.1–7.9: two-sided and one-sided limits, removable and jump discontinuities, asymptotes, exponential limits, continuous compounding, stable small-change calculations, the Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT), bisection, and the difference between numerical evidence and proof.

Complete the problems before consulting the solutions. Use a calculator or the [assessment lab](code/section_7_10.py) to check numerical results, but explain the mathematical assumptions independently.

## Part A — Conceptual and analytical exercises

**1. Limit versus function value.** Let \(f(x)=(x^2-4)/(x-2)\) for \(x\ne2\). Determine \(\lim_{x\to2}f(x)\), \(f(2)\), and a continuous extension.

**2. One-sided limits.** Let \(g(x)=x+1\) for \(x<1\) and \(g(x)=3x\) for \(x\ge1\). Find the left-hand limit, right-hand limit, and \(g(1)\). Is \(g\) continuous at 1?

**3. Removable versus jump.** Explain how a hole differs from a jump and give an example of each.

**4. Infinite limits.** Evaluate the one-sided behavior of \(h(x)=1/(x-5)\) as \(x\to5^-\) and \(x\to5^+\).

**5. Limits at infinity.** Find \(\lim_{x\to\infty}(4x^2+3)/(2x^2+1)\) and identify its horizontal asymptote.

**6. Growth comparison.** Determine \(\lim_{x\to\infty}x/e^x\) and explain why the result is consistent with the growth hierarchy.

**7. Exponential limit.** Evaluate \(\lim_{m\to\infty}(1+0.08/m)^m\).

**8. Small-change logarithm.** Evaluate \(\lim_{h\to0}\log(1+h)/h\) and explain its relevance to log returns.

**9. Small-change power.** Evaluate \(\lim_{h\to0}((1+h)^6-1)/h\).

**10. IVT assumptions.** State the hypotheses needed to guarantee a zero between \(a\) and \(b\). Does the theorem guarantee uniqueness?

## Part B — Financial applications

**11. Discrete versus continuous accumulation.** A principal of 2,500 earns a nominal 6% annual rate for three years. Calculate its future value under annual compounding and under continuous compounding.

**12. Continuous effective rate.** Convert a continuously compounded annual rate of 5% into an effective annual rate.

**13. Effective to continuous.** Convert an effective annual rate of 8% into its equivalent continuously compounded rate.

**14. Continuous discounting.** Find the present value of 10,000 payable in four years at a continuously compounded annual rate of 3%.

**15. Negative rates.** Compute the accumulation factor for a continuous rate of \(-1\%\) over two years and interpret it.

**16. Log versus simple return.** An asset increases from 100 to 103. Find its simple return and continuously compounded (log) return.

**17. Rate-shock approximation.** For a continuously discounted payment at \(t=7\), approximate the relative PV change when the continuous rate rises by 10 basis points. Compare with the exact result.

**18. Zero-rate annuity.** Find the limit as \(r\to0\) of \(((1+r)^{10}-1)/r\). Explain why direct substitution fails.

**19. Break-even root.** A project has fixed cost 1,800, variable cost 15 per unit, and revenue 24 per unit. Establish a sign-changing bracket and find the break-even sales volume.

**20. Bond yield.** A two-year instrument pays 60 at the end of year 1 and 1,060 at the end of year 2. Its price is 1,000. Define the yield residual and solve for the annual effective yield.

## Part C — Proof, numerical investigation, and reliability

**21. Counterexample to numerical proof.** Explain why evaluating a function at many sample points does not establish continuity everywhere. Supply a discontinuous counterexample.

**22. Root without sign change.** Show that \(f(x)=x^2\) has a root but no sign change around zero.

**23. Bisection error.** Starting with a bracket of width 2, how many halvings suffice to guarantee midpoint error no greater than \(10^{-5}\)?

**24. Uniqueness.** Explain why a bond-yield residual with strictly positive cash flows is strictly decreasing on \(y>-1\).

**25. Stability.** Explain when to use \`math.expm1\` and \`math.log1p\`, and why a tiny residual is not necessarily a tight root-error guarantee.

## Worked solutions

**1.** For \(x\ne2\), \(f(x)=x+2\). The limit is 4; \(f(2)\) is undefined. Set \(f(2)=4\) to extend continuously.

**2.** The left-hand limit is 2, the right-hand limit is 3, and \(g(1)=3\). Since the one-sided limits differ, the two-sided limit does not exist and the function is not continuous at 1.

**3.** A removable discontinuity has a finite two-sided limit but a missing or mismatched value, e.g. \((x^2-1)/(x-1)\) at 1. A jump has distinct finite one-sided limits, e.g. \(-1\) for \(x<0\) and \(1\) for \(x\ge0\).

**4.** \(h(x)\to-\infty\) from the left and \(+\infty\) from the right. There is a vertical asymptote \(x=5\).

**5.** Divide numerator and denominator by \(x^2\); the limit is \(4/2=2\), giving horizontal asymptote \(y=2\).

**6.** The limit is 0 because exponential growth eventually dominates polynomial growth.

**7.** The limit is \(e^{0.08}\), approximately 1.083287.

**8.** The limit is 1. Therefore \(\log(1+h)\approx h\) for small \(h>-1\), so simple and log returns are close for small returns.

**9.** The limit is 6 by the generalized power limit.

**10.** Continuity on \([a,b]\) and opposite signs at the endpoints guarantee at least one interior root. The IVT does not guarantee uniqueness.

**11.** Annual: \(2500(1.06)^3=2977.54\). Continuous: \(2500e^{0.18}\approx2993.04\). The nominal rates are equal but the effective returns differ.

**12.** \(e^{0.05}-1\approx0.0512711\), or 5.12711%.

**13.** \(\log(1.08)\approx0.0769610\), or 7.69610%.

**14.** \(10000e^{-0.12}\approx8869.20\).

**15.** \(e^{-0.02}\approx0.9801987\); the accumulated amount is about 98.02% of principal, absent other cash flows.

**16.** Simple return \(=0.03\); log return \(=\log(1.03)\approx0.0295588\).

**17.** Ten basis points equal 0.001. First-order relative change \(\approx-7(0.001)=-0.007\), or \(-0.7\%\). Exact change \(e^{-0.007}-1\approx-0.00697556\).

**18.** The limit is 10, because the quotient equals \(\sum_{k=0}^9(1+r)^k\) for \(r\ne0\). Direct substitution produces \(0/0\), an undefined expression.

**19.** Profit \(\Pi(q)=(24-15)q-1800=9q-1800\). At \(q=100\), profit is \(-900\); at \(q=300\), profit is \(900\). The unique break-even volume is \(q=200\) units.

**20.** \(F(y)=60/(1+y)+1060/(1+y)^2-1000\), for \(y>-1\). \(F(0)=120>0\), \(F(0.2)<0\). Substitution gives \(F(0.06)=0\), so the yield is 6%.

**21.** Finite sampling cannot inspect every point. The function \(s(x)=0\) for \(x<0\) and \(s(x)=1\) for \(x\ge0\) is discontinuous at zero even if samples omit that point.

**22.** \(x^2=0\) at \(x=0\), but \(x^2>0\) on either side of zero.

**23.** After \(n\) halvings the midpoint error is at most \(2/2^{n+1}\). Require \(2/2^{n+1}\le10^{-5}\), or \(2^n\ge100000\). Thus \(n=17\) suffices.

**24.** Each positive payment \(C_k/(1+y)^k\) strictly decreases as \(y\) increases for \(y>-1\). Their sum minus the fixed price is therefore strictly decreasing.

**25.** Use \`expm1(x)\` to evaluate \(e^x-1\) and \`log1p(x)\` to evaluate \(\log(1+x)\) accurately near zero. A small residual can occur when the function is flat, so root accuracy should also be checked with a justified bracket or conditioning analysis.
