## Learning objectives

Derive foundational small-change limits, recognize indeterminate \(0/0\) forms, apply first-order approximations to financial quantities, distinguish an approximation from an exact identity, and implement stable numerical evaluations near zero.

## 1. Why small changes matter

A limit of the form \(\lim_{h\to0}F(h)\) describes what happens as a perturbation becomes arbitrarily small. In financial models, \(h\) may represent a rate shock, a small return, a discount-rate adjustment, or a change in a model parameter.

A formula can be undefined at \(h=0\) and still have a finite limit there. Substituting zero into a \(0/0\) expression is not a valid calculation; the expression must be analyzed.

## 2. The logarithmic limit

For \(h>-1\),

\[
\boxed{\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\log(1+h)}{h}=1}.
\]

One way to establish the limit is to use the integral representation

\[
\log(1+h)=\int_0^h\frac{1}{1+t}\,dt.
\]

For small positive \(h\), the integrand lies between \(1/(1+h)\) and \(1\), so the quotient lies between those bounds and tends to 1. An analogous squeeze applies for negative \(h\).

Thus \(\log(1+h)\approx h\) for small \(h\). More precisely, the error is of order \(h^2\).

## 3. The exponential limit

\[
\boxed{\lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^h-1}{h}=1}.
\]

This follows from the inverse relationship between the exponential and logarithm, or from the standard exponential limit. Hence \(e^h\approx1+h\) for small \(h\).

Computationally, \`math.expm1(h)\` evaluates \(e^h-1\) accurately when \(h\) is tiny, whereas direct subtraction may lose significant digits.

## 4. The generalized power limit

For a fixed real \(a\), and \(h>-1\),

\[
\boxed{\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(1+h)^a-1}{h}=a}.
\]

Write \((1+h)^a=\exp(a\log(1+h))\) and combine the preceding limits. At \(a=0\), the numerator is identically zero and the limit is 0.

For small \(h\), \((1+h)^a\approx1+ah\). This underlies approximate percentage-change relationships.

## 5. Trigonometric limits

With angles in radians,

\[
\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1,\qquad
\lim_{h\to0}\frac{1-\cos h}{h}=0.
\]

The first is a foundational calculus limit. The second follows from the identity \(1-\cos h=2\sin^2(h/2)\), which also provides a stable way to evaluate \(1-\cos h\) near zero.

## 6. Financial example: small log returns

If \(P_0>0\) and \(P_1=P_0(1+r)\), with \(r>-1\), the simple return is \(r\) and the log return is

\[
\ell=\log(P_1/P_0)=\log(1+r).
\]

For small \(r\), \(\ell\approx r\). The approximation is not exact: for positive \(r\), \(\log(1+r)<r\); for negative \(r\), the log return is also less than the simple return.

For reliable calculations, compute \(\ell\) using \`math.log1p(r)\`, especially when \(|r|\) is small.

## 7. Financial example: sensitivity of discount factors

For a continuously compounded discount factor \(D(\delta,t)=e^{-\delta t}\), consider a small rate shock \(h\):

\[
\frac{D(\delta+h,t)}{D(\delta,t)}=e^{-ht}.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\frac{D(\delta+h,t)-D(\delta,t)}{D(\delta,t)}
=e^{-ht}-1\approx-th.
\]

The exact relative change is \(\operatorname{expm1}(-ht)\). The first-order approximation \(-th\) is accurate when \(|ht|\) is small; its error grows with the shock magnitude and horizon.

## 8. Financial example: a finite annuity at zero interest

For an integer \(n\ge1\),

\[
A_n(r)=\frac{(1+r)^n-1}{r},\quad r\ne0.
\]

The generalized power limit gives

\[
\lim_{r\to0}A_n(r)=n.
\]

Define \(A_n(0)=n\) to extend the finite annuity factor continuously. This is a limit of a finite sum, not a statement about the value of a perpetuity at zero interest.

## 9. Numerical safeguards

- Use \`math.log1p(h)\` rather than \`math.log(1+h)\` near zero.
- Use \`math.expm1(h)\` rather than \`math.exp(h)-1\` near zero.
- Evaluate \((1+h)^a-1\) as \`math.expm1(a*math.log1p(h))\`.
- Handle exact zero analytically to avoid division by zero.
- Check domain restrictions: \(\log(1+h)\) requires \(h>-1\), and real-valued \((1+h)^a\) is straightforward for \(h>-1\).
- Numerical agreement across a finite collection of \(h\) values is evidence, not a proof.
