## Learning objectives

Derive a financial sensitivity, express its units, compare analytic and numerical derivatives, construct a Taylor approximation, and report numerical and model limitations.

## 1. Sensitivity of present value

For a signed amount \(F\), time \(T\ge0\) in years and constant continuously compounded annual rate \(r\), define

\[
P(r)=Fe^{-rT}.
\]

Its first two rate derivatives are

\[
P'(r)=-TF e^{-rT},\qquad P''(r)=T^2F e^{-rT}.
\]

Rates are decimal annual rates. The first derivative is currency per unit change in that decimal rate. For \(F>0,T>0\), PV decreases as the rate rises and the second derivative is positive. A negative cash flow reverses both signs. At \(T=0\), both derivatives are zero.

## 2. Basis points and units

One basis point (bp) is \(0.0001\) in decimal rate units. For a one-bp increase, the first-order predicted change is \(P'(r)\times0.0001\). For \(F=100,T=1,r=0\), \(P=100\), \(P'=-100\), so a one-bp increase predicts approximately -0.01 currency units. This is a local approximation; exact revaluation differs by a higher-order amount.

## 3. Taylor approximations

For rate change \(h\), a first-order approximation is

\[
P(r+h)\approx P(r)+P'(r)h.
\]

A second-order approximation adds \(\tfrac12P''(r)h^2\). For a sufficiently smooth function, the remainder depends on a third derivative at an intermediate point. Neither approximation guarantees a chosen error threshold for arbitrary changes. Compare with exact revaluation for the stated model and report absolute and relative errors where meaningful.

## 4. Numerical differentiation

The central difference

\[
\frac{P(r+h)-P(r-h)}{2h}
\]

approximates \(P'(r)\) for \(h>0\). Under smoothness, its truncation error is of order \(h^2\) as \(h\to0\), while floating-point subtraction and rounding can dominate when \(h\) is too small. Use a step appropriate to scale, verify that \(r+h\) and \(r-h\) are distinct representable floats, and check numerical range.

A step sweep can reveal a stable region but does not prove correctness. Compare numerical results to an independently derived analytic derivative, and document the step and tolerance. The accompanying code rejects nonpositive or unrepresentable perturbations and reports non-finite outputs.

## 5. Interface and assumptions

The lab provides PV, analytic first and second derivatives, central difference, and first/second-order approximations for one cash flow. It accepts finite real inputs, rejects Boolean values and negative time, and reports overflow. It uses floating-point arithmetic; underflow can still yield zero. It does not model changing curves, credit, taxes, market impact or transaction costs.

A production interface would additionally specify error budgets, currency and rate convention, input precision, and observability. A passing numerical check does not validate the financial model.

## 6. Worked example

For \(F=100,T=2,r=0\), \(P=100\), \(P'=-200\), and \(P''=400\). For \(h=0.01\), the first-order estimate is 98 and the second-order estimate is 98.02. Exact model value is \(100e^{-0.02}\approx98.01986733\). The second-order result is close here; it is not always closer for every function or finite-precision input.

## 7. Twenty worked exercises

1. \(P(0)=F\).
2. \(P'(r)=-TF e^{-rT}\).
3. \(P''(r)=T^2F e^{-rT}\).
4. If \(F=100,T=1,r=0\), then \(P'= -100\).
5. Under exercise 4, a one-bp increase predicts a change of -0.01.
6. A basis point is 0.0001 in decimal rate units.
7. At \(T=0\), the rate derivative is zero.
8. For positive \(F,T\), increasing \(r\) reduces PV.
9. For negative \(F,T>0\), increasing \(r\) increases PV.
10. For \(F=100,T=2,r=0\), \(P''=400\).
11. With exercise 10 and \(h=0.01\), first-order PV is 98.
12. Under the same conditions, second-order PV is 98.02.
13. Exact PV is approximately 98.01986733.
14. Central differences use evaluations on both sides of the rate.
15. The finite-difference step must be positive.
16. A step too small may suffer cancellation.
17. A step too large may have substantial truncation error.
18. An invalid perturbation should be reported, not silently adjusted.
19. Relative error is undefined for a zero reference.
20. Agreement with a derivative does not prove the economic assumptions are appropriate.
