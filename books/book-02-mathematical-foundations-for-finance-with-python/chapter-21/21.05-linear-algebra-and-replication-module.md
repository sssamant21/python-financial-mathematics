## Learning objectives

Translate an exposure-matching problem into a linear system, solve a small example, inspect residuals and conditioning, and document the assumptions and limitations of a replication result.

## 1. Replication as a linear system

Suppose columns of a matrix \(A\) describe the exposures of available instruments, while \(b\) describes a target exposure. A vector \(x\) of instrument quantities satisfies

\[
Ax=b.
\]

Each row must describe the same exposure unit for the target and each instrument. Positions may be negative if shorting is allowed. A mathematical exposure match is not necessarily a tradable or affordable portfolio: prices, liquidity, constraints, transaction costs and model risk require separate analysis.

## 2. Worked two-instrument example

Let

\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad b=\begin{pmatrix}3\\5\end{pmatrix}.
\]

The equations are \(x_1+x_2=3\) and \(x_1+2x_2=5\). Subtraction gives \(x_2=2\), then \(x_1=1\). Thus \(x=(1,2)^T\) and \(Ax=(3,5)^T\). The determinant is 1, so the matrix is nonsingular and the exact system has a unique solution.

If instrument prices are \(p=(10,20)\) per unit, this particular position costs \(p^Tx=50\). A target exposure alone does not determine whether that cost is acceptable or whether replication captures every relevant risk.

## 3. Residual and conditioning

For computed solution \(\hat x\), the residual is

\[
r=A\hat x-b.
\]

Inspect its norm in the units of the exposures and relative to an appropriate scale. A small residual shows that the computed solution approximately satisfies the supplied equations. It does not prove the inputs are correct, the model is appropriate, or the solution is accurate when \(A\) is ill-conditioned.

A condition number describes sensitivity of a problem to perturbations under a specified norm. A matrix with nearly dependent columns can have a large condition number: small exposure changes may produce large changes in positions. A determinant alone is not a scale-independent conditioning measure.

## 4. Singular and near-singular systems

If columns are dependent, a square matrix is singular. The system may have no solutions or infinitely many; singularity alone does not decide which. For example, rows \((1,1)\) and \((2,2)\) with target \((2,4)\) admit infinitely many solutions, while target \((2,5)\) is inconsistent.

The companion lab uses a deliberately limited two-by-two direct formula and reports a scale-normalized determinant as a **screening diagnostic**, not a condition number. A small determinant relative to coefficient scale flags a potentially unstable system; the threshold is illustrative. For general systems use a well-tested numerical library and a method appropriate to rank and conditioning. Do not infer general singularity or optimality from this simple screen.

## 5. Interface and policy

The lab accepts exactly two rows of two finite real coefficients and two finite target values; Boolean values are rejected. It returns positions, residual and a normalized determinant diagnostic. It rejects zero coefficient scale and matrices at or below a configurable screening threshold. The threshold is a chosen policy, not proof of exact mathematical singularity. Finite arithmetic can still overflow or lose accuracy; errors must be reported, and no production suitability is claimed.

A general replication module should state dimensions, units, pricing convention, rank policy, permitted shorting, constraints, error budget and solver diagnostics. Least-squares approximations to inconsistent or overdetermined systems require their own objective and interpretation.

## 6. Twenty worked exercises

1. A column of \(A\) represents one instrument's exposure vector.
2. A row corresponds to one exposure dimension.
3. \(Ax=b\) expresses exact target matching.
4. In the worked example, \(x_2=2\).
5. In the worked example, \(x_1=1\).
6. Substitution gives \(Ax=(3,5)^T\).
7. With prices (10,20), the worked portfolio costs 50.
8. The determinant of the worked matrix is 1.
9. A nonsingular square matrix has a unique exact solution.
10. A singular system can have infinitely many solutions.
11. A singular system can instead be inconsistent.
12. A negative quantity represents a short position if allowed.
13. A residual is \(A\hat x-b\).
14. A zero residual for exact arithmetic means the supplied equations are satisfied.
15. A small residual does not guarantee an accurate solution for an ill-conditioned system.
16. A determinant is affected by scale and is not itself a condition number.
17. Two nearly dependent columns can make positions sensitive to input changes.
18. Exposure units must be consistent within each row.
19. Exact exposure matching does not account automatically for costs or liquidity.
20. A passing numerical test does not establish economic suitability.
