## Learning objectives

Express a constrained allocation problem, distinguish feasibility from optimality, derive a small analytic solution, report constraint violations, and design checks for boundaries and numerical limitations.

## 1. Decisions, objective, and constraints

An optimization model chooses decision variables to minimize or maximize an objective over a feasible set. For two weights \(w_1,w_2\), a long-only fully invested allocation requires

\[
w_1+w_2=1,\qquad w_1\ge0,\quad w_2\ge0.
\]

These weights are dimensionless fractions. The constraints define feasibility; they do not identify which feasible allocation is best. The objective, inputs and intended use must also be specified.

## 2. An analytic example

Minimize

\[
f(w_1,w_2)=(w_1-a)^2+(w_2-b)^2
\]

subject to the constraints above, where finite \(a,b\) are target weights. Substitute \(w_2=1-w_1\) and define \(x=w_1\):

\[
g(x)=(x-a)^2+(1-x-b)^2,\qquad 0\le x\le1.
\]

The unconstrained stationary point satisfies \(g'(x)=4x-2a-2(1-b)=0\), hence

\[
x_0=\frac{1+a-b}{2},\qquad
x^\star=\min(1,\max(0,x_0)),\qquad
w^\star=(x^\star,1-x^\star).
\]

Since \(g''(x)=4>0\), the objective is strictly convex; the clipped stationary point is the unique global minimizer for this two-variable problem. Clipping here is a derived solution, not a generic way to fix arbitrary infeasible optimizer outputs.

For \(a=0.7,b=0.2\), \(x_0=0.75\), so the optimum is \((0.75,0.25)\), with objective \(0.005\). For \(a=2,b=0\), \(x_0=1.5\); the constrained optimum lies at \((1,0)\).

## 3. Feasibility diagnostics

For candidate \(w\), report the equality residual

\[
e=w_1+w_2-1
\]

and the largest long-only violation

\[
v=\max(0,-w_1,-w_2).
\]

A numerical feasibility check may accept \(|e|\le\tau\) and \(v\le\tau\), where \(\tau\ge0\) is a documented dimensionless tolerance. Whether that tolerance is operationally acceptable depends on the application. Always retain the actual residual and violation, not only a Boolean.

Feasibility is necessary for this problem but does not prove optimality. For example, \((0.5,0.5)\) is feasible but has a larger objective than \((0.75,0.25)\) for the first example. General problems may need KKT analysis under appropriate assumptions, dual bounds or independent comparison.

## 4. Numerical and economic limits

The companion code solves only the analytic two-weight example. It validates finite real inputs, rejects Boolean values, reports the objective and constraint diagnostics, and detects non-finite intermediate or final results. It does not implement a general optimizer or provide a production allocation recommendation.

Target weights and this squared-distance objective are teaching choices. Real allocations may require transaction costs, uncertainty, liquidity, risk measures, taxes, leverage, regulatory constraints and independently verified inputs.

## 5. Verification plan

Test an interior solution, both boundaries, feasible and infeasible candidates, a negative or non-finite tolerance, and a known objective. Compare against independent hand calculations. Record the exact source, inputs, tolerance, results and reviewer status when checks are run. A solver success flag alone is not a release gate.

## 6. Twenty worked exercises

1. Full investment means the weights sum to 1.
2. Long-only weights are nonnegative.
3. \((0.5,0.5)\) is feasible.
4. \((1.2,-0.2)\) sums to one but violates long-only by 0.2.
5. \((0.4,0.5)\) has equality residual -0.1.
6. Feasibility alone does not prove optimality.
7. For \(a=0.7,b=0.2\), the unconstrained stationary \(x_0\) is 0.75.
8. The corresponding optimal weights are \((0.75,0.25)\).
9. Their objective value is \(0.05^2+0.05^2=0.005\).
10. The feasible candidate \((0.5,0.5)\) has objective \(0.04+0.09=0.13\).
11. For \(a=2,b=0\), the optimal \(x\) is 1.
12. For \(a=0,b=2\), the optimal \(x\) is 0.
13. \(g''(x)=4\), establishing strict convexity.
14. The derived clipped solution is globally optimal for this specific problem.
15. A negative feasibility tolerance is invalid.
16. A tolerance is dimensionless for weight constraints.
17. A small violation may still be unacceptable in a particular business context.
18. An infeasible problem is different from a solver that did not converge.
19. A numerical optimum under assumed inputs is not investment advice.
20. Independent validation is separate from an authored test.

## 7. Twelve-step lab

1. Define the decision variables and units.
2. State the objective and constraints.
3. Derive the reduced one-dimensional problem.
4. Compute the stationary point.
5. Apply the interval boundary conditions.
6. Verify the interior known value by hand.
7. Check each boundary case.
8. Compute equality residual and inequality violation.
9. Set and justify a feasibility tolerance.
10. Compare a feasible nonoptimal candidate with the optimum.
11. Exercise invalid and non-finite inputs.
12. Record source, results, tolerances and independent review status when checks run.
