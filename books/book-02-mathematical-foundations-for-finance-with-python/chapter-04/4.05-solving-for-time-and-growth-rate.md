## Learning outcomes

Solve exponential accumulation models for elapsed time, growth rate, and required completed compounding periods. Apply logarithms with valid domains; distinguish exact target times from first attainable whole-period times; recognize impossible or nonunique cases; and implement numerical checks with explicit tolerances.

## 1. Inverting a periodic accumulation model

Let \(P>0\), \(F>0\), periodic growth factor \(g=1+i>0\), and \(n\ge0\) periods:

\[
F=Pg^n.
\]

For \(g\ne1\), applying logarithms yields

\[
\boxed{n=\frac{\ln(F/P)}{\ln g}
=\frac{\ln(F/P)}{\ln(1+i)}}.
\]

This is a **real-valued** number of periods. In a contract with interest credited only at whole periods, it does not necessarily correspond to an attainable posting date.

For \(n>0\), the implied periodic rate is

\[
\boxed{i=(F/P)^{1/n}-1
=\exp\!\left(\frac{\ln(F/P)}n\right)-1}.
\]

When \(n=0\), the observed ratio must be one and the rate is not identifiable from that observation.

### Example: exact doubling time

For \(P=1000\) and \(i=0.05\),

\[
n=\frac{\ln2}{\ln1.05}\approx14.2067.
\]

If interest is posted only at whole periods and the goal is **at least** twice the initial amount, the first qualifying period is \(n=15\), not 14.2067.

## 2. Solving for a nominal annual rate

With \(m\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\) compounding events per year and \(n>0\) completed periods,

\[
F=P(1+j/m)^n.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{j=m\left((F/P)^{1/n}-1\right)}.
\]

The number of elapsed years is \(t=n/m\). Always distinguish the nominal annual rate \(j\), the per-period rate \(j/m\), and the effective annual rate \((1+j/m)^m-1\).

### Example: recovering a monthly nominal rate

A balance increases from 1000 to 1126.82503013 over twelve completed monthly periods. The implied monthly rate is approximately 1%; the corresponding nominal annual rate is approximately 12%, and the effective annual rate is approximately 12.6825%. These are different conventions describing the same one-year accumulation.

## 3. Continuous-time inversion

For \(P,F>0\), a continuous rate \(r\), and elapsed time \(t\ge0\),

\[
F=Pe^{rt}.
\]

For \(r\ne0\),

\[
\boxed{t=\frac{\ln(F/P)}r}.
\]

For \(t>0\),

\[
\boxed{r=\frac{\ln(F/P)}t}.
\]

If \(P=100\) and \(F=150\) after five years, the implied continuous rate is

\[
r=\frac{\ln1.5}{5}\approx0.0810930,
\]

or about 8.1093% per year.

## 4. Reachability and identifiability

An algebraically computed negative time is not feasible when the model only permits future dates.

| Rate / factor | Target | Nonnegative-time interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Positive rate, \(F>P\) | Growth | Reachable at positive time |
| Positive rate, \(F<P\) | Decline | Not reachable |
| Negative rate, \(F<P\) | Decline | Reachable at positive time |
| Negative rate, \(F>P\) | Growth | Not reachable |
| Any nonzero rate, \(F=P\) | No change | Time zero |
| Zero rate, \(F=P\) | No change | Every time works; not unique |
| Zero rate, \(F\ne P\) | Change | Not reachable |

If \(P=0\), the simple multiplicative model remains at zero regardless of rate. An observed zero-to-zero path therefore does not identify a growth rate. For log-based inversion we require strictly positive \(P\) and \(F\).

## 5. Threshold crossing versus exact equality

For an increasing periodic balance, the first whole number of completed periods to **reach or exceed** target \(F\ge P\) is the smallest integer \(n\ge0\) satisfying \(Pg^n\ge F\). Algebraically this is \(\lceil\ln(F/P)/\ln g\rceil\), but direct floating-point rounding near an integer can give the wrong answer.

The reference implementation computes a log-based initial estimate and then checks the neighboring integer candidates using the original growth inequality, rather than trusting the rounded quotient alone. The implementation rejects numerical cases it cannot evaluate reliably. For a decreasing balance, reaching **at most** a lower threshold requires a different inequality and is not silently conflated with the growth threshold.

An exact equality at a whole period is more restrictive than reaching a threshold. For example, with factor 1.1, a target ratio 1.15 is crossed at period 2 but is never exactly 1.15 at any integer period.

## 6. Stable logarithmic evaluation

For values close to each other, the log ratio can be evaluated as

\[
\ln(F/P)=\ln\left(1+\frac{F-P}{P}\right),
\]

using \`math.log1p\` where the intermediate fraction is representable and greater than -1. For small implied rates, \`math.expm1\` avoids subtracting nearly equal floating-point numbers. The reference implementation checks finite inputs, domain restrictions, and numerical overflow. Results are floating-point approximations, not exact financial settlement amounts.

## 8. Exercises

**1.** Derive the real period count from \(F=P(1+i)^n\).

**2.** At 5% growth per period, how many real periods are required to double? What is the first whole completed period that reaches at least double?

**3.** A value changes from 200 to 242 over two periods. Find the implied periodic rate.

**4.** A value grows from 100 to 150 in five years under continuous compounding. Derive the rate.

**5.** Explain why \(P=100\), \(F=80\), and \(i=0.05\) yield no nonnegative period count.

**6.** For a zero rate and unchanged target, is the elapsed time uniquely determined?

**7.** A monthly compounded balance grows from 1000 to \(1000(1.01)^{12}\) over twelve months. Find the nominal annual and effective annual rates.

**8.** Explain why the first completed period that exceeds a target is not necessarily a period of exact equality.

### Worked solutions

1. \(n=\ln(F/P)/\ln(1+i)\) for \(P,F>0\), \(i>-1\), \(i\ne0\).
2. \(\ln2/\ln1.05\approx14.2067\); first qualifying whole period is 15.
3. \(i=(242/200)^{1/2}-1=\sqrt{1.21}-1=0.10\).
4. \(r=\ln(1.5)/5\approx0.0810930\).
5. The balance increases with nonnegative time, so it cannot decline to 80.
6. No; every nonnegative time is consistent with an unchanged value at zero rate.
7. Monthly rate 1%; nominal annual 12%; EAR \((1.01)^{12}-1\approx12.6825\%\).
8. The threshold inequality may first hold at an integer even when the target lies strictly between two consecutive balances.
