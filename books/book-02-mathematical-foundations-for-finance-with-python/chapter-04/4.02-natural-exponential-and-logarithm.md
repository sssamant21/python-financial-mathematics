## Learning objectives

Explain the constant \(e\), the natural exponential \(e^x\), and the natural logarithm \(\ln x\); prove their inverse relationships and domains; derive continuously compounded growth and discounting; solve for time and continuous rate; and implement numerically stable, explicitly approximate Python calculations.

## 1. From discrete growth to the natural exponential

Section 4.1 modeled repeated growth as \(V_n=V_0(1+r)^n\). If a nominal annual rate \(r\) is compounded \(m\) times per year for \(t\) years, the corresponding model is

\[
V_m(t)=V_0\left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{mt}.
\]

For a nonnegative nominal rate and increasing \(m\), this expression approaches

\[
\boxed{V(t)=V_0e^{rt}},
\]

where

\[
e=\lim_{m\to\infty}\left(1+\frac1m\right)^m\approx2.718281828459045.
\]

The limiting formula also extends mathematically to negative continuous rates. A rate must be expressed **per unit of time** matching \(t\).

Continuous compounding is a mathematical convention, not a claim that cash flows are literally credited infinitely often.

## 2. Properties of the exponential function

The function \(f(x)=e^x\) is defined for all real \(x\), is strictly positive, and is strictly increasing. It satisfies

\[
e^0=1,\quad e^{x+y}=e^xe^y,\quad e^{-x}=\frac1{e^x}.
\]

Its range is \((0,\infty)\). For \(V_0>0\), the continuous growth model stays positive; for \(r>0\) it increases with time, for \(r<0\) it decreases, and for \(r=0\) it is constant.

## 3. Natural logarithm as the inverse

The natural logarithm is defined by

\[
\ln:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb R,\qquad \ln(e^x)=x.
\]

Conversely, \(e^{\ln y}=y\) for every \(y>0\). Therefore

\[
\ln1=0,\quad \ln e=1,\quad
\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b,\quad
\ln(a^k)=k\ln a
\]

for \(a,b>0\) and real \(k\). **The real natural logarithm is undefined for zero and negative inputs.** It is incorrect to apply \(\ln\) to a negative balance or zero growth ratio without a separately justified model transformation.

Logarithms turn multiplicative relationships into additive ones, which makes them useful for solving exponential equations.

## 4. Solving for time

Starting with

\[
V(t)=V_0e^{rt},
\]

assume \(V_0>0\), \(V(t)>0\), and \(r\ne0\). Divide by \(V_0\) and apply \(\ln\):

\[
\ln\left(\frac{V(t)}{V_0}\right)=rt.
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{t=\frac{\ln(V(t)/V_0)}{r}}.
\]

**Example:** An amount doubles at a hypothetical continuously compounded annual rate of 6%. The doubling time is

\[
t=\frac{\ln2}{0.06}\approx11.55245\text{ years}.
\]

This is a mathematical projection under a constant rate, not a guaranteed investment outcome.

If \(r=0\), the value is constant; there is no unique finite time to reach the same value, and a different positive target is unreachable. If a positive rate is paired with a smaller target and time must be nonnegative, the algebraic solution is negative and therefore infeasible.

## 5. Solving for a continuous rate

For \(t>0\) and positive initial and final values,

\[
\boxed{r=\frac{\ln(V(t)/V_0)}{t}}.
\]

**Example:** An amount changes from 100 to 121 over two years:

\[
r=\frac{\ln(121/100)}{2}=\ln(1.1)\approx0.09531018,
\]

or about 9.531% per year continuously compounded. This is not the same numerical rate as the effective annual rate of 10%, although both generate the same two-year ratio under their respective conventions.

## 6. Continuous discounting

If a future amount \(F>0\) is due at time \(t\ge0\), with continuously compounded rate \(r\), its present value is

\[
\boxed{PV=Fe^{-rt}}.
\]

For \(F=1000\), \(r=0.05\), and \(t=3\),

\[
PV=1000e^{-0.15}\approx860.70798.
\]

The rate convention must be documented. Discounting with an effective annual rate instead uses a different expression, \(F/(1+i)^t\), for matching period units.

## 7. Numerical accuracy and domain validation

Unlike the exact rational powers in Section 4.1, \(e^x\) and \(\ln x\) generally have irrational values. The standard-library functions \`math.exp\` and \`math.log\` use floating-point approximations.

For ratios close to one, use \`math.log1p(u)\` to evaluate \(\ln(1+u)\) more accurately than first forming \(1+u\). For small \(x\), \`math.expm1(x)\` evaluates \(e^x-1\) without unnecessary cancellation. These functions improve numerical behavior but do not provide arbitrary precision or eliminate overflow/underflow.

The companion lab explicitly validates finite real inputs, positive values where logarithms are required, and positive time where rate inversion is used. It does not silently treat NaN, infinity, or booleans as ordinary financial inputs.

## 9. Exercises

**1.** Explain why \(e^x\) has an inverse over all real inputs and state the inverse's domain.

**2.** Derive the expression for time \(t\) from \(V=V_0e^{rt}\).

**3.** Find the continuous rate that doubles a positive amount over ten years.

**4.** Compute the present-value expression for 500 due in four years at a continuous rate of 3%.

**5.** Show that a continuously compounded rate \(r=\ln(1.05)\) corresponds to an effective one-period rate of 5%.

**6.** Explain why \(\ln0\) and \(\ln(-2)\) are invalid in the real-valued financial model.

**7.** Explain why the expression \(\ln(V/V_0)/r\) cannot be used when \(r=0\).

### Worked answers

1. \(e^x\) is strictly increasing and maps \(\mathbb R\) onto \((0,\infty)\); its inverse is \(\ln x\) for \(x>0\).
2. Divide by \(V_0>0\), take natural logs, then divide by \(r\ne0\): \(t=\ln(V/V_0)/r\).
3. \(r=\ln2/10\approx0.0693147\), or 6.93147% per year continuously compounded.
4. \(PV=500e^{-0.03(4)}=500e^{-0.12}\approx443.4602\).
5. \(e^{\ln1.05}-1=0.05\).
6. Neither zero nor negative numbers belong to the real logarithm's domain.
7. A zero rate makes the balance constant; the denominator vanishes and the time problem requires separate case handling.
