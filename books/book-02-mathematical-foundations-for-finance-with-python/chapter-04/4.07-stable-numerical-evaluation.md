## Scope within Chapter 4

The central topic is reliable evaluation of small returns and near-zero rate changes with `log1p` and `expm1`. Log-space accumulation, arithmetic representations and range limits support that topic. Conditioning is introduced here to distinguish avoidable evaluation error from input sensitivity; Section 4.8 develops growth assumptions, sensitivities and model limitations.

## Learning objectives

Understand floating-point rounding, catastrophic cancellation, overflow, underflow, log-space computation, numerical conditioning, and the precision requirements of financial models. Implement stable Python formulas with \`log1p\`, \`expm1\`, and \`fsum\`.

## 1. Equivalent formulas can behave differently

Mathematically equal expressions need not yield identical floating-point results. Python \`float\` typically uses IEEE 754 binary64 with roughly 15–17 significant decimal digits. Each intermediate operation may round. When subtracting nearly equal quantities, relative precision may be lost.

For financial calculations, record rate units, compounding convention, time units, rounding policy, and validation tolerance. A stable algorithm reduces avoidable numerical error but cannot repair inaccurate inputs.

## 2. Small returns: \`log1p\` and \`expm1\`

A simple return \(r>-1\) corresponds to a log return

\[
\ell=\ln(1+r).
\]

If \(r=10^{-16}\), computing \`math.log(1+r)\` may return zero because the addition rounds to 1.0. Use \`math.log1p(r)\` to calculate the log without first forming that rounded sum.

The reverse conversion is

\[
r=e^\ell-1.
\]

Use \`math.expm1(ell)\` instead of \`math.exp(ell)-1\`, which loses precision when \(\ell\) is small.

**Worked example:** For an effective annual rate of 0.00000001, compute the equivalent continuous rate with \`math.log1p(1e-8)\`. Convert it back with \`math.expm1\`. These calculations are approximate, but avoid unnecessary cancellation.

## 3. Multiplicative returns in log space

For positive factors \(g_t=1+r_t>0\),

\[
G=\prod_{t=1}^n g_t,\qquad
L=\sum_{t=1}^n\ln(g_t),\qquad
G=e^L.
\]

The cumulative simple return is

\[
\boxed{R_{\rm total}=\operatorname{expm1}\left(\sum_{t=1}^n\operatorname{log1p}(r_t)\right)}.
\]

Use \`math.fsum\` for accurate summation. Keeping \(L\) in log space may avoid intermediate product overflow or underflow. If \(L\) itself is outside the representable exponential range, do not force a misleading finite value.

**Worked example:** Returns +100% and -50% have factors 2 and 0.5. Their product is 1, their cumulative log change is \(\ln2+\ln0.5=0\), and their cumulative simple return is zero.

## 4. Stable periodic compounding

With nominal annual rate \(j\), compounding frequency \(m\), and \(n\) completed periods, require \(m\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\), \(n\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), and \(1+j/m>0\).

\[
A=(1+j/m)^n,\qquad
\boxed{\ln A=n\operatorname{log1p}(j/m)}.
\]

For one year, \(n=m\), giving the effective annual rate

\[
\boxed{EAR=\operatorname{expm1}(m\operatorname{log1p}(j/m))}.
\]

For continuous compounding, \(\ln A=rt\). Present value is \(FV\exp(-\ln A)\) when representable. Log-space formulas are useful for extreme horizons but do not eliminate overflow when converting back to currency amounts.

## 5. Inverse problems and conditioning

For \(F=Pe^{rt}\), with positive \(P,F\) and nonzero \(r\),

\[
t=\frac{\ln(F/P)}r.
\]

When \(r\) is close to zero, small measurement errors in \(F/P\) can cause large errors in inferred time. This is **ill-conditioning** of the problem, not just floating-point instability.

Similarly, the periodic implied rate

\[
i=\operatorname{expm1}\left(\frac{\ln(F/P)}n\right)
\]

benefits from \`expm1\`, but remains sensitive to imprecise input measurements.

## 6. Choosing an arithmetic representation

Use \`fractions.Fraction\` for exact rational teaching examples; \`decimal.Decimal\` with an explicit context for controlled decimal calculations; and binary floats for approximate numerical models when their precision is sufficient. None of these automatically supplies a contractual interest-posting or currency-rounding convention.

Validate that inputs are finite, logarithm arguments are positive, and compounding periods and frequencies are appropriate integers. Use \`math.isclose\` with explicit tolerances appropriate to the scale and purpose of each test.

## 8. Exercises

1. Why can \`math.log(1+1e-16)\` equal zero, while \`math.log1p(1e-16)\` is nonzero?
2. Write a stable formula for converting continuous rate \(r\) over time \(t\) into a simple return.
3. Derive the stable EAR formula for nominal annual rate \(j\) compounded \(m\) times.
4. Compute cumulative log and simple returns for +100% followed by -50%.
5. Why can summing logarithms avoid overflow in an intermediate product?
6. Can \`log1p\` recover information lost before a price was supplied to the program?
7. Why is the inverse time formula poorly conditioned when the rate is near zero?
8. Distinguish exact rational arithmetic from controlled decimal and binary floating-point arithmetic.

### Worked answers

1. The addition rounds the tiny increment away; \`log1p\` accepts it directly.
2. \(R=\operatorname{expm1}(rt)\).
3. \(EAR=\operatorname{expm1}(m\operatorname{log1p}(j/m))\).
4. Log return \(\ln2+\ln(1/2)=0\); simple return zero.
5. Products become sums, which can remain representable even if intermediate products cannot.
6. No; numerical stability cannot reconstruct missing input precision.
7. Dividing a slightly erroneous log ratio by a near-zero rate magnifies the time error.
8. Fractions store rational values exactly, Decimal obeys configured decimal precision and rounding, and binary floats approximate most decimal and irrational values.
