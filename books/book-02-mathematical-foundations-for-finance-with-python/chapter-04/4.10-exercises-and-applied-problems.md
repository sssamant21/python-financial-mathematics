## Purpose and learning outcomes

This chapter-wide assessment integrates exponential growth and decay, logarithmic identities, nominal versus effective rates, discrete versus continuous compounding, inverse problems, cumulative returns, numerical stability, and model sensitivity. Problems require mathematical derivation, interpretation of assumptions, and reproducible Python evidence.

Use decimal rates throughout: 0.06 means 6%. Unless stated otherwise, monetary amounts are hypothetical, rates are constant, and no fees, taxes, external cash flows, or intermediate rounding apply.

## Part A — Conceptual and derivation exercises

**1. Exponential model.** Starting from a constant continuous proportional growth rate \(r\), derive \(V(t)=V_0e^{rt}\). State the units of \(r\) and \(t\).

**2. Logarithm domains.** Explain why \(\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b\) requires \(a,b>0\) in the real-valued formulation. Is \(\ln(x^2)=2\ln x\) valid for every nonzero real \(x\)?

**3. Periodic accumulation.** Derive \(FV=P(1+j/m)^n\) from the periodic rate \(j/m\). Explain why \(n\) must be a nonnegative integer in a completed-period model.

**4. Continuous limit.** Explain the limiting relationship \((1+r/m)^{mt}\to e^{rt}\) as \(m\to\infty\), for fixed finite \(r,t\).

**5. Rate conventions.** Derive the effective annual rate \(EAR=(1+j/m)^m-1\) and equivalent continuous rate \(r_c=m\ln(1+j/m)\).

**6. Inverse growth.** Derive \(t=\ln(F/P)/r\) and \(r=\ln(F/P)/t\) for continuous growth, including domain and identifiability restrictions.

**7. Log-return aggregation.** Prove that cumulative log changes add across time for strictly positive period factors. Explain why portfolio log return is not generally a weighted average of constituent log returns.

**8. Numerical methods.** Explain the roles of \`log1p\`, \`expm1\`, and \`fsum\`; distinguish numerical stability from conditioning.

## Part B — Applied calculation problems

**9. Quarterly compounding.** Find the future value of 5000 at nominal annual 8% compounded quarterly over three years. Compute EAR.

**10. Present value.** Find the present value of 1210 due in two years at 10% effective annual interest.

**11. Equivalent continuous rate.** Convert 7% EAR to a continuous annual rate. Verify that the one-year accumulation factors match.

**12. Doubling time.** For 6% growth per completed period, calculate the real-valued doubling period count and the first whole period that reaches or exceeds twice the initial value.

**13. Implied rate.** An investment rises from 1000 to 1250 over four years under continuous compounding. Find the implied annual continuous rate.

**14. Growth and loss.** A positive-valued investment earns +15%, -10%, and +5% over three periods. Calculate cumulative simple and log returns.

**15. Loss recovery.** After a 30% loss, what simple return is required to recover the original amount?

**16. Portfolio versus time aggregation.** Two assets have beginning-of-period weights 40% and 60% and simple returns +20% and -5%. Find the portfolio simple and log returns; compare with the weighted asset log returns.

**17. Sensitivity.** For \(FV=Pe^{rt}\), estimate the relative change in future value for a 15-basis-point rate increase over 20 years. Compare with the exact change.

**18. Small-rate numerical evaluation.** Evaluate the log return for \(r=10^{-16}\) using the stable method. Explain why a naïve \`log(1+r)\` computation may produce zero.

## Part C — Modeling and troubleshooting

**19. Cash flows.** A balance rises from 100 to 160 after a deposit of 60 and zero investment return. Explain why \(\ln(1.6)\) is not an investment log return.

**20. Unreachable target.** Determine whether 100 can grow to 150 under a constant continuous rate of -2% over nonnegative time.

**21. Zero-rate ambiguity.** If \(P=F=100\) and \(r=0\), can elapsed time be uniquely inferred? Why?

**22. Contract convention.** A savings product posts interest monthly. Explain why \(n=5.5\) in the periodic formula is not automatically a valid number of completed postings.

**23. Precision versus model validity.** Explain why adding more digits cannot correct an omitted fee, variable interest rate, or incorrect day-count convention.

**24. Verification plan.** Design at least five independent checks for a growth calculator, covering mathematical identities, domains, boundaries, and numerical sensitivity.

## Worked solutions

**1.** Solve \(dV/dt=rV\) by separation: \(dV/V=r\,dt\), hence \(\ln(V/V_0)=rt\) for positive \(V,V_0\), and \(V=V_0e^{rt}\). The rate has inverse-time units; if \(t\) is years, \(r\) is per year.

**2.** The real logarithms of \(a\) and \(b\) require positive inputs. For nonzero negative \(x\), \(\ln x\) is not real, while \(\ln(x^2)\) is defined. The correct identity is \(\ln(x^2)=2\ln|x|\).

**3.** Each completed period multiplies by \(1+j/m\), so \(n\) periods give the \(n\)-th power. An integer \(n\) counts actual postings; fractional-period accrual needs a separate contract convention.

**4.** Take logs: \(mt\ln(1+r/m)=rt\,[\ln(1+r/m)/(r/m)]\to rt\), using \(\ln(1+x)/x\to1\). Exponentiation yields \(e^{rt}\).

**5.** One-year factor is \((1+j/m)^m\); subtract one for EAR. Equate it with \(e^{r_c}\) and take logs to obtain \(r_c=m\ln(1+j/m)\).

**6.** Take logs of \(F/P=e^{rt}\). Require \(P,F>0\). Solve for time only if \(r\ne0\) and the result is nonnegative; solve for rate only if \(t>0\). If \(r=0\) and \(F=P\), time is not identifiable.

**7.** \(\ln\prod_t(1+R_t)=\sum_t\ln(1+R_t)\). Across assets, a one-period portfolio simple return is \(\sum_iw_iR_i\); \(\ln(1+\sum_iw_iR_i)\ne\sum_iw_i\ln(1+R_i)\) generally.

**8.** \`log1p(x)\` computes \(\ln(1+x)\) accurately for small \(x\); \`expm1(x)\` computes \(e^x-1\) accurately; \`fsum\` improves floating-point summation. Conditioning concerns input-to-output sensitivity, while stability concerns avoidable algorithmic error.

**9.** \(FV=5000(1.02)^{12}\approx6341.20897\). \(EAR=(1.02)^4-1\approx0.08243216\), or 8.243216%.

**10.** \(PV=1210/(1.1)^2=1000\).

**11.** \(r_c=\ln(1.07)\approx0.06765865\). Both one-year factors equal 1.07.

**12.** \(n=\ln2/\ln1.06\approx11.89566\). First completed period meeting the threshold is 12.

**13.** \(r=\ln(1.25)/4\approx0.05578589\), or 5.578589% per year.

**14.** Factor \(1.15\times0.90\times1.05=1.08675\). Cumulative simple return 8.675%; cumulative log return \(\ln(1.08675)\).

**15.** Required factor \(1/0.7\); simple return \(1/0.7-1=3/7\approx42.85714\%\).

**16.** Portfolio simple return \(0.4(0.2)+0.6(-0.05)=0.05\). Portfolio log return \(\ln1.05\). Weighted asset log returns \(0.4\ln1.2+0.6\ln0.95\), generally unequal to \(\ln1.05\).

**17.** \(\Delta r=0.0015\); first-order relative change \(20(0.0015)=0.03\), or 3%. Exact change \(e^{0.03}-1\approx3.04545\%\).

**18.** Use \`math.log1p(1e-16)\`, approximately \(10^{-16}\). The intermediate floating-point addition \`1+1e-16\` can round to one.

**19.** The balance change reflects an external contribution, not an investment return; the investment return is zero.

**20.** No. With negative rate, \(100e^{-0.02t}\le100\) for all \(t\ge0\).

**21.** No. At zero rate, every nonnegative time gives the same unchanged value.

**22.** Five and a half is not an integer count of completed monthly postings. A partial-month accrual policy must be specified.

**23.** Precision controls arithmetic error, not the correctness of missing business-model inputs or conventions.

**24.** Examples: zero-rate invariant; periodic/continuous equivalent-rate round trip; present/future-value inversion; negative and invalid factor rejection; small-rate \`log1p\` check; positive-rate frequency comparison; sensitivity finite-difference comparison; external cash-flow warning.
