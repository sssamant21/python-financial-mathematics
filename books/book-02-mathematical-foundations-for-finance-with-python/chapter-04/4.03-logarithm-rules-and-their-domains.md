## Learning objectives

Apply logarithm product, quotient, and power rules with correct real-number domains; identify invalid algebraic rewrites; distinguish logs of ratios from differences of logs; use log-return calculations in finance; and implement robust domain validation and numerical comparisons in Python.

## 1. Logarithm as an inverse

The natural logarithm \(\ln x\) is the inverse of \(e^x\) on the positive real numbers:

\[
\ln:(0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb R,\quad e^{\ln x}=x\ (x>0),\quad\ln(e^y)=y.
\]

A **real-valued logarithm requires a strictly positive argument**. Zero, negative values, NaN, and infinity are not valid finite real-valued financial log inputs.

## 2. Product rule

For \(a>0\) and \(b>0\),

\[
\boxed{\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b.}
\]

Proof: write \(a=e^u\), \(b=e^v\); then \(ab=e^{u+v}\), so \(\ln(ab)=u+v\).

**Financial example:** A balance grows by factors \(11/10\) and \(21/20\) in two periods. The cumulative log growth is

\[
\ln\left(\frac{11}{10}\frac{21}{20}\right)
=\ln\frac{11}{10}+\ln\frac{21}{20}.
\]

The corresponding simple cumulative return is \( (11/10)(21/20)-1=31/200=15.5\%\), while the cumulative **log return** is \(\ln(231/200)\), not 15.5%.

## 3. Quotient rule

For \(a>0\), \(b>0\),

\[
\boxed{\ln(a/b)=\ln a-\ln b.}
\]

A common financial application is the log change from a positive starting value \(P_0\) to a positive ending value \(P_1\):

\[
\ell=\ln(P_1/P_0)=\ln P_1-\ln P_0.
\]

This difference is meaningful only when both prices or modeled values are strictly positive.

**Example:** \(P_0=100\), \(P_1=110\). The simple return is 10%; the log return is \(\ln(1.1)\approx0.09531018\). These are different numerical measures.

## 4. Power rule

For \(a>0\) and real \(k\),

\[
\boxed{\ln(a^k)=k\ln a.}
\]

For example, if a positive balance grows by factor \(g>0\) over \(n\) periods,

\[
\ln(V_n/V_0)=\ln(g^n)=n\ln g.
\]

This is why log changes add across periods and why a continuous growth rate can be recovered by dividing cumulative log growth by elapsed time.

## 5. Domains matter when rewriting expressions

The log rules are identities **only where both sides are defined**.

For example, \(\ln(x^2)\) is defined for \(x\ne0\), including negative \(x\), but \(2\ln x\) is defined only for \(x>0\). The domain-preserving identity for real \(x\ne0\) is

\[
\ln(x^2)=2\ln|x|.
\]

Likewise, \(\ln(ab)\) may be defined when both \(a\) and \(b\) are negative (their product is positive), but \(\ln a+\ln b\) is not real-valued. Do not expand a logarithm into terms that are individually undefined.

For real \(x\), \(\ln(x-5)\) requires \(x>5\). For \(\ln[(x-5)/(x+2)]\), the quotient must be positive, giving \(x<-2\) or \(x>5\). Simply requiring the numerator and denominator to be positive would incorrectly exclude the \(x<-2\) branch.

## 6. Invalid identities

The following are **not** general logarithm identities:

\[
\ln(a+b)\ne\ln a+\ln b,\qquad
\ln(a-b)\ne\ln a-\ln b.
\]

For instance, \(\ln(2+3)=\ln5\) while \(\ln2+\ln3=\ln6\). The correct rules apply to products, quotients, and powers—not sums and differences.

Also distinguish \(\ln(x)^2\), meaning \((\ln x)^2\), from \(\ln(x^2)\). They are generally unequal.

## 7. Financial log returns and telescoping

For a sequence of **positive** prices \(P_0,P_1,\ldots,P_n\), define one-period log returns

\[
\ell_t=\ln(P_t/P_{t-1}).
\]

Their sum telescopes:

\[
\sum_{t=1}^{n}\ell_t
=\ln(P_n/P_0).
\]

The total simple return is

\[
\frac{P_n}{P_0}-1=e^{\sum\ell_t}-1.
\]

**Worked example:** Prices 100, 110, 105 yield two log returns \(\ln(11/10)\) and \(\ln(21/22)\). Their sum is \(\ln(21/20)\), because the total price ratio is \(105/100=21/20\). The total simple return is 5%.

The telescoping identity is algebraic. It does not account for dividends, splits, fees, or corporate actions unless the price series is adjusted appropriately. Log returns are undefined for nonpositive values and are not a substitute for modeling cash flows.

## 8. Numerically stable implementation

When \(P_1/P_0\) is close to one, computing \(u=(P_1-P_0)/P_0\) and using \`math.log1p(u)\` often reduces cancellation relative to evaluating \`math.log(P_1/P_0)\` after rounding the ratio.

Likewise, \`math.expm1(\ell)\` is preferable to \`math.exp(\ell)-1\` for small \(\ell\). Neither function provides exact arithmetic: logarithms are generally irrational and Python's \`math\` functions return floating-point approximations.

## 9. Exercises

**1.** Simplify \(\ln(4)+\ln(5)-\ln(2)\) and give the result.

**2.** For positive \(a,b\), prove \(\ln(a^3/b^2)=3\ln a-2\ln b\).

**3.** Find the real domain of \(\ln(x-5)\).

**4.** Find the real domain of \(\ln[(x-5)/(x+2)]\).

**5.** Explain why \(\ln(x^2)=2\ln x\) is not valid for all \(x\ne0\). Give a correct domain-preserving formula.

**6.** For positive prices 100, 110, 105, compute the cumulative log return and cumulative simple return.

**7.** Explain why \(\ln(2+3)\) cannot be expanded using the product rule.

**8.** A price ratio is \(1+10^{-12}\). Explain why \`log1p\` and \`expm1\` are useful when converting between tiny simple and log returns.

### Worked answers

1. \(\ln(4\cdot5/2)=\ln10\).
2. By the quotient and power rules, \(\ln(a^3/b^2)=\ln(a^3)-\ln(b^2)=3\ln a-2\ln b\).
3. \(x>5\).
4. \(x<-2\) or \(x>5\).
5. \(2\ln x\) is undefined for negative \(x\); use \(\ln(x^2)=2\ln|x|\) for \(x\ne0\).
6. Cumulative log return \(\ln(105/100)=\ln(21/20)\); simple return \(105/100-1=1/20=5\%\).
7. Addition is not multiplication; \(\ln5\ne\ln6\).
8. They avoid avoidable loss of precision when subtracting nearly equal numbers or forming a ratio extremely close to one.
