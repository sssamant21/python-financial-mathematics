## Learning objectives

## 1. Multiplicative growth across time

For a strictly positive starting value \(V_0\), let \(g_t>0\) denote the growth factor in period \(t\):

\[
V_t=V_{t-1}g_t.
\]

Repeated substitution gives

\[
\boxed{V_n=V_0\prod_{t=1}^n g_t}.
\]

If the period's simple return is \(R_t=g_t-1\), then

\[
\boxed{1+R_{\mathrm{total}}=\prod_{t=1}^n(1+R_t)}.
\]

The total simple return is the product minus one, **not** generally the sum of simple returns.

### Worked example

A hypothetical investment earns 10%, loses 5%, then earns 8%:

\[
(1.10)(0.95)(1.08)=1.1286.
\]

The cumulative simple return is \(12.86\%\), not \(10-5+8=13\%\).

## 2. Why logarithmic changes add

Since each \(g_t>0\), the natural logarithm of the product is defined:

\[
\ln\left(\prod_{t=1}^n g_t\right)=\sum_{t=1}^n\ln(g_t).
\]

Define the period log change

\[
\ell_t=\ln(g_t)=\ln(1+R_t).
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{L=\sum_{t=1}^n\ell_t=\ln(V_n/V_0)}.
\]

Converting back:

\[
\boxed{R_{\mathrm{total}}=e^L-1}.
\]

Log returns aggregate additively **over time along a single positive-valued, appropriately adjusted path**. This is an algebraic identity, not a claim that returns are predictable or independent.

## 3. Log changes telescope

For positive observations \(P_0,P_1,\ldots,P_n\),

\[
\sum_{t=1}^n\left(\ln P_t-\ln P_{t-1}\right)
=\ln P_n-\ln P_0
=\ln(P_n/P_0).
\]

For prices 100, 110, 105, the period log returns are \(\ln(1.1)\) and \(\ln(105/110)\). Their sum is \(\ln(1.05)\), corresponding to a cumulative simple return of 5%.

## 4. Growth and loss are asymmetric in simple-return space

A 20% loss followed by a 20% gain produces

\[
(0.8)(1.2)=0.96,
\]

a cumulative loss of 4%. Returning from 80 to 100 requires a 25% gain, not 20%.

In log space the inverse changes are symmetric:

\[
\ln(0.8)+\ln(1.25)=\ln(1)=0.
\]

The second example is a recovery to the original level, whereas \(0.8\times1.2\) is not.

## 5. Log returns across time versus portfolio returns across assets

Suppose two assets have period simple returns \(R_A\) and \(R_B\), with beginning-of-period portfolio weights \(w\) and \(1-w\), and no interim flows or rebalancing during the period. The portfolio simple return is

\[
R_p=wR_A+(1-w)R_B.
\]

Its log return is

\[
\ell_p=\ln(1+R_p),
\]

which is **not generally** \(w\ln(1+R_A)+(1-w)\ln(1+R_B)\).

**Example:** With equal weights and asset returns +20% and -10%, the portfolio simple return is 5%, so its log return is \(\ln(1.05)\). The weighted asset log returns instead equal \(\tfrac12\ln(1.2)+\tfrac12\ln(0.9)=\tfrac12\ln(1.08)\). These are unequal.

Time-additivity of log changes must not be mistaken for cross-asset linearity.

## 6. External cash flows break naive balance ratios

If deposits or withdrawals occur, the observed account balance ratio combines market performance and external flows. For example, an account starting at 100, earning 0%, and receiving a deposit of 50 ends at 150. Its raw balance log change is \(\ln(1.5)\), but the investment return is zero.

Use a properly defined time-weighted return, money-weighted return, or cash-flow model when contributions and withdrawals matter. The simple product of period return factors assumes each period's return is calculated consistently, excluding external cash-flow effects.

## 7. Domain and numerical constraints

The logarithm \(\ln(1+R_t)\) requires \(R_t>-1\). A -100% return has a zero growth factor, so its real log return is undefined; a return below -100% yields a negative factor and is also outside this positive-value model.

For small \(R_t\), evaluate \(\ln(1+R_t)\) with \`math.log1p(R_t)\`. For a small cumulative log change \(L\), compute the cumulative simple return with \`math.expm1(L)\`. These functions reduce cancellation, but floating-point results are approximate. A long product may overflow or underflow; summing logs can be more numerically robust within the finite floating-point range.

**7.** A balance grows from 100 to 150 solely because of a deposit of 50. Is \(\ln(1.5)\) a valid measure of investment performance?

**8.** Why are \`log1p\` and \`expm1\` preferable to naive formulas for very small changes?
