## 4.1.1 Learning objectives

Understand exponential models, distinguish linear from multiplicative changes, derive the discrete-time growth formula, identify growth and decay factors, calculate cumulative changes and exact inverse growth factors, and implement exact-rational financial examples in Python.

## 4.1.2 Repeated multiplicative change

Suppose a value starts at $V_0$ and changes by a fixed fraction $r$ per period. After one period,

$$V_1=V_0+rV_0=V_0(1+r).$$

Applying the same rule repeatedly gives

$$V_2=V_1(1+r)=V_0(1+r)^2.$$

By induction,

$$\boxed{V_n=V_0(1+r)^n},\qquad n\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}.$$

The **growth factor** is $g=1+r$. For a nonnegative-valued financial balance, we generally require $V_0\ge0$ and $g\ge0$; this lab uses $r>-1$ to keep the factor strictly positive. The period unit must be specified: monthly and annual rates are not interchangeable.

If $r>0$, the value grows; if $-1<r<0$, it decays; if $r=0$, it is constant. For positive $V_0$, the value is strictly increasing or decreasing with period count accordingly. If $V_0=0$, every value remains zero.

## 4.1.3 Linear versus exponential growth

Linear change adds a constant amount:

$$L_n=V_0+nd.$$

Exponential change multiplies by a constant factor:

$$E_n=V_0(1+r)^n.$$

For $V_0=100$, $d=10$, and $r=10\%$, both models produce 110 after one period. After two periods, the linear model gives 120 while the exponential model gives 121. The difference comes from earning a percentage change on the previously changed amount.

| Period $n$ | Linear $100+10n$ | Exponential $100(11/10)^n$ |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 100 | 100 |
| 1 | 110 | 110 |
| 2 | 120 | 121 |
| 3 | 130 | 133.1 |

The exponential model assumes a constant **percentage** change, not a constant currency increment.

## 4.1.4 Worked example: annual growth

An initial hypothetical amount of 1,000 currency units grows at 5% per year for three years:

$$V_3=1000(1+0.05)^3=1000\left(\frac{21}{20}\right)^3=1157.625.$$

The total gain is 157.625 currency units, corresponding to a cumulative growth rate

$$\left(\frac{21}{20}\right)^3-1=\frac{1261}{8000}=15.7625\%.$$

The cumulative percentage is **not** simply $3\times5\%=15\%$, because each year's percentage is applied to a different base.

## 4.1.5 Worked example: exponential decay

Suppose a modeled asset value decreases by 20% per period:

$$V_n=500\left(\frac45\right)^n.$$

At $n=3$,

$$V_3=500\left(\frac45\right)^3=256.$$

The cumulative decline is $1-(4/5)^3=48.8\%$. The model is a simplified depreciation assumption; actual asset prices may not follow a fixed percentage decay.

## 4.1.6 Recovering a per-period factor

If $V_0>0$ and $V_n>0$ are known, then

$$\frac{V_n}{V_0}=(1+r)^n.$$

For positive integer $n$, the unique positive factor is

$$1+r=\left(\frac{V_n}{V_0}\right)^{1/n}.$$

Thus $r=(V_n/V_0)^{1/n}-1$. In general this root may be irrational. **An exact rational-only implementation must not pretend that every root is rational.**

For example, if an amount doubles over two periods, the factor is $\sqrt2$, not a rational number. Our reference implementation supports exact integer exponents for forward calculations and exact rational roots when they exist; it explicitly reports when the inverse root is not rational.

## 4.1.7 Model assumptions and limitations

Exponential models require a clearly specified period, a constant multiplicative factor, and a decision about cash flows added or removed during the interval. This introductory formula assumes **no intermediate deposits, withdrawals, fees, taxes, or rate changes**. If any occur, a recurrence or piecewise model is needed.

The model is deterministic. A fixed growth rate should not be confused with a guaranteed investment return. Financial results should also specify currency rounding rules; exact fractions in this lab are mathematical quantities, not a settlement or accounting policy.

## 4.1.9 Exercises

**1.** Derive the three-period formula from the one-period recurrence.

**2.** Find the value of 200 currency units after four periods at 10% growth per period.

**3.** A quantity of 1,000 declines by 10% per period. Find its value after two periods and cumulative percentage decline.

**4.** Compare linear and exponential models starting at 100 with an initial increment of 5 after three periods.

**5.** A value rises from 100 to 121 over two periods. Recover the positive per-period growth factor and rate.

**6.** A value rises from 100 to 200 over two periods. Explain why the positive growth factor cannot be represented exactly as a rational number.

**7.** Explain why a monthly growth rate cannot be inserted into a formula where $n$ counts years without converting units or periods.

### Worked solutions

1. Repeated substitution gives $V_1=V_0g$, $V_2=V_0g^2$, and $V_3=V_0g^3$, where $g=1+r$.
2. $200(11/10)^4=292.82$.
3. $1000(9/10)^2=810$; cumulative decline 19%.
4. Linear $100+3(5)=115$; exponential $100(21/20)^3=115.7625$.
5. $(121/100)^{1/2}=11/10$; rate 10%.
6. The factor is $\sqrt2$, irrational; an exact rational-only interface must classify it rather than round silently.
7. The exponent counts rate periods. Monthly rates require monthly period counts, or an explicitly derived equivalent annual rate.
