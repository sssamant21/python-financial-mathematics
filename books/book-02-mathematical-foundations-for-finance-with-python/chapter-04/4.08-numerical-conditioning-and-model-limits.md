## Learning objectives

Distinguish a problem's *conditioning* from an algorithm's *stability*; quantify local sensitivities for compound growth and inverse rates; explain identifiability and uncertainty; recognize model assumptions that numerical precision cannot fix; and use Python to compare analytic and finite-difference sensitivities.

## 1. Conditioning versus stability

A mathematical problem is **well conditioned** if small changes in its inputs produce small changes in its outputs under an appropriate measure. It is **ill conditioned** if small input perturbations can produce large output changes. A numerical algorithm is **stable** if it avoids unnecessary error beyond the problem's inherent sensitivity.

Using \`math.log1p\` can improve the evaluation of \(\ln(1+r)\). It cannot repair uncertainty in the original measured return \(r\). Similarly, more decimal places do not make an incorrectly specified compounding convention correct.

## 2. Sensitivity of future value

For \(FV=Pe^{rt}\), with \(P>0\),

\[
\ln FV=\ln P+rt.
\]

For small changes in inputs,

\[
\frac{dFV}{FV}=\frac{dP}{P}+t\,dr+r\,dt.
\]

Thus the *local relative sensitivity* to a small change in rate is approximately \(t\,dr\); longer horizons amplify rate uncertainty. The sensitivity to a small change in time is approximately \(r\,dt\).

**Worked example:** With \(r=0.05\), \(t=30\), and a rate uncertainty of \(\pm0.001\) (10 basis points), the first-order relative future-value uncertainty from the rate alone is approximately \(\pm30(0.001)=\pm3\%\). This is a local approximation, not a probabilistic confidence interval.

For periodic compounding,

\[
FV=P(1+i)^n,\qquad
\frac{\partial\ln FV}{\partial i}=\frac{n}{1+i}.
\]

Near \(i=-1\), this derivative becomes very large, signaling high sensitivity.

## 3. Inverse-rate sensitivity

For positive \(P,F\) and \(t>0\), the implied continuous rate is

\[
r=\frac{\ln(F/P)}{t}.
\]

Its differential is

\[
dr=\frac{1}{t}\left(\frac{dF}{F}-\frac{dP}{P}\right)-\frac{\ln(F/P)}{t^2}dt.
\]

When the observed log change is close to zero, the **relative error in the inferred rate** can be large even if the absolute error is small. At \(F=P\), the implied rate is zero, so relative error in the rate is undefined; report absolute rate uncertainty instead.

**Example:** If \(F/P=1.001\) over one year, the inferred continuous rate is approximately 0.0009995. A measurement uncertainty of 0.1% in the ratio is comparable in magnitude to the inferred rate itself, so a highly precise-looking percentage is misleading.

## 4. Solving for time near zero rates

For a nonzero continuous rate,

\[
t=\frac{\ln(F/P)}r.
\]

Holding the observed ratio fixed,

\[
\frac{\partial t}{\partial r}=-\frac{\ln(F/P)}{r^2}.
\]

The absolute sensitivity increases rapidly as \(r\to0\). If \(F=P\) and \(r=0\), every nonnegative time is consistent with the model; time is not identifiable.

The formula may be evaluated stably and still yield a fundamentally uncertain result because the inverse problem is poorly conditioned.

## 5. Perturbation experiments

A finite-difference approximation to the derivative of a function \(f(x)\) is

\[
f'(x)\approx\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}.
\]

Choosing \(h\) requires care: very large steps introduce truncation error, while extremely small steps can introduce cancellation. The companion lab compares central differences with analytic derivatives over several step sizes. Agreement within a reasonable range supports the implementation; disagreement at tiny steps does not automatically invalidate the analytic formula.

## 6. Limits of the financial model

The following are **model limitations**, not floating-point problems:

- **Constant-rate assumption:** real yields and returns can change over time.
- **Day-count and payment timing:** calendar conventions and posting dates affect results.
- **Cash flows:** contributions, withdrawals, dividends, fees, and taxes must be represented explicitly.
- **Uncertainty and risk:** deterministic compounding does not quantify market volatility or default risk.
- **Identifiability:** one starting and ending value cannot uniquely determine a changing rate path.
- **Rounding policy:** contractual settlement may round per period, rather than only at the end.

A model can be evaluated to many digits while still being inappropriate for the business question.

## 7. Practical validation workflow

1. Define the financial quantity, time unit, rate convention, and input domain.
2. Write the mathematical formula and its assumptions.
3. Use stable primitives such as \`log1p\` and \`expm1\`.
4. Estimate sensitivity to plausible input changes; use absolute errors near zero.
5. Compare against an independent formulation or limiting case.
6. Document what is excluded from the model.
7. Report a defensible precision, not every available floating-point digit.

## 9. Exercises

**1.** Distinguish an ill-conditioned problem from an unstable algorithm.

**2.** Derive \(\partial\ln FV/\partial r\) for \(FV=Pe^{rt}\).

**3.** Estimate the relative change in \(FV\) for a 10-basis-point rate increase over 30 years.

**4.** Derive \(\partial\ln FV/\partial i\) for \(FV=P(1+i)^n\).

**5.** Derive the differential of \(r=\ln(F/P)/t\).

**6.** Why is relative error in an implied rate inappropriate when the rate is zero?

**7.** Explain why tiny finite-difference steps can worsen derivative estimates.

**8.** Name three model assumptions that cannot be fixed by using higher numerical precision.

### Worked answers

1. Conditioning concerns sensitivity inherent in the problem; stability concerns additional error introduced by the algorithm.
2. \(\partial\ln FV/\partial r=t\).
3. Approximately \(30(0.001)=0.03\), or 3%.
4. \(\partial\ln FV/\partial i=n/(1+i)\).
5. \(dr=(dF/F-dP/P)/t-[\ln(F/P)/t^2]dt\).
6. Dividing by zero makes relative error undefined; report absolute error.
7. Subtraction of nearly equal floating-point results magnifies rounding effects.
8. Examples: constant rates, absence of cash flows, day-count convention, fees, taxes, or market risk.
