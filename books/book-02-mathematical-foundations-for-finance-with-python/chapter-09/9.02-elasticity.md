## Learning objectives

Derive point and arc elasticity; interpret elastic, inelastic, and unit-elastic responses; calculate price and income elasticities; connect elasticity with revenue and discounted valuation; and recognize the limitations of elasticity near zero baselines and nonsmooth points.

## 1. Definition and derivation

For a differentiable relationship \(y=f(x)\), with \(x\ne0\) and \(f(x)\ne0\), the **point elasticity** is

\[
\boxed{\varepsilon_{y,x}(x)=\frac{x}{f(x)}f'(x)}.
\]

It measures the local proportional response of \(y\) to a proportional change in \(x\). Starting with the linear approximation \(\Delta y\approx f'(x)\Delta x\), divide by \(y\):

\[
\frac{\Delta y}{y}\approx\frac{xf'(x)}{y}\frac{\Delta x}{x}
=\varepsilon_{y,x}\frac{\Delta x}{x}.
\]

The elasticity is dimensionless, unlike \(f'(x)\), which has output units per input unit. If \(x>0\) and \(f(x)>0\), then

\[
\varepsilon_{y,x}=\frac{d\ln f(x)}{d\ln x}.
\]

The log-derivative identity requires positive \(x\) and \(f(x)\) for real logarithms. The algebraic ratio \(xf'/f\) can still exist at some negative nonzero baselines, but percentage interpretations need care.

## 2. Price elasticity of demand

For a positive demand function \(Q(p)\) with \(p>0\), the signed price elasticity is

\[
\varepsilon_p=\frac{pQ'(p)}{Q(p)}.
\]

Typically \(\varepsilon_p<0\). In absolute-value terminology, demand is:

- **Elastic:** \(|\varepsilon_p|>1\).
- **Unit elastic:** \(|\varepsilon_p|=1\).
- **Inelastic:** \(|\varepsilon_p|<1\).

These categories describe local responses; they do not assert that the entire demand curve has the same elasticity.

### Worked example: constant-elasticity demand

For \(Q(p)=Ap^{-k}\), where \(A>0\), \(k>0\), and \(p>0\),

\[
Q'(p)=-kAp^{-k-1},\qquad
\boxed{\varepsilon_p=-k}.
\]

With \(A=400\) and \(k=1.5\), the elasticity is \(-1.5\) at every positive price. A small 1% price rise produces approximately a 1.5% quantity decline.

### Worked example: linear demand

Let \(Q(p)=100-2p\), with \(0<p<50\). Then

\[
\varepsilon_p=-\frac{2p}{100-2p}.
\]

At \(p=10\), elasticity is \(-0.25\); at \(p=25\), it is \(-1\); and at \(p=40\), it is \(-4\). The absolute slope \(Q'=-2\) is constant, but elasticity varies with the price and demand baselines.

## 3. Revenue and elasticity

Revenue is \(R(p)=pQ(p)\). Differentiating,

\[
R'(p)=Q(p)+pQ'(p)
=Q(p)(1+\varepsilon_p).
\]

If \(Q>0\), then revenue rises with price locally when \(\varepsilon_p>-1\), falls when \(\varepsilon_p<-1\), and is stationary when \(\varepsilon_p=-1\). This is a mathematical result for the specified demand model; pricing decisions may involve costs, competition, capacity, and constraints.

For linear demand \(Q(p)=100-2p\),

\[
R(p)=100p-2p^2,\quad R'(p)=100-4p.
\]

Revenue is maximized at \(p=25\), where demand elasticity is \(-1\).

## 4. Income elasticity

For quantity \(Q(I)\) as a function of positive income \(I\),

\[
\varepsilon_I=\frac{IQ'(I)}{Q(I)}.
\]

Under standard economic definitions, positive income elasticity indicates a normal good; negative income elasticity indicates an inferior good, conditional on the demand model and other variables being held fixed. Values above one are sometimes associated with luxury demand, but classification is context dependent.

For \(Q(I)=aI^{0.7}\), \(a>0\), elasticity is \(0.7\): a small 1% income increase is associated with approximately 0.7% higher quantity.

## 5. Financial elasticity: discounted value

For a positive cash flow \(C\), horizon \(t\ge0\), and continuously compounded rate \(r\),

\[
V(r)=Ce^{-rt},\qquad V'(r)=-tV(r).
\]

For \(r\ne0\), the algebraic elasticity is

\[
\boxed{\varepsilon_{V,r}=\frac{rV'}{V}=-rt}.
\]

If \(r>0\), a 1% *relative* rise in \(r\) is a shock \(\Delta r=0.01r\), not 0.01. At \(r=0.04\), \(t=5\), elasticity is \(-0.2\); a 1% relative increase in the rate implies an approximate 0.2% decline in value.

At \(r=0\), \(rV'/V=0\) algebraically, but the relative input change \(\Delta r/r\) is undefined. In practice, normalized rate sensitivity \(-V'/V=t\) is often more informative across zero rates. Do not apply a logarithmic-rate interpretation when \(r\le0\).

## 6. Arc elasticity for finite changes

When derivatives are unavailable or a finite interval is of interest, the **midpoint arc elasticity** is

\[
\boxed{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{arc}}=
\frac{(y_2-y_1)/((y_1+y_2)/2)}
{(x_2-x_1)/((x_1+x_2)/2)}}.
\]

This expression is undefined when its denominators vanish, including zero midpoint baselines or no input change. For positive quantities and prices, it is symmetric under reversing endpoints, unlike a percentage-change calculation using only the initial baseline.

**Example:** Price increases from 10 to 12 and quantity falls from 80 to 70. The midpoint relative quantity change is \(-10/75\), and the midpoint relative price change is \(2/11\), so arc elasticity is

\[
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{arc}}=-\frac{11}{15}\approx-0.7333.
\]

Arc elasticity summarizes a finite interval; it need not equal point elasticity at either endpoint.

## 7. Cross elasticity and multivariable context

For demand \(Q(p_1,p_2,I)\), cross-price elasticity with respect to \(p_2\) is

\[
\varepsilon_{Q,p_2}=\frac{p_2}{Q}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial p_2},
\]

holding other inputs fixed. Positive values are often consistent with substitute goods and negative values with complements, under the model's assumptions. Partial derivatives are introduced here as notation; multivariable calculus receives dedicated treatment later.

## 8. Numerical validation and safeguards

Given an analytic elasticity, a centered difference can check the derivative:

\[
\widehat{\varepsilon}_h=\frac{x}{f(x)}
\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}.
\]

Use perturbations valid for the model's domain. Check several step sizes, not just one. At kinks, caps, zero output, or very small baselines, the numerical result may be unstable or economically misleading. Simulation noise can also overwhelm finite differences.

## 9. Exercises

**1.** Derive point elasticity from the first-order approximation to \(f(x+\Delta x)\).

**2.** Explain why elasticity is dimensionless while \(f'(x)\) has units.

**3.** Compute elasticity for \(Q(p)=500p^{-2}\).

**4.** Compute elasticity of \(Q(p)=100-2p\) at \(p=10\), \(25\), and \(40\).

**5.** Derive \(R'(p)=Q(p)(1+\varepsilon_p)\).

**6.** Find the revenue-maximizing price for \(Q(p)=100-2p\).

**7.** Compute income elasticity for \(Q(I)=5I^{0.7}\).

**8.** Compute discounted-value rate elasticity for \(V(r)=10000e^{-5r}\) at \(r=0.04\).

**9.** Compute midpoint arc elasticity for price 10→12 and quantity 80→70.

**10.** Explain two reasons why numerical elasticity estimates can fail.

### Worked solutions

1. Divide \(\Delta y\approx f'(x)\Delta x\) by \(y\) and substitute \(\Delta x=x(\Delta x/x)\), giving \(\varepsilon=xf'/f\).
2. The factors \(x/f\) cancel the units of \(f'\).
3. \(Q'=-1000p^{-3}\), so \(\varepsilon=-2\).
4. \(-2p/(100-2p)\): respectively \(-0.25,-1,-4\).
5. \(R'=Q+pQ'=Q[1+(pQ'/Q)]\).
6. \(R=100p-2p^2\); \(R'=100-4p=0\) gives \(p=25\), with \(R''=-4<0\).
7. \(Q'=3.5I^{-0.3}\); \(\varepsilon_I=0.7\).
8. \(\varepsilon_{V,r}=-5(0.04)=-0.2\).
9. \([(-10)/75]/[2/11]=-11/15\approx-0.7333\).
10. A near-zero output baseline makes \(xf'/f\) unstable; an invalid perturbation can cross a domain boundary. Nondifferentiability and simulation noise are additional hazards.
