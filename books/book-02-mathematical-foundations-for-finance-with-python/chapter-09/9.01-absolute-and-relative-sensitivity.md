## Learning objectives

Distinguish absolute from relative changes; calculate local sensitivities; interpret derivative units; normalize financial sensitivities; identify zero-baseline and sign pitfalls; and compare finite-change calculations with first-order approximations.

## 1. Changes versus sensitivities

For an input \(x\) and model output \(V(x)\), an input shock \(\Delta x\) produces

\[
\Delta V=V(x+\Delta x)-V(x).
\]

This is the **exact finite change** for that scenario. If \(V\) is differentiable at \(x\), its **absolute sensitivity** is

\[
\boxed{S_{\mathrm{abs}}(x)=\frac{dV}{dx}}.
\]

It measures output units per one input unit. For a sufficiently small shock,

\[
\boxed{\Delta V\approx V'(x)\Delta x}.
\]

The derivative is a local rate of change, not the finite change itself. The approximation may deteriorate for large shocks or nonlinear models.

## 2. Relative changes

When the baseline \(V(x)\ne0\), the relative output change is

\[
\boxed{\frac{\Delta V}{V(x)}}.
\]

The relative input change is \(\Delta x/x\) when \(x\ne0\). Both are dimensionless, but their interpretation depends on whether the baselines are economically meaningful.

For positive \(V\) and positive \(x\), the **relative sensitivity** to a fractional input change is

\[
\boxed{S_{\mathrm{rel}}(x)=\frac{x}{V(x)}V'(x)}.
\]

This is also called point elasticity. Its magnitude describes the approximate percentage change in output associated with a 1% input change, locally:

\[
\frac{\Delta V}{V}\approx S_{\mathrm{rel}}\frac{\Delta x}{x}.
\]

If \(x=0\) or \(V=0\), this normalization is undefined; report the absolute sensitivity or choose a different reference scale. If inputs or outputs are negative, the algebra remains possible for nonzero baselines, but conventional percentage interpretations may be misleading.

## 3. Units and normalization

Suppose \(V\) is measured in dollars and \(r\) is a decimal annual interest rate. Then \(dV/dr\) is measured in dollars per unit of decimal rate. A one-basis-point change corresponds to

\[
\Delta r=0.0001.
\]

Thus the first-order dollar effect of a one-basis-point increase is

\[
\Delta V_{\mathrm{1bp}}\approx 0.0001\,\frac{dV}{dr}.
\]

Do not confuse a derivative per unit decimal rate with a change per basis point.

For rate-based present values, a useful **normalized rate sensitivity** is

\[
D(r)=-\frac{1}{V(r)}\frac{dV}{dr},
\]

provided \(V(r)>0\). This has units of inverse decimal-rate units (often interpreted as time under specific discounting conventions), and is **not** the same as elasticity \(rV'/V\). It avoids the singular normalization by \(r\) when \(r=0\).

## 4. Worked example: continuous discounting

Let

\[
V(r)=Ce^{-rt},\qquad C>0,\quad t>0.
\]

Holding \(C,t\) fixed,

\[
V'(r)=-tCe^{-rt}=-tV(r).
\]

Therefore

\[
S_{\mathrm{abs}}=-tV,\qquad D=-V'/V=t,\qquad
S_{\mathrm{rel}}=\frac{rV'}V=-rt.
\]

For \(C=10{,}000\), \(r=0.04\), \(t=5\),

\[
V=10000e^{-0.2}\approx8187.307531,\qquad
V'\approx-40936.537655.
\]

The approximate impact of a one-basis-point rate rise is \(-4.093654\) dollars. The exact impact is

\[
V(r+0.0001)-V(r)=V(r)\operatorname{expm1}(-0.0005),
\]

which differs slightly because the discount curve is nonlinear.

The rate elasticity is \(-rt=-0.2\). A 1% *relative* increase in the rate from 4% to 4.04% corresponds to \(\Delta r=0.0004\), **not** 0.01. Its approximate relative value effect is \((-0.2)(0.01)=-0.002\), or \(-0.2\%\).

## 5. Worked example: demand and price

Let \(Q(p)=400p^{-1.5}\), for \(p>0\). Then

\[
Q'(p)=-600p^{-2.5},\qquad
\frac{pQ'(p)}{Q(p)}=-1.5.
\]

At any positive price, a small 1% price increase is associated with an approximately 1.5% decrease in quantity, assuming the model remains valid. This is a **local** statement, not an exact finite-change rule.

## 6. Worked example: portfolio value

Suppose portfolio value depends linearly on asset price \(p\):

\[
V(p)=np+B,
\]

where \(n\) is a fixed share count and \(B\) is a fixed cash balance. Then

\[
\frac{dV}{dp}=n,\qquad
\frac{pV'}V=\frac{np}{np+B},
\]

when \(V\ne0\). Absolute sensitivity equals the number of shares; relative sensitivity equals the fraction of total portfolio value represented by the asset position when all components are nonnegative.

If \(n=20\), \(p=50\), and \(B=1000\), then \(V=2000\), \(V'=20\), and elasticity \(=0.5\).

## 7. Multiple inputs and partial sensitivities

For a differentiable model \(V(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\), the absolute sensitivity to \(x_i\), holding other inputs fixed, is the partial derivative \(\partial V/\partial x_i\). For small simultaneous changes,

\[
\Delta V\approx\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial V}{\partial x_i}\Delta x_i.
\]

This linearization excludes second-order interactions. A full treatment of multivariable calculus appears later in the curriculum.

## 8. When sensitivity can mislead

- **Zero baselines:** \(\Delta V/V\) and \(xV'/V\) are undefined at \(V=0\); \(xV'/V\) is also undefined at \(x=0\).
- **Large shocks:** derivative-based approximations may be inaccurate.
- **Nonsmooth models:** derivatives may not exist at thresholds, caps, or exercise boundaries.
- **Model changes:** fixed-cash-flow assumptions may fail for prepayable or callable instruments.
- **Different units:** always distinguish decimal rates, percentages, and basis points.
- **Negative baselines:** percentages may have counterintuitive economic interpretations.
- **Ill-conditioned models:** a large normalized sensitivity can flag instability, but not necessarily a numerical implementation error.

## 10. Exercises

**1.** Define absolute sensitivity and give its units.

**2.** Distinguish the exact change \(V(x+h)-V(x)\) from \(V'(x)h\).

**3.** Derive the elasticity formula \(xV'/V\).

**4.** Explain why elasticity is undefined when the output baseline is zero.

**5.** Derive \(V'(r)\) for \(V(r)=Ce^{-rt}\).

**6.** Find the normalized rate sensitivity \(D=-V'/V\) for continuous discounting.

**7.** Calculate the first-order effect of a 1-basis-point rate rise for \(C=10000,t=5,r=0.04\).

**8.** For \(Q(p)=400p^{-1.5}\), derive price elasticity.

**9.** For \(V(p)=20p+1000\), find absolute and relative sensitivities at \(p=50\).

**10.** Explain why a 1% relative increase from a 4% interest rate is not a one-percentage-point increase.

### Worked solutions

1. \(dV/dx\), in output units per input unit.
2. The first is the actual model change; the second is its local linear approximation.
3. Divide \(\Delta V\approx V'\Delta x\) by \(V\), then write \(\Delta x=x(\Delta x/x)\).
4. Division by zero is undefined; use absolute sensitivity or another normalization.
5. \(V'(r)=-tCe^{-rt}=-tV(r)\).
6. \(D=t\) for \(C>0\).
7. \(-tV(0.04)(0.0001)=-5(10000e^{-0.2})(0.0001)\approx-4.093654\).
8. \(Q'=-600p^{-2.5}\); \(pQ'/Q=-1.5\).
9. \(V=2000\), \(V'=20\), and \(pV'/V=0.5\).
10. A 1% relative increase in 0.04 is \(0.04\times0.01=0.0004\), yielding 0.0404 (4.04%), while one percentage point gives 0.05 (5%).
