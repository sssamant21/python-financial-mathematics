## Learning objectives

Identify kinks in financial payoff functions; derive left- and right-hand derivatives; distinguish directional sensitivities from a two-sided derivative; apply piecewise analysis to options, caps, and floors; and design reliable scenario and finite-difference checks near thresholds.

## 1. Why kinks matter

A first-order Taylor approximation assumes a derivative at the expansion point. Many financial contracts contain **piecewise** payoffs, such as \(\max(S-K,0)\), \(\min(r,c)\), or a fee that changes above a threshold. Their values can be continuous while their slopes jump.

For a function \(f\) at \(x_0\), define the one-sided derivatives

\[
f'_-(x_0)=\lim_{h\to0^-}\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h},
\qquad
f'_+(x_0)=\lim_{h\to0^+}\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}.
\]

A finite two-sided derivative exists precisely when both one-sided derivatives exist as finite values and agree. If they differ, the point is a **kink** and no unique ordinary tangent-line sensitivity exists.

## 2. Call-option payoff at expiry

For strike \(K>0\) and underlying price \(S\ge0\), the intrinsic payoff of a European call at expiry is

\[
g(S)=\max(S-K,0).
\]

For \(S<K\), \(g'(S)=0\); for \(S>K\), \(g'(S)=1\). At \(S=K\),

\[
g'_-(K)=0,\qquad g'_+(K)=1.
\]

Thus the payoff is continuous but not differentiable at the strike.

**Worked example:** Let \(K=100\). At \(S=100\), a +2 price shock increases payoff by 2, while a −2 shock leaves payoff at zero. A single linear sensitivity cannot reproduce both outcomes.

This describes **expiry payoff**, not the delta of an option's pre-expiry market price. Pre-expiry valuation depends on time, volatility, rates, dividends, and the pricing model; it may be smooth even though the terminal payoff has a kink.

## 3. Put payoff

For the corresponding put payoff

\[
p(S)=\max(K-S,0),
\]

the left derivative at \(K\) is \(-1\) and the right derivative is \(0\). For \(S<K\), the slope is \(-1\); for \(S>K\), it is \(0\).

A short position reverses the signs of these sensitivities. Sign conventions must be stated before interpreting an exposure report.

## 4. Capped and floored payments

Consider a payment based on a reference rate \(r\), capped at \(c\):

\[
f(r)=N\min(r,c),\qquad N>0.
\]

At \(r=c\), the left derivative is \(N\), and the right derivative is \(0\). If the payment is also floored at \(a<c\),

\[
f(r)=N\min(\max(r,a),c),
\]

then the derivative is zero below \(a\), equals \(N\) strictly between \(a\) and \(c\), and is zero above \(c\). At the floor \(a\), the one-sided derivatives are \(0\) and \(N\); at the cap \(c\), they are \(N\) and \(0\).

These are sensitivities of the **payment rule**, not necessarily of a discounted, stochastic market value.

## 5. Directional approximation at a kink

At a kink \(x_0\), a direction-aware approximation uses

\[
f(x_0+h)\approx
\begin{cases}
f(x_0)+f'_+(x_0)h,&h>0,\\
f(x_0)+f'_-(x_0)h,&h<0.
\end{cases}
\]

For a piecewise-linear function such as a call payoff, this expression is exact for shocks that remain within the adjacent branches. For nonlinear branches, it is only a local approximation and still needs error analysis.

Do **not** average one-sided derivatives and present the result as the ordinary derivative. The average can be a numerical summary, but it is not a valid two-sided derivative at a genuine kink.

## 6. Finite-difference traps

A centered finite difference is

\[
D_h^{(c)}f(x)=\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h},\quad h>0.
\]

At the call strike \(S=K\), this gives \(1/2\) for any \(h>0\) with \(K-h\ge0\). Yet the actual one-sided slopes are 0 and 1; the centered estimate is **not** a derivative.

Instead, compute

\[
D_h^-f(x)=\frac{f(x)-f(x-h)}h,\qquad
D_h^+f(x)=\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}h
\]

and compare their behavior as \(h\downarrow0\). Use perturbations that remain inside the valid input domain.

At points away from the kink, a large difference step can cross the threshold and produce a misleading slope. The step must be small relative to the distance to the nearest threshold, while not so small that floating-point cancellation dominates.

## 7. Discounted piecewise cash flows

Suppose a payoff \(g(S)\) occurs at a fixed future time \(t\) and is discounted at a fixed continuous rate \(r\):

\[
V(S)=e^{-rt}g(S).
\]

With \(r,t\) fixed, the one-sided sensitivities are

\[
V'_\pm(S)=e^{-rt}g'_\pm(S).
\]

For a call payoff at \(S=K\), these are \(0\) and \(e^{-rt}\). Discounting scales the slopes but does not remove the kink. This is a simplified deterministic payoff calculation, **not** a full option-pricing model.

## 8. Portfolio aggregation and cancellation

For a portfolio of piecewise payoffs \(F(S)=\sum_i w_i f_i(S)\), one-sided derivatives, when they exist, add:

\[
F'_\pm(S)=\sum_i w_i f'_{i,\pm}(S).
\]

A portfolio can be smooth even if its components are not: for example,

\[
\max(S-K,0)-\max(K-S,0)=S-K.
\]

The left and right slopes of this call-minus-put **payoff identity** are both 1. This algebraic identity is not by itself a complete market-pricing put-call parity statement.

## 9. Operational sensitivity reporting

A threshold-aware report should include the baseline, threshold, distance to threshold, left slope, right slope, exact upward and downward scenarios, and whether a two-sided derivative exists. For exposure near a strike or cap, prefer direct scenario revaluation to an unjustified single-delta approximation.

A kink does not imply the function is discontinuous. Conversely, a jump discontinuity can prevent finite one-sided derivatives altogether; do not treat every threshold as a simple kink.

## 11. Exercises and worked solutions

**1.** Define left and right derivatives and state when a two-sided derivative exists.

*Solution:* They are limits of difference quotients from negative and positive shocks. The two-sided derivative exists when both finite limits agree.

**2.** Find the one-sided slopes of \(g(S)=\max(S-100,0)\) at \(S=100\).

*Solution:* \(g'_-(100)=0\), \(g'_+(100)=1\).

**3.** Evaluate the call payoff at \(S=98,100,102\), with strike 100.

*Solution:* Respectively \(0,0,2\).

**4.** Find the one-sided slopes of \(p(S)=\max(100-S,0)\) at 100.

*Solution:* Left \(-1\), right \(0\).

**5.** Compute the centered difference for the call payoff at strike 100 with step \(h=2\).

*Solution:* \([g(102)-g(98)]/4=(2-0)/4=0.5\); it is not the two-sided derivative.

**6.** Find the slopes of \(f(r)=1000\min(r,0.05)\) immediately below and above \(r=0.05\).

*Solution:* Left slope \(1000\), right slope \(0\).

**7.** For \(f(r)=1000\min(\max(r,0.02),0.05)\), list slopes below the floor, inside the band, and above the cap.

*Solution:* \(0,1000,0\), respectively.

**8.** If a call payoff is discounted by \(e^{-0.04(2)}\), what are its one-sided sensitivities at strike?

*Solution:* \(0\) and \(e^{-0.08}\approx0.923116\).

**9.** Show that a call-minus-put payoff at the same strike equals \(S-K\).

*Solution:* For \(S\ge K\), the difference is \((S-K)-0\). For \(S<K\), it is \(0-(K-S)=S-K\).

**10.** Explain why a pre-expiry option's delta should not be identified with the slope of its expiry payoff.

*Solution:* Pre-expiry value depends on the full pricing model, including uncertainty and time value; the expiry payoff alone omits these inputs.

**11.** What must be checked before applying a finite-difference step near a cap?

*Solution:* Whether the step crosses the cap, whether inputs remain in-domain, and whether one-sided results differ.

**12.** Can two nonsmooth positions combine to form a smooth portfolio?

*Solution:* Yes. The call-minus-put payoff identity has derivative 1 everywhere, including the strike, even though the individual payoff slopes jump.
