## Learning objectives

Derive the first-order Taylor approximation; distinguish an approximate level from an approximate change; calculate interest-rate and price sensitivities; quantify approximation errors; and identify when local linearization is unreliable.

## 1. From a derivative to a local model

For a differentiable function \(f\) at \(x_0\),

\[
f'(x_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}.
\]

Consequently, for sufficiently small \(h\),

\[
\boxed{f(x_0+h)\approx f(x_0)+f'(x_0)h.}
\]

The **first-order Taylor polynomial** around \(x_0\) is

\[
T_1(x)=f(x_0)+f'(x_0)(x-x_0).
\]

This is the tangent line. It approximates a **level**; the corresponding change approximation is

\[
\boxed{\Delta f\approx f'(x_0)\Delta x}.
\]

If \(f\) is differentiable at \(x_0\), the remainder satisfies \(f(x_0+h)-T_1(x_0+h)=o(h)\) as \(h\to0\). This alone does not guarantee a uniform error bound for large shocks.

## 2. Deriving the error term

If \(f\) is twice continuously differentiable on an interval between \(x_0\) and \(x_0+h\), Taylor's theorem gives

\[
f(x_0+h)=f(x_0)+f'(x_0)h+\frac{f''(\xi)}2h^2
\]

for some \(\xi\) between \(x_0\) and \(x_0+h\). Thus

\[
E_1=f(x_0+h)-T_1(x_0+h)=\frac{f''(\xi)}2h^2.
\]

If \(|f''(x)|\le M\) throughout the interval,

\[
\boxed{|E_1|\le\frac M2h^2.}
\]

The bound requires an actual curvature bound over the entire shock interval. It must not be inferred solely from \(f''(x_0)\).

## 3. Worked polynomial example

For \(f(x)=x^2\) at \(x_0=3\), \(f(3)=9\) and \(f'(3)=6\). Therefore

\[
T_1(3+h)=9+6h.
\]

At \(h=0.2\), the linear estimate is \(10.2\), while the exact value is \(3.2^2=10.24\). The signed error (exact minus estimate) is \(0.04=h^2\).

At \(h=-0.2\), the linear estimate is \(7.8\), while the exact value is \(7.84\). The same positive curvature makes the tangent line underestimate on both sides.

## 4. Financial application: continuous discounting

For a positive cash flow \(C\) paid after \(t\) years at continuously compounded decimal rate \(r\),

\[
V(r)=Ce^{-tr},\qquad V'(r)=-tV(r).
\]

At a baseline rate \(r_0\), the first-order rate-shock approximation is

\[
\boxed{V(r_0+h)\approx V(r_0)(1-th)}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{\Delta V\approx-tV(r_0)h}.
\]

Take \(C=10000\), \(t=5\), \(r_0=0.04\), and a **10-basis-point** increase \(h=0.001\):

\[
V_0=10000e^{-0.2}\approx8187.307531,
\]

\[
\Delta V_{\mathrm{linear}}=-5V_0(0.001)\approx-40.936538.
\]

The exact change is

\[
\Delta V_{\mathrm{exact}}=V_0(e^{-0.005}-1)
=V_0\operatorname{expm1}(-0.005)\approx-40.834366.
\]

The linear approximation is more negative by approximately \(0.102172\). Since \(V''(r)=t^2V(r)>0\), the tangent line underestimates the exact value for any nonzero shock.

For stable evaluation of small exponential changes, Python's \`math.expm1(z)\` calculates \(e^z-1\) without subtracting two nearly equal floating-point values.

## 5. Financial application: duration-like sensitivity

Define the normalized sensitivity

\[
D(r)=-\frac{V'(r)}{V(r)}
\]

for positive \(V\). Then

\[
\frac{\Delta V}{V}\approx-D(r_0)\Delta r.
\]

For continuous discounting of a single fixed cash flow, \(D=t\). For multiple fixed positive cash flows,

\[
V(r)=\sum_{i=1}^n C_i e^{-t_i r},
\qquad
V'(r)=-\sum_{i=1}^n t_i C_i e^{-t_i r},
\]

and

\[
D(r)=\frac{\sum_i t_i C_i e^{-t_i r}}{\sum_i C_i e^{-t_i r}}.
\]

This is a present-value-weighted average time for the continuous-rate convention. It is not automatically identical to modified duration defined under periodic yield conventions.

## 6. Financial application: revenue and marginal analysis

If revenue is \(R(q)=40q-0.1q^2\), then \(R'(q)=40-0.2q\). At \(q_0=100\),

\[
R(100)=3000,\qquad R'(100)=20.
\]

A five-unit quantity increase gives

\[
R(105)\approx3000+20(5)=3100.
\]

The exact revenue is \(R(105)=3097.5\), so the first-order approximation overestimates by \(2.5\), consistent with \(R''=-0.2<0\).

## 7. Comparing exact and approximate changes

Define

\[
E_{\mathrm{signed}}=f(x_0+h)-T_1(x_0+h),\qquad
E_{\mathrm{abs}}=|E_{\mathrm{signed}}|.
\]

If the exact change is nonzero, the **relative change-approximation error** can be defined as

\[
E_{\mathrm{rel,change}}=
\frac{|\Delta f_{\mathrm{exact}}-\Delta f_{\mathrm{linear}}|}
{|\Delta f_{\mathrm{exact}}|}.
\]

This quantity is undefined if the exact change is zero and can be unstable for tiny exact changes. An alternative is to scale error by the baseline level when that baseline is nonzero and economically meaningful. State the chosen denominator explicitly.

## 8. Step-size experiment

For the discount model, compare shocks \(h=0.01,0.005,0.001,0.0001\). Record exact change, first-order change, signed error, and absolute error. When \(h\) is halved, the leading \(O(h^2)\) error should shrink by approximately a factor of four in the asymptotic regime.

This is an expected mathematical trend, **not a substitute for recorded runtime evidence**. Very small shocks may expose floating-point cancellation if differences are computed naively.

## 9. Boundaries and assumptions

- The approximation is **local**: large shocks require direct revaluation or higher-order analysis.
- The derivative must exist at the baseline, and a Taylor remainder bound needs additional smoothness.
- For capped, floored, or piecewise payoff functions, a kink can invalidate a two-sided derivative.
- Fixed cash-flow sensitivities may not apply when cash flows change with the rate.
- The sign of the approximation error depends on curvature and shock interval.
- Always report shock units: 1 basis point = \(0.0001\) in decimal-rate units; 1 percentage point = \(0.01\).
- Numerical agreement at a few points does not prove an analytic derivative is correct.
