## Learning objectives

Derive first- and second-order Taylor remainder bounds; distinguish an observed error from a guaranteed upper bound; construct interval-wide derivative bounds; assess financial rate shocks; and document assumptions, units, and failure conditions.

## 1. Approximation and remainder

For a function \(f\), expansion point \(x_0\), and shock \(h\),

\[
T_1=f(x_0)+f'(x_0)h,\qquad
T_2=T_1+\frac{f''(x_0)}2h^2.
\]

The **signed remainder** of order \(n\) is \(R_n=f(x_0+h)-T_n\). The **absolute error** is \(|R_n|\). An error bound \(B_n\) is a nonnegative quantity proven to satisfy \(|R_n|\le B_n\). An observed error from one experiment is not, by itself, a guarantee for other shocks.

## 2. First-order Lagrange bound

Suppose \(f\) has a continuous second derivative on the closed interval connecting \(x_0\) and \(x_0+h\). Taylor's theorem implies

\[
R_1=\frac{f''(\xi)}2h^2
\]

for some intermediate \(\xi\). If \(|f''(x)|\le M_2\) throughout that interval, then

\[
\boxed{|R_1|\le \frac{M_2}{2}|h|^2.}
\]

The constant \(M_2\) must cover the **entire interval**, not only the expansion point.

## 3. Second-order Lagrange bound

If \(f\) has a continuous third derivative on the shock interval,

\[
R_2=\frac{f'''(\xi)}6h^3.
\]

For \(|f'''(x)|\le M_3\) throughout that interval,

\[
\boxed{|R_2|\le \frac{M_3}{6}|h|^3.}
\]

The order \(O(h^3)\) describes behavior as \(h\to0\) under the stated smoothness assumptions. A finite bound requires a valid \(M_3\).

## 4. Polynomial example

For \(f(x)=x^3\) about \(x_0=2\),

\[
f(2+h)=8+12h+6h^2+h^3.
\]

The quadratic remainder is exactly \(R_2=h^3\). Because \(f'''(x)=6\), the bound \(M_3|h|^3/6=|h|^3\) is exact.

For the linear approximation, \(f''(x)=6x\). On \(x\in[2,2.1]\), \(M_2=12.6\), giving \(|R_1|\le6.3(0.1)^2=0.063\). The actual linear error is \(0.061\), so the bound holds but is not tight.

## 5. Discounted cash flow: analytic bounds

For a fixed positive cash flow \(C\) at time \(t\ge0\), discounted continuously at decimal rate \(r\),

\[
V(r)=Ce^{-tr}.
\]

The derivatives are

\[
V'(r)=-tV(r),\quad V''(r)=t^2V(r),\quad
V'''(r)=-t^3V(r).
\]

Let \(r_0\) be the baseline and \(h\) the shock. Set \(r_{\min}=\min(r_0,r_0+h)\). Because \(V(r)\) decreases with \(r\) for \(t>0\), the maximum magnitude of each derivative over the interval occurs at \(r_{\min}\). Hence

\[
M_2=t^2Ce^{-tr_{\min}},\qquad
M_3=t^3Ce^{-tr_{\min}}.
\]

The guaranteed bounds are

\[
\boxed{B_1=\frac{t^2Ce^{-tr_{\min}}}{2}|h|^2},\qquad
\boxed{B_2=\frac{t^3Ce^{-tr_{\min}}}{6}|h|^3}.
\]

These formulas also cover \(t=0\), when both bounds are zero. They assume fixed cash flow and a single continuously compounded rate. Large negative rates or extreme maturities may overflow floating-point arithmetic; such cases must be reported rather than silently treated as valid.

## 6. Worked 10-basis-point shock

Choose \(C=10000\), \(t=5\), \(r_0=0.04\), and \(h=0.001\). Then

\[
V_0=10000e^{-0.2}\approx8187.307531,
\quad r_{\min}=0.04.
\]

The first-order and second-order changes are

\[
\Delta V_1=-tV_0h,\qquad
\Delta V_2=-tV_0h+\frac{t^2V_0}{2}h^2.
\]

The exact change is computed stably as

\[
\Delta V_{\rm exact}=V_0\operatorname{expm1}(-th).
\]

For this upward rate shock, the bounds simplify to

\[
B_1=\frac{25V_0}{2}(0.001)^2\approx0.1023413,
\]

\[
B_2=\frac{125V_0}{6}(0.001)^3\approx0.00017057.
\]

The actual absolute errors are below these bounds. Notice that \(B_1\) is a bound on the *linear approximation error*, not the full valuation change.

## 7. Multi-cash-flow portfolio

For positive fixed cash flows \(C_i\) at nonnegative times \(t_i\),

\[
V(r)=\sum_i C_ie^{-rt_i}.
\]

The derivatives satisfy

\[
V''(r)=\sum_i t_i^2C_ie^{-rt_i},\qquad
V'''(r)=-\sum_i t_i^3C_ie^{-rt_i}.
\]

Therefore valid interval-wide bounds are

\[
M_2=\sum_i t_i^2C_ie^{-r_{\min}t_i},\qquad
M_3=\sum_i t_i^3C_ie^{-r_{\min}t_i}.
\]

Use \(B_1=M_2h^2/2\) and \(B_2=M_3|h|^3/6\). Positivity of cash flows makes this particularly straightforward. For signed cash flows, sums of absolute derivative contributions can provide conservative bounds; do not assume the positive-cash-flow formulas remain tight.

## 8. Bound tightness and shock direction

A bound may be conservative because it uses the largest derivative magnitude anywhere on the interval. For \(h<0\), the minimum rate is \(r_0+h\), where discount values are higher; the bound adjusts automatically. Always use \(|h|\) in the error magnitude.

A useful **bound utilization** metric is \(|R_n|/B_n\) when \(B_n>0\). Values at most one indicate consistency with the bound; a value above one indicates a bug, incorrect assumptions, or floating-point limitations. When \(B_n=0\), inspect the actual error directly rather than dividing by zero.

## 9. Choosing a tolerance

If an application requires error no larger than \(\tau\), accept the approximation only when its **proven** bound \(B_n\le\tau\), subject to floating-point and model assumptions. A computed error below \(\tau\) at one shock is not sufficient to certify an interval of shocks.

The smallest admissible Taylor order depends on the shock range, curvature, derivative bounds, and required precision. If the bound is too large, reduce the shock, increase approximation order with valid smoothness assumptions, or directly revalue the model.

## 10. Limitations and engineering safeguards

- A kink, discontinuity, or domain boundary can invalidate Taylor's theorem assumptions.
- A bound derived for fixed cash flows does not automatically apply to rate-dependent cash flows.
- Floating-point rounding error is **not** covered by the mathematical Taylor remainder alone.
- Derivative estimates from noisy simulation require additional statistical or numerical error analysis.
- State rate units explicitly: 1 basis point is \(0.0001\) in decimal rate units.
- Use \`math.expm1\` for small exponential differences.
- Validate every derivative bound over the full shock interval.
- Do not equate a small approximation remainder with low model risk.

### Worked solutions

1. \(R_n=f(x_0+h)-T_n(x_0+h)\).
2. \(R_1=f''(\xi)h^2/2\), requiring appropriate twice-continuous differentiability on the interval.
3. \(R_2=f'''(\xi)h^3/6\), requiring appropriate three-times-continuous differentiability on the interval.
4. \(R_2=h^3\), because \((2+h)^3=8+12h+6h^2+h^3\).
5. \(M_2=\max_{[2,2.1]}|6x|=12.6\); \(B_1=12.6(0.1)^2/2=0.063\).
6. \(V''=t^2Ce^{-tr}\), \(V'''=-t^3Ce^{-tr}\).
7. For \(t\ge0\), \(e^{-tr}\) is nonincreasing in \(r\), so it is largest at the minimum rate.
8. \(B_2=(125/6)(10000e^{-0.2})(0.001)^3\approx0.00017057\).
9. \(M_3=\sum_i t_i^3C_ie^{-r_{\min}t_i}\); \(B_2=M_3|h|^3/6\).
10. The bound concerns approximation of the *chosen function*, not whether its cash-flow, rate, credit, or behavioral assumptions match reality.
