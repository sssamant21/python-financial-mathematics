## Objectives

Derive second-order Taylor approximations; interpret curvature; apply duration and convexity to fixed cash flows; compare exact, linear, and quadratic revaluation; understand remainder bounds and limitations.

## 1. From tangent line to curvature

The first-order approximation at \(x_0\) is \(T_1(x_0+h)=f(x_0)+f'(x_0)h\). When \(f\) is twice differentiable at \(x_0\), the **second-order Taylor polynomial** is

\[
\boxed{T_2(x_0+h)=f(x_0)+f'(x_0)h+\tfrac12f''(x_0)h^2}.
\]

Thus the predicted change is

\[
\boxed{\Delta f\approx f'(x_0)h+\tfrac12f''(x_0)h^2}.
\]

The linear term changes sign when the shock reverses; the quadratic term has the same sign for \(h\) and \(-h\). Positive \(f''\) means locally convex, while negative \(f''\) means locally concave. Curvature is not itself a guarantee that the approximation works for large shocks.

If \(f\) is twice differentiable at the expansion point, the error is \(o(h^2)\) as \(h\to0\). If \(f\) has a continuous third derivative on the shock interval, the Lagrange remainder is

\[
R_2=\frac{f'''(\xi)}{6}h^3,
\quad |R_2|\le\frac M6|h|^3
\]

provided \(|f'''|\le M\) throughout that interval. A bound on the third derivative at just the baseline is insufficient.

## 2. Worked polynomial example

For \(f(x)=x^3\) at \(x_0=2\),

\[
f(2)=8,\quad f'(2)=12,\quad f''(2)=12.
\]

Therefore

\[
T_1(2+h)=8+12h,\qquad T_2(2+h)=8+12h+6h^2.
\]

The exact expansion is \((2+h)^3=8+12h+6h^2+h^3\). At \(h=0.1\), the exact value is \(9.261\), the linear estimate \(9.2\), and the quadratic estimate \(9.26\). The second-order error is \(+0.001\), precisely \(h^3\).

## 3. Financial model: continuous-discount present value

For a fixed cash flow \(C>0\), horizon \(t\ge0\), and decimal continuously compounded rate \(r\),

\[
V(r)=Ce^{-tr},\quad V'(r)=-tV(r),\quad V''(r)=t^2V(r).
\]

At a baseline \(r_0\), write \(V_0=V(r_0)\). Then

\[
\boxed{V(r_0+h)\approx V_0(1-th+\tfrac12t^2h^2)}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{\frac{\Delta V}{V_0}\approx-th+\tfrac12t^2h^2}.
\]

Define normalized duration-like sensitivity \(D=-V'/V\) and convexity \(K=V''/V\). For this convention, \(D=t\), \(K=t^2\), and

\[
\boxed{\frac{\Delta V}{V}\approx-Dh+\tfrac12Kh^2}.
\]

The shock \(h\) is in **decimal rate units**: 1 basis point = 0.0001; 10 basis points = 0.001; 100 basis points = 0.01. These are not interchangeable with relative percentage changes in the interest rate.

## 4. Worked 10-basis-point shock

Let \(C=10000\), \(t=5\), \(r_0=0.04\), and \(h=0.001\). The baseline value is

\[
V_0=10000e^{-0.2}\approx8187.307531.
\]

The linear value change is \(-5V_0(0.001)\approx-40.936538\). The second-order curvature correction is

\[
\tfrac12(25V_0)(0.001)^2\approx0.102341.
\]

Hence the quadratic value change is approximately \(-40.834197\). The exact change is

\[
\Delta V_{\rm exact}=V_0\operatorname{expm1}(-0.005)
\approx-40.834366.
\]

The quadratic estimate is closer to the exact value than the linear estimate. For this exponential model, the third-order term is negative for a positive rate shock; consequently, the quadratic approximation slightly overestimates the exact shocked value.

## 5. Multiple fixed cash flows

For \(n\) fixed positive cash flows \(C_i\) at horizons \(t_i\ge0\),

\[
V(r)=\sum_{i=1}^n C_i e^{-rt_i},
\]

\[
V'(r)=-\sum_i t_i C_i e^{-rt_i},\qquad
V''(r)=\sum_i t_i^2 C_i e^{-rt_i}.
\]

If \(V>0\), the normalized measures are

\[
D(r)=\frac{\sum_i t_i C_i e^{-rt_i}}{V(r)},\qquad
K(r)=\frac{\sum_i t_i^2 C_i e^{-rt_i}}{V(r)}.
\]

They are present-value-weighted moments of payment time under **continuous compounding**. \(K\ge0\), so positive fixed cash flows have positive rate convexity in this model. Other yield conventions produce different duration and convexity formulas.

**Example:** For \(C_1=100\) at \(t_1=1\) and \(C_2=1100\) at \(t_2=2\), compute \(V,D,K\) at \(r=0.04\), then estimate the impact of a 50-basis-point rate rise \(h=0.005\). The companion lab implements the calculation and compares it with direct revaluation.

## 6. When convexity helps—and when it does not

Second-order Taylor approximation typically improves accuracy for smooth models under sufficiently small shocks. It is **not** universally more accurate for every finite shock. Model-dependent cash flows, optionality, discontinuities, yield-curve reshaping, and changing credit assumptions can invalidate a fixed-cash-flow scalar-rate sensitivity.

For a callable bond or prepayable loan, cash flows may depend on interest rates. Derivatives of a fixed cash-flow model then omit an important source of risk. A full scenario revaluation or an appropriate option-adjusted model may be necessary.

## 7. Numerical error analysis

Define the exact change \(\Delta V_{\rm exact}=V(r+h)-V(r)\), the linear estimate \(\Delta V_1=V'h\), and the quadratic estimate \(\Delta V_2=V'h+\tfrac12V''h^2\).

Use signed errors \(E_j=\Delta V_{\rm exact}-\Delta V_j\) and absolute errors \(|E_j|\). For smooth models with nonzero leading derivatives, first-order error is generally \(O(h^2)\) and second-order error is generally \(O(h^3)\).

A useful experiment evaluates \(h\in\{-0.01,-0.005,-0.001,0.001,0.005,0.01\}\). Record units and exact, linear, and quadratic changes for each shock. Positive and negative shocks need not have identical approximation errors because odd-order terms reverse sign.
