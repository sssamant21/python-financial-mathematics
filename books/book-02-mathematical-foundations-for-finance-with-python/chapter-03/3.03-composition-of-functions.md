Financial calculations often proceed through several stages: a transaction is adjusted for a fee, a resulting cash flow is discounted, and the final amount is compared with a target. **Function composition** describes such pipelines precisely, including the order in which operations occur and which inputs are valid.

## 3.3.1 Learning objectives

By the end of this section, you should be able to define $f\circ g$, evaluate compositions in the correct order, determine their domains, distinguish composition from ordinary multiplication, recognize when order changes a financial result, prove associativity for compatible functions, and implement a small exact-arithmetic financial pipeline in Python.

## 3.3.2 Definition and notation

Let $g:A\to B$ and $f:B\to C$. Their composition is the function

$$(f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x)).$$

The **right-hand function acts first**: compute $g(x)$, then apply $f$ to that result.

For example, if

$$g(x)=2x+3,\qquad f(y)=y^2,$$

then

$$(f\circ g)(x)=(2x+3)^2,$$

while

$$(g\circ f)(x)=2x^2+3.$$

At $x=1$, the first expression is 25 and the second is 5. Thus composition is generally **not commutative**.

Composition is also not multiplication: $(f\circ g)(x)$ means $f(g(x))$, whereas $(fg)(x)$ usually means $f(x)g(x)$.

## 3.3.3 The domain of a composition

If $g$ is defined on $D_g$ and $f$ on $D_f$, the domain of $f\circ g$ is

$$D_{f\circ g}=\{x\in D_g:g(x)\in D_f\}.$$

Both requirements matter. An input may be valid for the inner function yet produce an output that the outer function cannot accept.

**Worked example:** Let $g(x)=x-5$ with domain $\mathbb R$, and let $f(y)=\sqrt{y}$ with domain $[0,\infty)$. Then

$$(f\circ g)(x)=\sqrt{x-5}$$

has domain $[5,\infty)$. Although $g$ accepts negative numbers, the outer square-root function restricts the composition.

For $g(x)=1/(x-2)$ and $f(y)=1/y$, the composition simplifies algebraically to $x-2$. However, its original domain still excludes $x=2$ (where $g$ is undefined). Simplification must not silently restore an excluded input.

## 3.3.4 Financial example: net cash flow followed by discounting

Consider a synthetic transaction with gross future cash flow $G$, a fee $F$ deducted at the same future date, and a one-period discount rate $r$.

Define the net-cash-flow function

$$N(G)=G-F$$

and the discount function

$$D(C)=\frac{C}{1+r}.$$

Then the present value of the **future net cash flow** is

$$(D\circ N)(G)=\frac{G-F}{1+r}.$$

Assume $G\ge F\ge0$ and $r>-1$ for this example. These restrictions make net cash flow nonnegative and keep the discount denominator positive. A more general cash-flow model could allow negative amounts, but its domain would need to say so.

Let $G=110$, $F=10$, and $r=1/10$. Then

$$N(110)=100,\qquad D(100)=\frac{100}{11/10}=\frac{1000}{11}.$$

The exact present value is $1000/11$, approximately $90.91$ currency units. Keep the exact fraction during calculation; round only for display.

### A different financial timing assumption

If the fee is instead paid **today**, the appropriate expression is

$$D(G)-F=\frac{G}{1+r}-F.$$

For the same values,

$$\frac{110}{11/10}-10=100-10=90.$$

The difference is

$$\frac{G-F}{1+r}-\left(\frac{G}{1+r}-F\right)
=\frac{Fr}{1+r}.$$

At $F=10$ and $r=1/10$, this difference is $10/11$ currency units. The calculations differ because the fee occurs at a different time—not because one algebraic method is automatically preferable. Always specify cash-flow timing.

## 3.3.5 Composition of affine functions

An affine function has the form $f(x)=ax+b$. Let

$$f(x)=ax+b,\qquad g(x)=cx+d.$$

Then

$$(f\circ g)(x)=a(cx+d)+b=acx+ad+b,$$

whereas

$$(g\circ f)(x)=c(ax+b)+d=acx+cb+d.$$

Although the coefficient of $x$ is the same for real scalars, the intercepts generally differ.

For $f(x)=2x+3$ and $g(x)=4x+5$,

$$(f\circ g)(x)=8x+13,\qquad(g\circ f)(x)=8x+17.$$

At $x=1$, the outputs are 21 and 25 respectively. This is a useful minimal test for detecting reversed function order.

## 3.3.6 Associativity and identity

When the functions have compatible domains and codomains,

$$h\circ(g\circ f)=(h\circ g)\circ f.$$

Both sides evaluate to $h(g(f(x)))$. **Composition is associative**, even though it is generally not commutative.

The identity function $I(x)=x$ leaves a function unchanged:

$$I\circ f=f,\qquad f\circ I=f$$

on the appropriate domains. These laws help organize longer financial workflows into reusable stages.

## 3.3.8 Common mistakes

- Evaluating $f$ before $g$ when reading $f\circ g$.
- Confusing composition with multiplication.
- Checking only the inner function's domain.
- Restoring excluded inputs after algebraic simplification.
- Assuming composition is commutative.
- Treating fees at different dates as equivalent.
- Rounding intermediate amounts and introducing avoidable discrepancies.
- Calling two pipelines economically equivalent when they encode different timing assumptions.

## 3.3.9 Exercises

**1.** Let $f(x)=3x-2$ and $g(x)=x^2+1$. Find $(f\circ g)(x)$ and $(g\circ f)(x)$.

**2.** Let $f(x)=1/x$ and $g(x)=x-4$. Find the domain of $f\circ g$.

**3.** Let $f(y)=\sqrt{y}$ and $g(x)=9-x^2$. Find the real domain of $f\circ g$.

**4.** A future payment is 220 currency units, with a fee of 20 deducted at payment and a discount rate of 10%. Find the present value of the future net payment exactly.

**5.** Recompute Exercise 4 if the fee is paid immediately instead. Explain the difference.

**6.** For $f(x)=2x+3$, $g(x)=4x+5$, and $h(x)=3x-1$, verify $(f\circ(g\circ h))(1)=((f\circ g)\circ h)(1)$.

**7.** Suppose $g(x)=1/(x-2)$ and $f(y)=1/y$. Simplify $f(g(x))$ and state its correct original domain.

### Worked answers

1. $f(g(x))=3x^2+1$. $g(f(x))=(3x-2)^2+1=9x^2-12x+5$.
2. $f(g(x))=1/(x-4)$, so $x\in\mathbb R\setminus\{4\}$.
3. Require $9-x^2\ge0$, giving $-3\le x\le3$.
4. $(220-20)/(11/10)=2000/11$ currency units.
5. $220/(11/10)-20=200-20=180$. The future-fee scenario is larger by $20/11$ currency units because that fee is also discounted.
6. $h(1)=2$, $g(2)=13$, $f(13)=29$. Both parenthesizations yield 29.
7. The simplified formula is $x-2$, but the domain remains $\mathbb R\setminus\{2\}$ because the inner function is undefined at 2.
