Monotonicity describes whether outputs consistently rise or fall as inputs increase. Bounds describe how small or large an output can be. Together they let us reason about financial decisions without needing to inspect every point of a graph.

## 3.6.1 Learning outcomes

You will learn to distinguish strictly increasing, nondecreasing, strictly decreasing, and nonincreasing functions; calculate exact extrema on finite domains; derive bounds algebraically; identify how discontinuities and volume discounts defeat simple monotonicity claims; and validate these properties in Python.

## 3.6.2 Definitions

For an ordered domain $D\subseteq\mathbb R$, a function $f$ is **nondecreasing** if

$$x_1<x_2\Longrightarrow f(x_1)\le f(x_2).$$

It is **strictly increasing** if the conclusion is $f(x_1)<f(x_2)$. Similarly, a function is **nonincreasing** if $f(x_1)\ge f(x_2)$, and **strictly decreasing** if $f(x_1)>f(x_2)$ whenever $x_1<x_2$.

A constant function is both nondecreasing and nonincreasing, but neither strictly increasing nor strictly decreasing on a domain with at least two distinct inputs. On a singleton domain, the strict conditions are vacuously true; this is a logical convention, not evidence of economic growth or decline.

A number $L$ is a **lower bound** for $f$ on $D$ if $L\le f(x)$ for all $x\in D$. An **upper bound** $U$ satisfies $f(x)\le U$ for all $x\in D$. A minimum or maximum must also be *attained* by some input in $D$.

For example, $f(x)=x$ on $(0,1)$ has lower bound 0 and upper bound 1, but neither a minimum nor a maximum. Its infimum is 0 and supremum is 1. On $[0,1]$, both extrema are attained.

## 3.6.3 Linear financial models

For a fixed price $p$, variable cost $v$, and fixed cost $F$, profit is

$$\Pi(q)=(p-v)q-F,\qquad q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}.$$

Let $m=p-v$ be the contribution margin. For any $q_2>q_1$,

$$\Pi(q_2)-\Pi(q_1)=m(q_2-q_1).$$

Because $q_2-q_1>0$, the sign of the difference is determined entirely by $m$:

- If $m>0$, profit is strictly increasing.
- If $m=0$, profit is constant.
- If $m<0$, profit is strictly decreasing.

This is a proof over the entire stated domain, not merely a conclusion drawn from a few sampled points.

### Capacity-limited bounds

Suppose $q\in\{0,1,\ldots,Q\}$, with integer capacity $Q\ge0$. For $m>0$,

$$-F=\Pi(0)\le\Pi(q)\le\Pi(Q)=mQ-F.$$

For $m<0$, the inequalities reverse in terms of which endpoint attains the extrema:

$$mQ-F=\Pi(Q)\le\Pi(q)\le\Pi(0)=-F.$$

For $m=0$, both bounds coincide at $-F$.

**Worked example:** With $p=20$, $v=12$, $F=40$, and $Q=6$, profit is $\Pi(q)=8q-40$. Its minimum is $-40$ at $q=0$ and maximum is 8 at $q=6$. Both bounds are attained.

With $p=5$, $v=8$, $F=40$, and the same capacity, profit is $-3q-40$. Its maximum is $-40$ at zero and minimum is $-58$ at six items.

## 3.6.4 Monotonicity and break-even reasoning

For a strictly increasing profit function, a nonnegative-profit threshold—when it exists—divides feasible quantities into a loss region and a nonnegative-profit region. If the exact zero occurs between two integer quantities, the first nonnegative-profit quantity is the ceiling of the algebraic root.

For strictly decreasing profit, selling additional units cannot improve profit under the model. A quantity-based break-even strategy that assumes increasing profit would be invalid.

Monotonicity also helps validate inverse calculations: a strictly monotone function is injective on its domain, so it has an inverse from its range back to its domain. A constant profit function is not injective on a multi-point quantity domain.

## 3.6.5 Why piecewise models can break monotonicity

Consider an all-units volume discount:

$$R(q)=\begin{cases}12q,&0\le q<10,\\10q,&q\ge10,\end{cases}\qquad q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}.$$

Each branch is increasing within its own region. Yet

$$R(9)=108,\qquad R(10)=100.$$

The output *decreases* when quantity rises from 9 to 10. Therefore the complete function is not nondecreasing, even though both branches have positive slopes.

This is an important distinction: proving monotonicity branch by branch is insufficient when a boundary jump is present. Compare values on both sides of every threshold.

Similarly, a fixed-cost step can cause profit to fall as volume rises even when contribution margin is positive.

## 3.6.6 Local bounds versus global bounds

A model may be bounded over an operational range but unbounded over its mathematical extension.

For example, $\Pi(q)=8q-40$ on $q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$ has a minimum $-40$ but no finite upper bound. Restricting the domain to $q\in\{0,\ldots,6\}$ gives a maximum of 8.

Bounds should always be stated together with the **domain and parameter assumptions**. A claim such as “maximum profit is 8” is incorrect without the capacity restriction.

## 3.6.8 Worked exercises

**1.** Classify $f(x)=4x+7$ on $\mathbb R$ and prove your answer using $f(x_2)-f(x_1)$.

**2.** Find the minimum and maximum of $f(q)=6q-25$ for $q\in\{0,\ldots,10\}$.

**3.** Find the bounds of $g(q)=-2q+15$ on $q\in\{0,\ldots,8\}$. Identify the quantities attaining them.

**4.** Is $h(x)=5$ strictly increasing, nondecreasing, strictly decreasing, or nonincreasing on $[0,10]$?

**5.** For $R(q)=12q$ below 10 items and $R(q)=10q$ at 10 or more items, show a counterexample to nondecreasing revenue.

**6.** Does $f(x)=1/x$ on $(0,\infty)$ have a minimum or maximum? Give its infimum and supremum in the extended real sense.

**7.** A profit model has positive contribution margin but a capacity expansion increases fixed cost at 11 items. Explain why the positive contribution margin alone does not establish global monotonicity.

### Worked solutions

1. $f(x_2)-f(x_1)=4(x_2-x_1)>0$ for $x_2>x_1$, so strictly increasing.
2. Minimum $-25$ at $q=0$; maximum $35$ at $q=10$.
3. Maximum $15$ at $q=0$; minimum $-1$ at $q=8$.
4. Both nondecreasing and nonincreasing; neither strict classification holds on this multi-point domain.
5. $R(9)=108>100=R(10)$ even though $9<10$.
6. Neither extremum is attained. Infimum 0; supremum $+\infty$.
7. The extra contribution from the 11th item may be smaller than the fixed-cost jump. Check the cross-threshold difference explicitly.

## 3.6.9 Common mistakes

- Calling a function strictly increasing when it contains flat segments.
- Confusing a bound with an attained extremum.
- Inferring a global property from a finite sample.
- Forgetting that bounds depend on the domain.
- Checking individual branches but not threshold transitions.
- Confusing the output value of an extremum with the input where it occurs.
- Assuming a positive selling price implies increasing profit; the contribution margin matters.
