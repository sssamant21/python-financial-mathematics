Convexity and concavity describe how a function bends, rather than simply whether it rises or falls. In finance, these ideas help distinguish increasing costs from *accelerating* costs, and increasing profits from profits with *diminishing incremental gains*.

## 3.7.1 Learning objectives

After this section, you should be able to distinguish convexity from monotonicity, interpret the chord inequality, classify quadratic functions, use first and second differences on equally spaced quantities, explain why concave profit models can have interior optima, and verify examples with exact Python arithmetic.

## 3.7.2 The chord definition

Let $D$ be a **convex domain**: whenever $x,y\in D$, every weighted average $tx+(1-t)y$ with $0\le t\le1$ also lies in $D$.

A function $f:D\to\mathbb R$ is **convex** if

$$f(tx+(1-t)y)\le tf(x)+(1-t)f(y)$$

for all $x,y\in D$ and $t\in[0,1]$. In words, the graph lies on or **below** the straight chord joining any two of its points.

It is **concave** if the inequality is reversed:

$$f(tx+(1-t)y)\ge tf(x)+(1-t)f(y).$$

Strict convexity or concavity uses a strict inequality for distinct $x,y$ and $0<t<1$.

A straight-line function is both convex and concave. A convex function need not be increasing: $f(x)=x^2$ decreases for negative $x$ and increases for positive $x$, yet is convex over all real numbers.

## 3.7.3 Quadratics: a complete algebraic test

Consider

$$f(x)=ax^2+bx+c.$$

For any $x,y$ and $t\in[0,1]$, subtract the value at the weighted input from the chord value:

$$tf(x)+(1-t)f(y)-f(tx+(1-t)y)
=a\,t(1-t)(x-y)^2.$$

Since $t(1-t)(x-y)^2\ge0$, the sign depends on $a$:

- $a>0$: strictly convex over the real numbers.
- $a<0$: strictly concave over the real numbers.
- $a=0$: affine, hence both convex and concave.

This proof does not require calculus. Later chapters will connect it to the sign of the second derivative.

### Worked example: a convex cost curve

Suppose total cost at quantity $q$ is modeled by

$$C(q)=2q^2+5q+40,\qquad q\ge0.$$

The positive quadratic coefficient makes its continuous extension strictly convex. The cost increases as quantity rises, and its incremental increases grow:

$$C(0)=40,\quad C(1)=47,\quad C(2)=58,\quad C(3)=73.$$

Successive one-item cost increments are 7, 11, and 15. Their difference is 4.

The quadratic term is a *modeling assumption* that might represent congestion, overtime, or other accelerating costs. It is not an empirical claim about every business.

## 3.7.4 Concave profit and an interior optimum

Assume revenue is $R(q)=25q$. With the cost model above,

$$\Pi(q)=25q-C(q)=-2q^2+20q-40.$$

Profit is strictly concave in the continuous extension because its quadratic coefficient is negative.

Complete the square:

$$\Pi(q)=-2(q-5)^2+10.$$

Since $(q-5)^2\ge0$,

$$\Pi(q)\le10,$$

with equality at $q=5$. Thus the continuous model's unique maximum is 10 currency units at five items.

For the whole-item domain $q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$, $q=5$ is feasible, so it is also the unique discrete maximizer. Compare:

| Quantity | Cost | Revenue | Profit |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 | 73 | 75 | 2 |
| 4 | 92 | 100 | 8 |
| 5 | 115 | 125 | 10 |
| 6 | 142 | 150 | 8 |
| 7 | 173 | 175 | 2 |

If capacity is limited to four items, the maximum feasible profit is instead 8 at $q=4$. **Optimization claims must include the feasible domain.**

## 3.7.5 Discrete curvature and second differences

Whole-item quantities form a discrete domain, not a convex subset of the real numbers. The continuous chord definition therefore does not directly apply to a function defined *only* at integer quantities.

For equally spaced observations $f(0),f(1),\ldots$, define the first difference

$$\Delta f(q)=f(q+1)-f(q)$$

and the second difference

$$\Delta^2f(q)=f(q+2)-2f(q+1)+f(q).$$

Nonnegative second differences indicate a **discretely convex sequence**; nonpositive values indicate a **discretely concave sequence**. These are discrete analogues of curvature, not automatically proofs of a continuous extension's convexity.

For $C(q)=2q^2+5q+40$,

$$\Delta C(q)=4q+7,\qquad\Delta^2 C(q)=4.$$

For $\Pi(q)=-2q^2+20q-40$,

$$\Delta\Pi(q)=18-4q,\qquad\Delta^2\Pi(q)=-4.$$

Cost increments grow; profit increments shrink. These conclusions are valid at every nonnegative integer quantity under the stated formulas.

For observations with unequal horizontal spacing, ordinary second differences are not a valid curvature test without adjusting for spacing.

## 3.7.6 Concavity does not mean decreasing

A concave function may increase, decrease, or first increase and then decrease. The profit example rises up to five items and falls afterward. Likewise, a convex function may first decrease and then increase.

Monotonicity concerns the **sign of changes**; convexity concerns how those changes **themselves evolve**. They answer different questions.

A negative second difference does not mean the output is negative. Profit can be positive while its incremental gains are declining.

## 3.7.7 Averages, uncertainty, and Jensen's inequality

For a convex function and two possible inputs $x,y$ with weights $t,1-t$,

$$f(tx+(1-t)y)\le tf(x)+(1-t)f(y).$$

This is the two-point form of **Jensen's inequality**. For a concave function, the inequality reverses.

For example, with $f(x)=x^2$, equally weighted outcomes 0 and 2 have average input 1:

$$f(1)=1\le\tfrac12f(0)+\tfrac12f(2)=2.$$

Applying a nonlinear function to an average input generally differs from averaging the function's outputs. In finance this matters when analyzing variable costs, nonlinear payoffs, and scenario-based results.

**Important:** This is a mathematical comparison, not a recommendation to take investment risk. Any financial interpretation requires a defensible model and probability assumptions.

## 3.7.9 Exercises

**1.** Determine whether $f(x)=3x^2-2x+1$ is convex or concave on $\mathbb R$. Justify algebraically.

**2.** Determine the curvature of $g(x)=-4x^2+8x-1$. Find its maximizing input and output by completing the square.

**3.** Compute first and second differences for the sequence $2,5,10,17,26$ and classify its discrete curvature.

**4.** Let $C(q)=q^2+3q+20$ and $R(q)=15q$. Find profit, its continuous maximizing quantity, and the maximum profit.

**5.** Suppose the domain in Exercise 4 is restricted to whole-unit quantities $q\in\{0,\ldots,4\}$. Find the feasible maximum profit.

**6.** Use $f(x)=x^2$, $x=0$, $y=4$, and $t=1/2$ to verify the convex chord inequality.

**7.** Explain why a concave profit function need not be decreasing over its entire domain.

### Worked answers

1. Strictly convex because $a=3>0$.
2. Strictly concave. $g(x)=-4(x-1)^2+3$, so the maximum is 3 at $x=1$.
3. First differences: $3,5,7,9$. Second differences: $2,2,2$. The sequence is strictly discretely convex.
4. $\Pi(q)=-q^2+12q-20=-(q-6)^2+16$. The continuous maximum is 16 at $q=6$.
5. On $\{0,\ldots,4\}$, profit increases up to the capacity boundary; $\Pi(4)=-16+48-20=12$.
6. $f(2)=4$ and $\tfrac12 f(0)+\tfrac12 f(4)=8$; thus $4\le8$.
7. Concavity means increments are nonincreasing, not necessarily negative. Profit can increase while its increments shrink, then decrease after an optimum.

## 3.7.10 Common mistakes and acceptance criteria

Common errors include confusing curvature with monotonicity, inferring global convexity from a few samples, using second differences with unequal spacing, forgetting capacity constraints, assuming a discrete domain is a real interval, and treating a model's optimal quantity as an empirically validated business recommendation.
