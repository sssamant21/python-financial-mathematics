A single formula often cannot describe a financial rule over its entire domain. Transaction fees may change at a threshold, unit prices may depend on volume, and fixed operating costs may rise when capacity is expanded. **Piecewise functions** represent these rules without hiding their boundary conditions.

## 3.5.1 Learning objectives

By the end of this section, you should be able to read and write piecewise definitions, identify which branch applies at a threshold, distinguish all-units pricing from marginal-tier pricing, determine continuity or jumps at boundaries, evaluate step costs and resulting profits, and implement and test the cases in Python.

## 3.5.2 Definition and domain partitions

A piecewise function uses different expressions on different subsets of its domain. For example,

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}2,&0\le x\le100,\\5,&x>100.\end{cases}$$

The branches must cover the intended domain and must not assign conflicting outputs to the same input. Here $x=100$ belongs to the **first** branch; $x=100.01$ belongs to the second.

A threshold is not necessarily a discontinuity. Whether a function jumps depends on the formulas and their limiting values, not merely on the existence of multiple branches.

## 3.5.3 Example A: flat transaction fee with a jump

Consider a synthetic transaction amount $A\ge0$ and a flat fee

$$F_{\mathrm{flat}}(A)=
\begin{cases}
2,&0\le A\le100,\\
5,&A>100.
\end{cases}$$

At $A=99.99$, the fee is 2; at $A=100$, it remains 2; and at $A=100.01$, it is 5. The left-hand limit at 100 is 2, but the right-hand limit is 5. This is a **jump discontinuity** at the threshold.

If the transaction's net proceeds are $N(A)=A-F_{\mathrm{flat}}(A)$, then just above 100, net proceeds drop by almost 3 compared with the threshold value. A larger gross transaction can therefore produce smaller net proceeds near the threshold. This is a consequence of the assumed *flat fee schedule*, not a general financial law.

## 3.5.4 Example B: marginal-tier fee without a jump

A different fee policy charges 1% on the first 100 currency units and 2% only on the amount above 100:

$$F_{\mathrm{marginal}}(A)=
\begin{cases}
0.01A,&0\le A\le100,\\
1+0.02(A-100),&A>100.
\end{cases}$$

At 100, the first branch gives 1. The second branch approaches $1+0.02(0)=1$ as $A\to100^+$. Therefore the function is **continuous at 100**.

Its slope changes from 0.01 to 0.02. This is a *kink* rather than a jump. A tiered policy can be continuous even when its marginal rate changes abruptly.

At $A=200$, the marginal-tier fee is $1+0.02(100)=3$, not $0.02(200)=4$. Applying the higher rate to the **entire** amount would define a different fee model.

### Compare the two schedules

| Amount | Flat fee | Marginal-tier fee |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 2 | 0 |
| 99.99 | 2 | 0.9999 |
| 100 | 2 | 1 |
| 100.01 | 5 | 1.0002 |
| 200 | 5 | 3 |

The flat-fee example deliberately charges a minimum fee even for a zero-amount transaction. A real business might instead waive such a fee; the mathematical definition must state the policy.

## 3.5.5 Example C: all-units volume pricing

Suppose every item in an order costs 12 currency units when fewer than 10 items are purchased, but **every** item costs 10 currency units when the order contains 10 or more items. For whole-unit quantity $q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$,

$$p(q)=\begin{cases}12,&0\le q<10,\\10,&q\ge10.\end{cases}$$

Revenue is $R(q)=q\,p(q)$:

$$R(q)=\begin{cases}12q,&0\le q<10,\\10q,&q\ge10.\end{cases}$$

At $q=9$, revenue is 108. At $q=10$, revenue is 100. At $q=11$, revenue is 110. Revenue **falls** when quantity increases from 9 to 10, because the lower price applies to all units.

This is an **all-units discount**. It differs from a marginal discount where the first nine items retain their original price and only later units are discounted. Do not mix these policies in a single formula.

Because the domain is discrete, ordinary real-variable continuity is not the primary property of this model. The relevant operational question is the **difference between neighboring feasible quantities**.

## 3.5.6 Example D: fixed-cost capacity step

A hypothetical operation incurs fixed cost 40 currency units through 10 items, then requires additional capacity with fixed cost 70 for 11 or more items:

$$F(q)=\begin{cases}40,&0\le q\le10,\\70,&q\ge11,\end{cases}\qquad q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}.$$

Let price be 12 and variable cost 5 per item. Then profit is

$$\Pi(q)=7q-F(q).$$

At $q=10$,

$$\Pi(10)=70-40=30.$$

At $q=11$,

$$\Pi(11)=77-70=7.$$

At $q=12$,

$$\Pi(12)=84-70=14.$$

The extra item from 10 to 11 increases contribution by 7 but triggers 30 additional fixed-cost units. The net change is $7-30=-23$ currency units.

This example shows why a smooth linear profit graph can be misleading when fixed costs change at a capacity threshold.

## 3.5.7 Threshold conventions and testing

At every boundary, document whether the condition is $<$, $\le$, $>$, or $\ge$. Then test:

1. A feasible value immediately **below** the threshold.
2. The threshold **itself**.
3. A feasible value immediately **above** the threshold.

For a continuous amount, choose exact rational test values such as 99.99, 100, and 100.01. For whole-unit quantities, use 9, 10, and 11. Also test zero, negative inputs, fractional quantities where prohibited, and large values within the supported model.

A boundary test checks the implementation's branch selection. It does not establish whether the underlying fee policy is commercially appropriate.

## 3.5.9 Common mistakes

- Assigning a threshold input to the wrong branch.
- Leaving a gap in the domain or overlapping branches inconsistently.
- Assuming a piecewise definition must have a jump.
- Applying a marginal-tier rate to the entire amount.
- Confusing an all-units discount with a marginal discount.
- Treating discrete quantity graphs as continuous without saying so.
- Ignoring a fixed-cost step when evaluating profit.
- Using binary floats for exact boundary comparisons without an explicit precision policy.

## 3.5.10 Exercises

**1.** Evaluate $f(x)=3$ for $0\le x<50$ and $f(x)=7$ for $x\ge50$ at $x=49$, $50$, and $51$. Which branch includes the threshold?

**2.** A fee is 2% on the first 200 currency units and 3% only on the excess. Write a continuous piecewise formula and evaluate it at 300.

**3.** A flat fee is 4 through amount 100 and 8 above 100. Determine the left-hand and right-hand limits at 100 and classify continuity.

**4.** A whole-order price is 15 currency units per item for fewer than 5 items and 12 per item for 5 or more. Find revenue at 4, 5, and 6 items. Explain the change from 4 to 5.

**5.** A model has contribution margin 9 currency units/item, fixed cost 50 through quantity 20, and fixed cost 100 for quantity 21 or more. Compute profit at 20 and 21 items.

**6.** Explain why testing $A=100$ alone is insufficient to validate a tiered-fee implementation.

### Worked answers

1. $f(49)=3$, $f(50)=7$, $f(51)=7$. The second branch includes 50.
2. $F(A)=0.02A$ for $0\le A\le200$ and $F(A)=4+0.03(A-200)$ for $A>200$. At 300, the fee is 7.
3. The left limit is 4 and right limit is 8; the function has a jump at 100.
4. $R(4)=60$, $R(5)=60$, $R(6)=72$. The lower all-units price offsets the fifth item's added quantity.
5. $\Pi(20)=9(20)-50=130$; $\Pi(21)=9(21)-100=89$. The extra item reduces profit by 41.
6. A bug might handle the threshold correctly but apply the wrong rate immediately above or below it; all three boundary cases are necessary.
