A graph is a visual representation of a mathematical relationship, not a substitute for stating the model. In finance, graphs can make break-even thresholds, unit economics, and sensitivity to quantity easier to interpret—provided that the axes, domain, and assumptions are correct.

## 3.2.1 Learning objectives

After this section you should be able to identify inputs and outputs on labeled axes; calculate and interpret intercepts and slopes; distinguish discrete attainable points from continuous curves; locate intersections of revenue and cost; recognize how scaling an axis can change visual impressions; and generate and independently validate graph coordinates in Python.

## 3.2.2 From a function to a graph

For a function $f:D\to\mathbb R$, its graph is

$$G_f=\{(x,f(x)):x\in D\}.$$

A graph consists of **ordered pairs**. The horizontal axis normally represents the independent variable and the vertical axis its output. The horizontal and vertical units should be explicit.

For example, if $q$ is items sold and $R(q)$ is revenue, the point $(4,100)$ means *four items correspond to 100 currency units of revenue*. It does not mean four currency units or 100 items.

The graph depends on the domain. If $D=\{0,1,\ldots,6\}$, the mathematical graph contains seven points. Connecting them with a line is a visualization convention or an additional continuous-model assumption; it does not make fractional items attainable.

## 3.2.3 Three related financial graphs

Consider a hypothetical one-product business with:

| Parameter | Value | Units |
|---|---:|---|
| Unit selling price $p$ | 25 | currency/item |
| Variable cost $v$ | 15 | currency/item |
| Fixed cost $F$ | 40 | currency |
| Quantity $q$ | $0,1,\ldots,6$ | items |

Define revenue, total cost, and profit:

$$R(q)=25q,$$

$$C(q)=40+15q,$$

$$\Pi(q)=R(q)-C(q)=10q-40.$$

The following exact coordinates are sufficient to construct the three graphs.

| Quantity | Revenue | Total cost | Profit |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0 | 40 | -40 |
| 1 | 25 | 55 | -30 |
| 2 | 50 | 70 | -20 |
| 3 | 75 | 85 | -10 |
| 4 | 100 | 100 | 0 |
| 5 | 125 | 115 | 10 |
| 6 | 150 | 130 | 20 |

The vertical units in each output column are currency. Every row must satisfy $\Pi(q)=R(q)-C(q)$; this is a useful graph-data invariant.

## 3.2.4 Intercepts and their meaning

An **output-axis intercept** is the output at input zero, provided zero belongs to the domain.

- Revenue intercept: $R(0)=0$. No units sold means no sales revenue in this simplified model.
- Cost intercept: $C(0)=40$. The model incurs 40 currency units of fixed cost even at zero sales.
- Profit intercept: $\Pi(0)=-40$. This is the period's loss at zero sales.

An **input-axis intercept** is an input where the output equals zero. For profit,

$$10q-40=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q=4.$$

At four items, revenue and total cost are both 100, so profit is zero. This is the **break-even point**. A revenue–cost intersection and a profit zero are two representations of the same equation:

$$R(q)=C(q)\iff R(q)-C(q)=0.$$

If the algebraic break-even quantity were fractional while the domain contained only whole items, the line could cross the horizontal axis between points without the discrete profit graph containing a zero-profit point.

## 3.2.5 Slopes as rates of change

For two distinct quantities $q_1$ and $q_2$, the secant slope of a function is

$$\frac{f(q_2)-f(q_1)}{q_2-q_1}.$$

For a linear function $f(q)=aq+b$, this slope equals $a$ for any two distinct inputs.

In the example:

$$\frac{R(4)-R(0)}{4-0}=\frac{100-0}{4}=25,$$

$$\frac{C(4)-C(0)}{4-0}=\frac{100-40}{4}=15,$$

$$\frac{\Pi(4)-\Pi(0)}{4-0}=\frac{0-(-40)}{4}=10.$$

These slopes have units **currency per item**. They describe marginal changes in this *linear model*, not necessarily the prices or costs of an actual business at all volumes.

For discrete quantities, the one-item difference $f(q+1)-f(q)$ is often the most operationally meaningful quantity. Derivatives will be introduced in later chapters.

## 3.2.6 Discrete versus continuous visualization

The domain $\{0,1,\ldots,6\}$ yields seven attainable profit points. A plotted straight line through those points represents the continuous extension

$$\widetilde\Pi(q)=10q-40,\qquad 0\le q\le6.$$

The extension is mathematically valid but may not be financially meaningful for a half-item sale. A good graph caption states whether connecting lines are interpolations or part of the actual domain.

For large discrete domains, points may appear visually connected. This is a rendering effect, not a change in the underlying domain.

## 3.2.7 How graphs can mislead

**Truncated axes.** A vertical axis starting near a data point can exaggerate differences. Always check the scale before interpreting steepness.

**Mixed units.** Plotting revenue in thousands of currency units and costs in single currency units on an unlabeled common axis makes comparisons invalid.

**Unequal axis scaling.** The *visual angle* of a line depends on the axis scales; its numerical slope is defined by the underlying units and coordinate differences.

**Unlabeled assumptions.** Straight-line cost graphs assume constant variable cost. Capacity constraints, bulk discounts, fixed-cost steps, taxes, and nonlinear demand can invalidate extrapolation.

**Extrapolation.** A line that fits quantities 0–6 does not establish that the same unit economics hold at 60,000 items.

**False precision.** Plotting software can draw smooth curves from sparse data; smoothness does not create evidence for unobserved outcomes.

## 3.2.9 Worked comparison: negative contribution margin

Suppose price is 5, unit variable cost is 10, and fixed cost is 40. Then

$$\Pi(q)=-5q-40.$$

The algebraic zero is $q=-8$, outside the nonnegative quantity domain. The graph begins at $(0,-40)$ and moves downward as quantity increases. A line crossing zero at a *negative* input does not establish a feasible business break-even point.

When price equals unit variable cost and fixed cost is zero, profit is identically zero: every nonnegative quantity is a break-even quantity. When price equals unit variable cost but fixed cost is positive, profit is a constant negative value and there is no break-even quantity.

## 3.2.10 Exercises

**1.** For $R(q)=30q$, $C(q)=90+18q$, and whole-item $q\ge0$, derive profit, find its output-axis intercept and slope, and calculate its algebraic break-even quantity.

**2.** For the same model, identify the minimum whole-unit quantity required for nonnegative profit. Explain why it differs from the algebraic break-even value.

**3.** Calculate revenue, cost, and profit at $q=0$, $q=7$, and $q=8$. Verify the profit identity in every row.

**4.** For $\Pi(q)=12q-90$ on $D=\{0,1,\ldots,10\}$, does the graph contain the point $(7.5,0)$? Explain.

**5.** Explain why the apparent steepness of a line changes when the vertical axis switches from currency units to thousands of currency units, even though the business relationship has not changed.

**6.** If $R(q)=20q$ and $C(q)=20q+50$, classify the intersection of revenue and cost for $q\ge0$.

### Companion answers

1. $\Pi(q)=12q-90$; intercept $-90$ currency; slope 12 currency/item; algebraic break-even $q=7.5$.
2. The minimum whole-item quantity with nonnegative profit is 8. The exact zero at 7.5 is not attainable in a whole-item domain.
3. At $q=0$: $(R,C,\Pi)=(0,90,-90)$; at $q=7$: $(210,216,-6)$; at $q=8$: $(240,234,6)$. Each satisfies $R-C=\Pi$.
4. No. The domain contains only integer quantities; $(7.5,0)$ belongs to a continuous extension, not the original discrete graph.
5. The vertical coordinate numbers are rescaled by a factor of 1000. The visual angle changes, while the underlying monetary difference is unchanged.
6. No intersection: cost exceeds revenue by 50 currency units at every quantity.
