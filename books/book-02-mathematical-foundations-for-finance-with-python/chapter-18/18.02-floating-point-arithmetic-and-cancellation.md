## 1. Binary floating-point

Python's ordinary \`float\` is generally IEEE 754 binary64, with a 53-bit significand and an 11-bit exponent field. Many decimal fractions, including \(0.1\), do not have finite binary representations. Python prints a short decimal that round-trips to the stored binary value; this does not imply the stored value is mathematically exact.

For a normal binary64 number, spacing depends on magnitude. \`sys.float_info.epsilon\` is \(2^{-52}\), the distance between \(1\) and the next larger representable number. Under round-to-nearest, the unit roundoff is \(u=2^{-53}\). Neither is a universal absolute-error bound for every operation or magnitude.

## 2. A conditional rounding model

For many correctly rounded basic operations, away from exceptional overflow, underflow, and other special cases, the standard relative-error model is

\[
\operatorname{fl}(a\circ b)=(a\circ b)(1+\delta),\qquad |\delta|\le u.
\]

This is an analysis tool, not a promise that arbitrary expressions have relative error at most \(u\). Error may accumulate across operations, and subtraction can be highly sensitive when operands are nearly equal.

## 3. Catastrophic cancellation

When \(a\approx b\), computing \(a-b\) can discard leading significant digits. Even if the subtraction is itself correctly rounded, small representation errors in \(a\) and \(b\) may dominate the tiny true difference.

For small \(x\),

\[
e^x-1=x+\tfrac12x^2+O(x^3).
\]

Directly evaluating \`math.exp(x)-1\` may lose precision. Use \`math.expm1(x)\`, which is designed to compute \(e^x-1\) accurately for small \(x\).

Likewise, \(\log(1+x)\) should usually be evaluated with \`math.log1p(x)\` when \(x\) is small.

## 4. Stable financial growth calculations

For periodic return \(r>-1\) and \(n\) periods, the compound growth factor is

\[
G=(1+r)^n.
\]

When \(r\) is tiny, forming \(1+r\) can round to exactly one. For finite, in-range results, a stable alternative is

\[
\boxed{G=\exp(n\log(1+r))}
\]

implemented as \`math.exp(n*math.log1p(r))\`. To calculate the *growth increment* \(G-1\) accurately, use

\[
\boxed{\operatorname{expm1}(n\operatorname{log1p}(r))}.
\]

The latter avoids subtracting nearly equal growth factors. These formulas still require overflow, underflow, and domain checks.

## 5. Stable discount factors

A discount factor for \(n\) periods is \(D=(1+r)^{-n}\). For tiny \(r\), evaluate \(\exp(-n\log(1+r))\) using \`math.log1p\`. To compute the *discount change* \(D-1\), use \`math.expm1(-n*math.log1p(r))\` rather than subtracting one from a nearly unit discount factor.

For large negative exponents, underflow can produce zero. A zero result should not automatically be interpreted as a mathematically exact zero.

## 6. Stable aggregation and summation

Adding a small quantity to a much larger one may lose the smaller quantity. Order of summation can affect floating-point results. Python's \`math.fsum\` uses a more accurate summation approach than ordinary \`sum\` for many floating-point sequences.

For example, \`sum([1e16,1,-1e16])\` can produce \(0\), whereas \`math.fsum([1e16,1,-1e16])\` gives \(1\). This illustrates why cash-flow aggregation, portfolio totals, and residual checks may benefit from accurate summation.

## 7. Cancellation in financial annuities

For \(n\) end-of-period payments of amount \(C\), at periodic rate \(r\ne0\), the present value is

\[
PV=C\frac{1-(1+r)^{-n}}{r}.
\]

For tiny \(r\), the numerator subtracts two values close to one. A more stable form is

\[
\boxed{PV=C\frac{-\operatorname{expm1}(-n\operatorname{log1p}(r))}{r}}.
\]

At \(r=0\), the continuous limit is \(PV=Cn\). For extremely small nonzero \(r\), further relative-accuracy analysis or a local series may be appropriate; the stable form substantially reduces the most obvious cancellation.

## 8. Decimal money versus binary floating point

Binary floats are appropriate for many scientific calculations, but accounting rules may require decimal quantization and a specified rounding convention. Python's \`decimal.Decimal\` can represent finite decimal inputs exactly when constructed from strings. It does not automatically correct an invalid financial formula or guarantee accuracy for transcendental approximations.

Do not construct \`Decimal\` from an already rounded binary float when the original decimal text is available and exact decimal interpretation is required.

## 9. Comparison and tolerance discipline

Avoid exact equality for results of independent floating-point calculations unless exactness is guaranteed by the algorithm. \`math.isclose\` uses relative and absolute tolerances; select these based on units, scaling, and the financial decision threshold.

For comparisons against zero, an explicit absolute tolerance is often essential. A tolerance is not a substitute for checking domain restrictions, monotonicity, or independent mathematical benchmarks.

## 10. Limits of stability

Stable formulas do not remove economic model error, estimation uncertainty, or ill-conditioning. Even a stable algorithm may return a result with large forward error when the underlying problem is sensitive to tiny input changes.

The goal is to avoid *avoidable* numerical error and make remaining limitations visible.

## 12. Twenty worked exercises

**1.** How many significant binary digits does binary64 provide?  
*Solution:* 53, including the implicit leading bit for normal values.

**2.** What is machine epsilon near one?  
*Solution:* \(2^{-52}\).

**3.** What is unit roundoff under round-to-nearest?  
*Solution:* \(2^{-53}\).

**4.** Is \(0.1\) represented exactly in binary64?  
*Solution:* No.

**5.** Define catastrophic cancellation.  
*Solution:* Loss of relative accuracy when subtracting nearly equal values.

**6.** Give a stable expression for \(e^x-1\).  
*Solution:* \`math.expm1(x)\`.

**7.** Give a stable expression for \(\log(1+x)\).  
*Solution:* \`math.log1p(x)\`.

**8.** Why can \`1+r\` be problematic for tiny \(r\)?  
*Solution:* It can round to exactly one, losing the increment.

**9.** Give a stable growth factor.  
*Solution:* \`math.exp(n*math.log1p(r))\`.

**10.** Give a stable growth increment.  
*Solution:* \`math.expm1(n*math.log1p(r))\`.

**11.** Give a stable discount factor.  
*Solution:* \`math.exp(-n*math.log1p(r))\`.

**12.** Give a stable discount-factor change.  
*Solution:* \`math.expm1(-n*math.log1p(r))\`.

**13.** State the annuity PV formula for \(r\ne0\).  
*Solution:* \(C[1-(1+r)^{-n}]/r\).

**14.** Give its stable numerator.  
*Solution:* \`-math.expm1(-n*math.log1p(r))\`.

**15.** What is the annuity PV at zero rate?  
*Solution:* \(Cn\).

**16.** What is the exact mathematical sum of \(10^{16}+1-10^{16}\)?  
*Solution:* \(1\).

**17.** Which Python summation routine often handles cancellation better?  
*Solution:* \`math.fsum\`.

**18.** Why use \`Decimal("0.1")\` instead of \`Decimal(0.1)\` for exact decimal intent?  
*Solution:* The string preserves the intended decimal fraction rather than importing its binary approximation.

**19.** Can numerical stability fix a wrong compounding convention?  
*Solution:* No.
