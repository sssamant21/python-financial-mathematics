## Learning objectives

Establish sign-change brackets for continuous functions, derive the bisection method and its error bounds, select meaningful stopping rules, recognize roots that do not change sign, and independently verify cash-flow break-even and yield calculations.

## 1. Roots in financial models

Many financial questions ask for \(x\) such that \(f(x)=0\). Examples include the interest rate that equates a bond price to discounted payments, a project internal rate of return (IRR), or a break-even financing rate.

A numerical root is not automatically a valid financial answer: the domain, units, root uniqueness, and interpretation must also be checked.

## 2. Intermediate Value Theorem

If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(f(a)f(b)<0\), at least one root lies strictly between \(a\) and \(b\). If an endpoint evaluates exactly to zero, that endpoint is already a root.

A sign change is sufficient for existence under continuity, but not necessary: \(f(x)=(x-1)^2\) has a root at \(x=1\) without a sign change. A sign change does not guarantee a *unique* root.

## 3. Bisection algorithm

Start with a valid bracket \([a_0,b_0]\). At iteration \(k\), evaluate

\[
m_k=a_k+\frac{b_k-a_k}{2}.
\]

If \(f(m_k)=0\), stop. Otherwise retain the half-interval whose endpoints have opposite signs. The method preserves a root-containing bracket whenever continuity and the sign condition hold.

After \(k\) bisections, the interval width is

\[
b_k-a_k=\frac{b_0-a_0}{2^k}.
\]

The midpoint of that final interval differs from **some bracketed root** by at most half its width. For a unique root this bounds the error to that root.

## 4. Iteration budget

To guarantee final bracket width no greater than \(\varepsilon_x>0\), use

\[
N=\max\left(0,\left\lceil\log_2\frac{b_0-a_0}{\varepsilon_x}\right\rceil\right).
\]

For an initial width of \(0.2\) and desired width \(10^{-6}\), \(N=\lceil\log_2(200000)\rceil=18\).

The guarantee concerns **interval width**, not the residual \(|f(m)|\). A small residual need not imply a small root error when \(f\) is flat.

## 5. Robust stopping criteria

Prefer a combination of bracket-width tolerance, exact-root detection, and iteration limits. A practical width tolerance can be

\[
b-a\le \mathrm{atol}+\mathrm{rtol}\max(|a|,|b|).
\]

A residual threshold may be recorded as an additional diagnostic, but should not silently replace the bracket guarantee. A floating-point midpoint may equal an endpoint when the interval can no longer be bisected; report **stagnation** rather than pretending convergence.

## 6. Finance example: zero-coupon yield

For a zero-coupon instrument with face value \(F=1000\), current price \(P=900\), and maturity \(T=2\) years, solve

\[
f(r)=\frac{1000}{(1+r)^2}-900=0,\qquad r>-1.
\]

The analytical benchmark is

\[
r^*=\left(\frac{1000}{900}\right)^{1/2}-1
\approx0.054092553389.
\]

A bracket \([0,0.2]\) works: \(f(0)=100>0\) and \(f(0.2)\approx-205.5556<0\). The function is continuous and strictly decreasing on \(r>-1\), so the root is unique.

## 7. Cash-flow NPV example

For an initial outlay of \(1000\) and a single cash inflow of \(1100\) one year later,

\[
NPV(r)=-1000+\frac{1100}{1+r},\qquad r>-1.
\]

The root is \(r=0.10\). A sign-change bracket \([0,0.2]\) contains it. For cash-flow streams with multiple sign changes, the NPV function can have multiple real IRRs; bracketing one root does not establish that it is the only root.

## 8. Numerical evaluation

Evaluate discount factors with \`math.log1p\` and \`math.exp\` when the rate is close to zero and the domain is valid. For the zero-coupon example, \(\exp(-T\log(1+r))\) avoids some avoidable loss from forming \(1+r\).

All function evaluations must be finite. Reject NaN and infinity rather than allowing comparisons to hide invalid arithmetic. Rates \(r\le-1\) are invalid for the chosen real-valued financial formulas.

## 9. Sign tests without multiplying

Checking \`f(a)*f(b) < 0\` can overflow or underflow even if each function value is finite. Test signs directly: one endpoint positive and the other negative. Keep the original signed values in the audit report.

## 10. Independent verification

A checker should recompute the objective equation and bracket signs independently of the bisection implementation. For the zero-coupon example, compare the computed root with the closed-form benchmark and verify that the returned interval still encloses it.

Distinguish **root accuracy**, **financial-model validity**, and **implementation reliability**. Passing a numerical check does not validate market prices, cash-flow timing, or yield conventions.

## 11. Twelve-step hands-on lab

1. State the cash-flow equation and domain \(r>-1\).
2. Compute endpoint residuals at rates \(0\) and \(0.2\).
3. Verify continuity and a strict sign change.
4. Implement a midpoint calculation.
5. Retain the root-containing half-interval.
6. Record every bracket width and midpoint residual.
7. Stop on the width criterion or an exact zero.
8. Enforce a maximum iteration count and detect stagnation.
9. Compare the root with the analytical zero-coupon yield.
10. Solve the one-period NPV break-even example.
11. Reject invalid brackets, nonfinite evaluations, and invalid rate domains.

## 12. Twenty worked exercises

**1.** Define a root of \(f\).  
*Solution:* A value \(x\) satisfying \(f(x)=0\).

**2.** State the sign-change condition.  
*Solution:* \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\) have opposite signs.

**3.** What additional property guarantees a bracketed root?  
*Solution:* Continuity on \([a,b]\).

**4.** Does a sign change guarantee uniqueness?  
*Solution:* No.

**5.** Can an even-multiplicity root lack a sign change?  
*Solution:* Yes; consider \((x-1)^2\).

**6.** Give the bisection midpoint.  
*Solution:* \(a+(b-a)/2\).

**7.** What happens to interval width after one step?  
*Solution:* It is halved.

**8.** Give the width after \(k\) steps.  
*Solution:* \((b_0-a_0)/2^k\).

**9.** Bound the midpoint error relative to a bracketed root.  
*Solution:* At most half the final interval width.

**10.** Find the iteration count for width \(0.2\to10^{-6}\).  
*Solution:* 18 bisections.

**11.** State the zero-coupon pricing equation.  
*Solution:* \(P=F/(1+r)^T\).

**12.** Solve for its annual effective yield.  
*Solution:* \(r=(F/P)^{1/T}-1\).

**13.** Give the yield for \(F=1000,P=900,T=2\).  
*Solution:* Approximately \(0.054092553389\).

**14.** Evaluate \(f(0)\) for the yield equation.  
*Solution:* \(100\).

**15.** Evaluate \(f(0.2)\).  
*Solution:* \(1000/1.44-900\approx-205.5556\).

**16.** Is the zero-coupon yield function decreasing on \(r>-1\)?  
*Solution:* Yes, for positive face value and maturity.

**17.** Solve \(-1000+1100/(1+r)=0\).  
*Solution:* \(r=0.1\).

**18.** Does a small residual always imply a small root error?  
*Solution:* No, particularly near flat functions.

**19.** Why avoid multiplying endpoint residuals to check signs?  
*Solution:* The product can overflow or underflow.
