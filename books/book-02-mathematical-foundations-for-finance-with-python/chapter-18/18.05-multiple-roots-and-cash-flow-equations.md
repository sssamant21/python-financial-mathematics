## Learning objectives

Explain why nonconventional cash flows can produce multiple internal rates of return; transform finite-period NPV equations into polynomials; distinguish sign-change root discovery from proof of completeness; isolate roots with brackets; and compare IRR with NPV at a stated discount rate.

## 1. The NPV equation

For cash flows \(C_0,\ldots,C_n\) at equally spaced periods and a periodic effective rate \(r>-1\),

\[
NPV(r)=\sum_{t=0}^n\frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t}.
\]

An internal rate of return (IRR) is any admissible real root of \(NPV(r)=0\). The discounting convention, time units, and cash-flow signs must be explicit.

For nonzero \(C_0\), define \(z=1/(1+r)>0\). Then

\[
NPV(r)=C_0+C_1z+\cdots+C_nz^n.
\]

Thus, a finite-period IRR problem can be analyzed as a polynomial equation in positive \(z\), with \(r=1/z-1\). The transformation excludes \(r=-1\) and requires \(z>0\).

## 2. Conventional versus nonconventional cash flows

A conventional project often has one initial negative cash flow followed by positive inflows. For strictly positive future inflows, its NPV is strictly decreasing on \(r>-1\):

\[
NPV'(r)=-\sum_{t=1}^n tC_t(1+r)^{-t-1}<0.
\]

If the endpoint behavior crosses zero, there is exactly one admissible IRR.

A nonconventional stream changes sign more than once. Its NPV may be nonmonotonic and can have multiple, repeated, or no admissible real IRRs. A numerical solver returning one root does not establish uniqueness.

## 3. Two-IRR example

Consider the three cash flows

\[
(C_0,C_1,C_2)=(-100,230,-132).
\]

The NPV equation is

\[
-100+\frac{230}{1+r}-\frac{132}{(1+r)^2}=0.
\]

Multiply by \((1+r)^2>0\):

\[
-100(1+r)^2+230(1+r)-132=0,
\]

which simplifies to

\[
-100r^2+30r-2=0.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
100r^2-30r+2=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r=\frac{30\pm\sqrt{900-800}}{200}.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\boxed{r_1=0.10,\qquad r_2=0.20.}
\]

Both rates are admissible (\(r>-1\)), and both make NPV zero.

## 4. The sign pattern

For this example,

\[
NPV(0)=-2,\qquad NPV(0.15)>0,\qquad NPV(0.3)<0.
\]

Hence the intervals \([0,0.15]\) and \([0.15,0.3]\) each contain a root by continuity and the Intermediate Value Theorem.

A single wide interval \([0,0.3]\) has negative NPV at both ends and therefore **does not** form a valid sign-change bracket, even though two roots lie inside it.

## 5. Root scanning and limitations

A practical scanner evaluates NPV on a finite ordered grid and creates a bracket whenever adjacent evaluations have opposite signs. Exact grid-point roots are recorded separately.

**Important limitation:** finite sign-change scanning is a discovery method, not a proof that every root has been found. It can miss two roots within one grid cell and roots of even multiplicity that touch zero without changing sign. Completeness requires additional mathematical analysis, such as polynomial factorization, derivative-based interval partitioning, or certified root isolation.

The accompanying lab explicitly reports that its scan is **not exhaustive**.

## 6. Numerical conditioning

A root \(r^*\) is simple if \(NPV'(r^*)\ne0\). Near a simple root, a small NPV perturbation \(\delta f\) produces approximately

\[
\delta r\approx-\frac{\delta f}{NPV'(r^*)}.
\]

A small derivative can make the rate sensitive to cash-flow rounding or measurement errors. Near repeated roots, sign-change scanning and ordinary Newton iterations are particularly fragile.

## 7. NPV as the decision measure

IRR is not necessarily a unique or sufficient project ranking criterion. NPV at a specified required return measures the project's value under that discount convention.

For the example, at a required return of \(15\%\),

\[
NPV(0.15)=-100+\frac{230}{1.15}-\frac{132}{1.15^2}
\approx0.1890359168>0.
\]

At \(5\%\), NPV is negative. At \(25\%\), NPV is also negative. This pattern shows why selecting one of the two IRRs without examining the NPV profile is misleading.

## 8. Independent validation

A reliable assessment separates:

1. Input validation: finite cash flows, at least one nonzero cash flow, consistent periods and units.
2. Root discovery: candidate brackets and exact grid-point roots.
3. Numerical refinement: a bracket-preserving bisection solver.
4. Independent reference: the transformed polynomial and known roots.
5. Decision context: NPV at the required return, not a single arbitrary IRR.

When the root count is uncertain, state that uncertainty. Never label a finite grid scan a proof of completeness.

## 9. Failure and edge cases

- A cash-flow stream of all zeros makes NPV identically zero; it does not define a unique IRR.
- A stream with all positive cash flows has no IRR on \(r>-1\).
- A repeated root can have no sign change and escape ordinary bracketing.
- Invalid rates \(r\le-1\) must be rejected.
- Nonfinite cash flows, rates, or intermediate NPV results must be rejected.
- A scan grid that does not cover a root cannot discover it.
- IRRs computed using annual cash flows must not be reported as monthly yields.

## 11. Twenty worked exercises

**1.** Define IRR.  
*Solution:* An admissible periodic rate \(r>-1\) satisfying \(NPV(r)=0\).

**2.** State the finite-period NPV formula.  
*Solution:* \(\sum_{t=0}^n C_t(1+r)^{-t}\).

**3.** Define the polynomial variable.  
*Solution:* \(z=1/(1+r)>0\).

**4.** Express the inverse transformation.  
*Solution:* \(r=1/z-1\).

**5.** What is a conventional cash-flow pattern?  
*Solution:* Typically one initial outflow followed by nonnegative inflows, with at least one strictly positive inflow.

**6.** Why is NPV decreasing for strictly positive future inflows?  
*Solution:* Its derivative is strictly negative for \(r>-1\).

**7.** State the nonconventional example.  
*Solution:* \((-100,230,-132)\).

**8.** Give its NPV equation.  
*Solution:* \(-100+230/(1+r)-132/(1+r)^2=0\).

**9.** Give its quadratic in \(r\).  
*Solution:* \(100r^2-30r+2=0\).

**10.** Find the discriminant.  
*Solution:* \(30^2-4(100)(2)=100\).

**11.** Find the smaller IRR.  
*Solution:* \(0.10\).

**12.** Find the larger IRR.  
*Solution:* \(0.20\).

**13.** Compute \(NPV(0)\).  
*Solution:* \(-2\).

**14.** Determine the sign of \(NPV(0.15)\).  
*Solution:* Positive.

**15.** Determine the sign of \(NPV(0.3)\).  
*Solution:* Negative.

**16.** Why is \([0,0.3]\) not a valid sign-change bracket?  
*Solution:* Its endpoint NPVs are both negative.

**17.** Can a repeated root lack a sign change?  
*Solution:* Yes.

**18.** Can a finite grid guarantee that all roots are found?  
*Solution:* No.

**19.** What should guide a project decision when IRR is ambiguous?  
*Solution:* NPV at an appropriately chosen required return, alongside risk and other decision criteria.

## Represented-input NPV and root-merging policy — validation follow-up

The lab's equally spaced integer periods make NPV a rational polynomial for the represented finite cash flows and rate. It now evaluates that polynomial by exact rational Horner arithmetic and rounds the complete result once. Earlier separately rounded discount powers could create false zero NPVs near closely spaced roots. A nonzero result that underflows to zero, or a result outside float range, raises `ValueError`; genuine rational cancellation to zero remains valid. This addresses arithmetic error for the accepted numeric inputs, not cash-flow measurement or model error. Rational arithmetic has a cost that grows with input length and precision; this teaching lab does not promise large-scale throughput.

The scan retains its actual `grid` and `npv_values`. Each grid point uses the exact represented-endpoint interpolation before final rounding, avoiding an overflowing intermediate width-times-index. A grid whose requested points collapse to duplicate floats is rejected. `exact_grid_roots` therefore identifies exact polynomial zeros at represented grid rates under this contract, while both completeness flags remain false.

`discover_roots(..., dedup_tol=1e-9)` retains the default absolute periodic-rate merging threshold and now declares it in `deduplication`. Sorted `candidate_roots` remain visible. A candidate is kept only when its exact represented-float distance from the last retained candidate is strictly greater than the threshold. This is a greedy rule, not transitive clustering or proof that merged roots are mathematically identical. `dedup_tol=0` preserves distinct represented candidates and merges only identical rates; root-refinement error can still matter.

For cash flows `(-1, 2+2**-30, -(1+2**-30))`, multiplying NPV by `(1+r)**2` gives `-r*(r-2**-30)`. The two close roots are `0` and `2**-30`. The default threshold merges them, while zero threshold preserves both when the grid resolves them. Four repeated-root grid examples distinguish hitting a repeated root from missing it without a sign change. [Runnable checks](code/check_root_scan_contract.py) also cover thirteen rational NPV references, five isolated merging boundaries, four grid references, selected range/input rejections and JSON round-trip. Run `python check_root_scan_contract.py` from `chapter-18/code`. [Original failures and rerun evidence](../validation/chapter-18-root-scan-followup.md).

A finite scan can still miss unobserved roots, nearby roots inside one cell and non-grid even-multiplicity roots. This correction does not supply certified global root isolation, arbitrary cash-flow provenance, supported-platform validation or independent mathematical review.
