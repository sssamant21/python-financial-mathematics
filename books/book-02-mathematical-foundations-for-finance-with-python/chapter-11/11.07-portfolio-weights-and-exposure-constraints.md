## Learning objectives

Convert position values into portfolio weights; distinguish net and gross exposure; interpret long, short, and leveraged positions; formulate elementary allocation constraints; and verify feasibility using label-safe Python.

## 1. From holdings to weights

For asset prices \(p_i\) in USD/share and signed holdings \(q_i\) in shares, position market values are \(v_i=p_iq_i\). Define signed net portfolio value

\[
V=\sum_{i=1}^n v_i.
\]

If \(V\ne0\), **net-value-normalized weights** are

\[
\boxed{w_i=\frac{v_i}{V}},\qquad \sum_iw_i=1.
\]

This convention is useful but can be misleading for a market-neutral portfolio whose net value is zero or near zero. For actual portfolio allocation, the appropriate denominator is often independently specified net asset value (NAV), which includes cash, liabilities, and other assets. **Do not silently substitute net risky-position value for NAV.**

## 2. Long-only example

Three positions have market values \((50000,30000,20000)\) USD, with independently verified NAV of 100000 USD. The weights are

\[
\mathbf w=(0.50,0.30,0.20).
\]

They sum to one, are nonnegative, and have gross exposure \(\sum_i|w_i|=1\).

A fully invested, long-only portfolio may require

\[
w_i\ge0,\qquad\sum_iw_i=1.
\]

The sum constraint by itself does not enforce long-only status.

## 3. Long/short exposure

Consider signed positions \((120000,-40000,20000)\) USD and NAV 100000 USD. Then

\[
\mathbf w=(1.2,-0.4,0.2),\qquad
\sum_iw_i=1,\qquad
\sum_i|w_i|=1.8.
\]

The long exposure is \(1.2+0.2=1.4\), short exposure magnitude is \(0.4\), net exposure is \(1.0\), and gross exposure is \(1.8\). Gross leverage exceeds 100% even though weights sum to one.

These quantities describe signed marked positions relative to NAV, not financing terms, margin requirements, or economic risk.

## 4. Basic constraints

Common portfolio policies can be expressed mathematically:

| Policy | Mathematical condition | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Budget | \(\sum_iw_i=1\) | Fully allocated net capital |
| Long-only | \(w_i\ge0\) | No short positions |
| Single-name cap | \(w_i\le u_i\) | Maximum long weight per asset |
| Absolute cap | \(|w_i|\le c_i\) | Limits long and short magnitude |
| Gross cap | \(\sum_i|w_i|\le G\) | Limits aggregate absolute exposure |
| Sector cap | \(\sum_{i\in S}w_i\le U_S\) | Caps signed sector allocation |

Constraints require an explicit mandate. A signed sector cap can conceal offsetting long and short positions; a gross sector cap may be preferable for some risk controls.

## 5. Feasibility versus optimization

A candidate allocation is **feasible** when it satisfies every stated constraint. This section evaluates a candidate; it does not solve for an optimal portfolio.

For example, weights \((0.5,0.3,0.2)\) satisfy long-only, budget \(=1\), individual cap \(0.6\), and gross cap \(1.0\). Weights \((0.7,0.2,0.1)\) violate the individual cap despite satisfying the budget and long-only conditions.

For floating-point weights, check equalities using explicit absolute tolerances; limits should not be silently relaxed by a large tolerance.

## 6. Cash, NAV, and denominator risks

Suppose the risky positions total 80000 USD and cash is 20000 USD. With NAV 100000 USD, the risky weights sum to \(0.8\), not \(1.0\). A budget test of risky assets alone would fail unless cash is explicitly included as a labeled component. Similarly, a zero or negative NAV requires a separate documented policy; this lab requires positive finite NAV.

Prices, holdings, NAV, and cash must share currency, valuation timestamp, and accounting conventions. A stale price can produce mathematically consistent but economically incorrect weights.

*Solution:* \((1.2,-0.4,0.2)\).

**5.** Find the net exposure in Exercise 4.  
*Solution:* 1.

**6.** Find gross exposure in Exercise 4.  
*Solution:* 1.8.

**7.** Find long exposure in Exercise 4.  
*Solution:* 1.4.

**8.** Find short exposure magnitude in Exercise 4.  
*Solution:* 0.4.

**9.** Is Exercise 4 long-only?  
*Solution:* No; its second weight is negative.

**10.** Does \((0.7,0.2,0.1)\) satisfy a 0.6 per-asset long cap?  
*Solution:* No; the first component breaches it.

**11.** Does \((0.5,0.3,0.2)\) satisfy a gross cap of 1?  
*Solution:* Yes, at the boundary.

**12.** Why is NAV preferable to net risky-position value for cash-inclusive portfolios?  
*Solution:* It preserves the full economic denominator including cash and other balance-sheet items.

**13.** What happens when risky positions total 80 and cash is 20 with NAV 100?  
*Solution:* Risky weights sum to 0.8; including cash gives total weights 1.

**14.** Does feasibility establish optimality?  
*Solution:* No; it only establishes satisfaction of the stated constraints.
