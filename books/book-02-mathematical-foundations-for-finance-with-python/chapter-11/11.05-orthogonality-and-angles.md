## Learning objectives

Compute Euclidean angles and cosine similarity; recognize orthogonal vectors; distinguish geometric orthogonality from statistical independence and risk neutrality; and perform label-safe numerical calculations with explicit unit and axis contracts.

## 1. Dot products and geometry

For vectors \(\mathbf x,\mathbf y\in\mathbb R^n\),

\[
\mathbf x^\mathsf T\mathbf y=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i.
\]

The Euclidean norm is \(\|\mathbf x\|_2=\sqrt{\mathbf x^\mathsf T\mathbf x}\). For **nonzero** vectors, the angle \(\theta\in[0,\pi]\) is defined by

\[
\boxed{\cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf x^\mathsf T\mathbf y}{\|\mathbf x\|_2\|\mathbf y\|_2}},
\qquad
\boxed{\theta=\arccos\left(\frac{\mathbf x^\mathsf T\mathbf y}{\|\mathbf x\|_2\|\mathbf y\|_2}\right)}.
\]

The ratio is called **cosine similarity**. It is dimensionless when the components of each vector have internally consistent units. Because of floating-point rounding, clamp a computed ratio to \([-1,1]\) before applying \(\arccos\), but do not use clamping to conceal nonfinite inputs or invalid metadata.

## 2. Orthogonality

Two vectors are **orthogonal** if their dot product is zero:

\[
\boxed{\mathbf x\perp\mathbf y\iff\mathbf x^\mathsf T\mathbf y=0}.
\]

For nonzero vectors, this is equivalent to a 90° angle. The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector by the dot-product definition, but the angle involving a zero vector is undefined.

Example:

\[
\mathbf x=(3,4),\qquad\mathbf y=(4,-3).
\]

Then \(\mathbf x^\mathsf T\mathbf y=12-12=0\), so the vectors are orthogonal.

## 3. Angles: worked cases

| Vectors | Dot product | Cosine similarity | Angle |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| \((1,0)\), \((1,0)\) | 1 | 1 | 0° |
| \((1,0)\), \((0,1)\) | 0 | 0 | 90° |
| \((1,0)\), \((-1,0)\) | -1 | -1 | 180° |
| \((1,1)\), \((1,0)\) | 1 | \(1/\sqrt2\) | 45° |

Positive cosine similarity indicates an acute angle; negative similarity indicates an obtuse angle. These geometric statements do not by themselves establish financial diversification or a hedge.

## 4. Financial exposure example

Suppose two portfolios have USD exposures to the same assets, in ALPHA/BETA/GAMMA order:

\[
\mathbf a=(100,0,0),\qquad\mathbf b=(0,80,0).
\]

Their Euclidean dot product is zero. Their exposure directions are orthogonal in the **chosen asset-coordinate system**.

But asset returns can be correlated. A risk model with covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) may yield \(\mathbf a^\mathsf T\Sigma\mathbf b\ne0\), even though \(\mathbf a^\mathsf T\mathbf b=0\). Thus, exposure orthogonality is not equivalent to zero return covariance, independence, or hedging effectiveness.

## 5. Geometric versus statistical orthogonality

For centered random returns \(X,Y\), zero covariance means \(\mathbb E[(X-\mathbb EX)(Y-\mathbb EY)]=0\). This resembles an inner product in a space of random variables, but it is **not** the same as the ordinary dot product of uncentered position vectors. Zero covariance does not generally imply independence; additional distributional assumptions would be needed.

The ordinary cosine between two raw return-history vectors is not necessarily their Pearson correlation. Correlation requires centering and normalization and assumes compatible sampling and missing-data treatment.

## 6. Factor sensitivity example

Consider two sensitivities over factor labels RATE/CREDIT:

\[
\mathbf s_1=(2,1),\qquad\mathbf s_2=(1,-2).
\]

Their dot product is \(2(1)+1(-2)=0\). They are Euclidean-orthogonal. If factors have different units or risk scales, this coordinate-space result may have little economic meaning until an appropriate common scaling or covariance metric is specified.

## 7. Numerical reliability

Directly multiplying very large components can overflow even when the normalized cosine is finite. The companion implementation first scales each vector by its maximum absolute component, computes a normalized dot product, and divides by normalized L2 norms. It rejects nonfinite components and zero vectors for angle calculations.

A tolerance-based orthogonality check should be **scale invariant**:

\[
|\cos\theta|\le\tau,
\]

rather than a fixed absolute dot-product threshold. Choose \(\tau\) based on numerical and application requirements, and record it in the result.

## 9. Exercises with worked solutions

**1.** Find \((1,0)\cdot(0,1)\).  
*Answer:* 0.

**2.** Find the angle between \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\).  
*Answer:* 90°.

**3.** Find the angle between \((1,0)\) and \((1,0)\).  
*Answer:* 0°.

**4.** Find the angle between \((1,0)\) and \((-1,0)\).  
*Answer:* 180°.

**5.** Find the cosine between \((1,1)\) and \((1,0)\).  
*Answer:* \(1/\sqrt2\).

**6.** Find the angle in Exercise 5.  
*Answer:* 45°.

**7.** Are \((3,4)\) and \((4,-3)\) orthogonal?  
*Answer:* Yes; \(12-12=0\).

**8.** Are \((2,1)\) and \((1,-2)\) orthogonal?  
*Answer:* Yes; \(2-2=0\).

**9.** What is the angle between a zero vector and a nonzero vector?  
*Answer:* Undefined.

**10.** Does a zero dot product imply statistical independence?  
*Answer:* No.

**11.** Does orthogonality of asset exposures imply zero portfolio-return covariance?  
*Answer:* No; covariance depends on the return covariance matrix.

**12.** Why must financial vector labels align?  
*Answer:* Otherwise the dot product pairs unrelated instruments or factors.

**13.** Why is \(|\cos\theta|\le\tau\) preferable to \(|\mathbf x^\mathsf T\mathbf y|\le\tau\) for numerical orthogonality?  
*Answer:* The cosine criterion is invariant under nonzero rescaling of the input vectors.

**14.** Why might factor sensitivities need normalization before comparing angles?  
*Answer:* Their coordinates can have different physical units and economic risk scales.
