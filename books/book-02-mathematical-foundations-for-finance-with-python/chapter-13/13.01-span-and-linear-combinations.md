## Learning objectives

Define linear combinations, spans, and subspaces; identify attainable and unattainable exposure targets; distinguish unrestricted linear span from constrained feasible portfolios; and verify claims with a label-safe Python lab.

## 1. Linear combinations

Given vectors \(v_1,\ldots,v_k\in\mathbb R^m\), a **linear combination** is any vector

\[
c_1v_1+\cdots+c_kv_k
\]

for scalar coefficients \(c_i\in\mathbb R\). The scalars may be positive, zero, or negative. For example, with \(v_1=(2,1)^\mathsf T\) and \(v_2=(1,-1)^\mathsf T\),

\[
2v_1+v_2=(5,1)^\mathsf T.
\]

The coefficients can represent signed quantities of financial instruments, but this interpretation assumes unrestricted shorting and continuous quantities. Actual portfolios may impose bounds, lot sizes, or costs.

## 2. Span

The **span** of \(v_1,\ldots,v_k\) is the set of all their linear combinations:

\[
\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\ldots,v_k\}
=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_iv_i:c_i\in\mathbb R\right\}.
\]

A span always includes the zero vector and is closed under addition and scalar multiplication; it is therefore a vector subspace. In \(\mathbb R^2\), a single nonzero vector spans a line through the origin. Two nonparallel vectors span the whole plane. A zero vector alone spans only \(\{0\}\).

## 3. Matrix interpretation

Arrange the vectors as columns:

\[
A=[v_1\ \cdots\ v_k].
\]

Then \(\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\ldots,v_k\}\) is the **column space** of \(A\). A target \(b\) lies in the span precisely when the equation \(Ax=b\) is consistent. From Section 12.5,

\[
b\in\operatorname{col}(A)
\iff \operatorname{rank}(A)=\operatorname{rank}([A\mid b]).
\]

This is an existence test, not necessarily a uniqueness test. Column-space and null-space structure will be developed further in Section 13.3.

## 4. Worked example: two independent hedge directions

Suppose the RATE/CREDIT sensitivities of two instruments are

\[
v_A=(2,1)^\mathsf T,\qquad v_B=(1,-1)^\mathsf T.
\]

The determinant of \(A=[v_A\ v_B]\) is \(-3\), so these columns span \(\mathbb R^2\). Every two-factor target has exactly one allocation using these two instruments when quantities are unrestricted.

For \(b=(5,1)^\mathsf T\), the allocation is \((2,1)\). For \(b=(0,3)^\mathsf T\), solve

\[
2q_A+q_B=0,\qquad q_A-q_B=3,
\]

which yields \(q_A=1,\ q_B=-2\). The second solution requires a short position in BOND_B.

## 5. Redundant hedge directions

Consider

\[
w_A=(1,2)^\mathsf T,\qquad w_B=(2,4)^\mathsf T.
\]

These vectors are collinear, so their span is the line

\[
\{(t,2t)^\mathsf T:t\in\mathbb R\}.
\]

The target \((3,6)\) is attainable, with infinitely many allocations satisfying \(q_A+2q_B=3\). The target \((3,7)\) is **not** in the span: no unrestricted linear combination of these instruments matches both factors.

Adding more instruments does not necessarily expand the span; a new instrument must contribute an independent exposure direction.

## 6. Linear span versus financially feasible sets

Linear span assumes arbitrary real coefficients. If positions must be nonnegative, attainable exposures form a **cone** rather than generally a subspace. If position weights must be nonnegative and sum to one, attainable exposures lie in the **convex hull** of instrument vectors.

For example, \(b=(0,3)\) lies in the span of \(v_A,v_B\), but its allocation \((1,-2)\) violates a long-only policy. Thus:

\[
\text{mathematically attainable}\not\Rightarrow\text{financially feasible}.
\]

Exposure units, market data, trading limits, and risk-model definitions must be validated before using any solution operationally.

## 7. Numerical verification

A computed allocation \(\hat x\) can be checked by its residual \(r=b-A\hat x\). A small residual supports approximate equation satisfaction but does not prove exact span membership when inputs are rounded or the system is ill-conditioned. Numerical rank and span tests depend on tolerance; for production use, rely on stable, rank-revealing factorizations.

The companion lab uses explicit, labeled two-factor examples and a transparent \(2\times2\) determinant-based classifier. It is a teaching implementation, not a general-purpose SVD rank test.

## 9. Worked exercises

**1.** Define a linear combination.  
*Answer:* A sum of scalar multiples of vectors.

**2.** Is \(0\) in every span?  
*Answer:* Yes, by taking every coefficient zero.

**3.** What does a single nonzero vector span in \(\mathbb R^2\)?  
*Answer:* A line through the origin.

**4.** What does the zero vector alone span?  
*Answer:* \(\{0\}\).

**5.** Calculate \(2(2,1)+(1,-1)\).  
*Answer:* \((5,1)\).

**6.** Find the determinant of \(\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\).  
*Answer:* \(-3\).

**7.** Do these two columns span \(\mathbb R^2\)?  
*Answer:* Yes; the determinant is nonzero.

**8.** Express \((5,1)\) in these columns.  
*Answer:* \(2v_A+v_B\).

**9.** Express \((0,3)\) in these columns.  
*Answer:* \(v_A-2v_B\).

**10.** Is the latter allocation long-only?  
*Answer:* No; the second coefficient is negative.

**11.** What is the span of \((1,2)\) and \((2,4)\)?  
*Answer:* The line \(\{(t,2t):t\in\mathbb R\}\).

**12.** Is \((3,6)\) in this span?  
*Answer:* Yes, for example \(3(1,2)+0(2,4)\).

**13.** Is \((3,7)\) in this span?  
*Answer:* No.

**14.** How many allocations match \((3,6)\) using these redundant vectors?  
*Answer:* Infinitely many.

**15.** State the rank test for span membership.  
*Answer:* \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=\operatorname{rank}([A\mid b])\).

**16.** Does adding a duplicate instrument expand span?  
*Answer:* No.

**17.** Is span always a vector subspace?  
*Answer:* Yes.

**18.** What is the long-only attainable set of nonnegative combinations called?  
*Answer:* A convex cone.

**19.** What is the set of nonnegative combinations whose weights sum to one?  
*Answer:* The convex hull.

**20.** Why is a small residual not enough for business approval?  
*Answer:* It does not guarantee numerical conditioning, feasible positions, or valid economic assumptions.

## 10. Applied assessment

Evaluate two labeled hedge universes: independent RATE/CREDIT directions and redundant proportional directions. Demonstrate a target in the span, a target outside the span, and a target requiring a short position. Verify factor-label permutation invariance. Reject nonfinite inputs, duplicate labels, missing factor labels, and inconsistent dimensions.

**Acceptance:** independent example yields \((2,1)\); redundant example recognizes \((3,6)\) as attainable and \((3,7)\) as unattainable; long-only feasibility rejects \((0,3)\) for the independent example.
