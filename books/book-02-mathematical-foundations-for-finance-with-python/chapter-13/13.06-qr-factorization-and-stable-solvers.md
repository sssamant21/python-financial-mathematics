## Learning objectives

Derive reduced QR factorization for a tall, full-column-rank matrix; solve least-squares problems using orthogonal transformations and back substitution; explain why normal equations are numerically fragile; recognize rank-deficient and poorly scaled systems; and apply a labeled exposure-matching workflow.

## 1. Why solver choice matters

For an exposure matrix \(A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\) and target \(b\in\mathbb R^m\), ordinary least squares minimizes

\[
J(x)=\frac12\|Ax-b\|_2^2.
\]

Section 13.5 derived the normal equations \(A^\mathsf TAx=A^\mathsf Tb\). Although mathematically correct, forming \(A^\mathsf TA\) can approximately square the 2-norm condition number:

\[
\kappa_2(A^\mathsf TA)=\kappa_2(A)^2
\]

for full-column-rank \(A\). Thus a matrix with condition number \(10^6\) produces normal equations with condition number \(10^{12}\), potentially losing significant accuracy in floating-point arithmetic.

## 2. Reduced QR factorization

For a real \(m\times n\) matrix with \(m\ge n\) and linearly independent columns, reduced QR writes

\[
\boxed{A=QR}
\]

where \(Q\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\) has orthonormal columns (\(Q^\mathsf TQ=I_n\)) and \(R\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}\) is upper triangular with nonzero diagonal entries.

An orthogonal change of coordinates preserves Euclidean length. Decompose the target into the column-space projection and an orthogonal residual:

\[
b=QQ^\mathsf Tb+(I-QQ^\mathsf T)b.
\]

Then

\[
\|Ax-b\|_2^2
=\|Rx-Q^\mathsf Tb\|_2^2+\|(I-QQ^\mathsf T)b\|_2^2.
\]

The minimizing coordinates satisfy

\[
\boxed{Rx=Q^\mathsf Tb}.
\]

This triangular system can be solved by back substitution without explicitly constructing \(A^\mathsf TA\).

## 3. Gram–Schmidt, Householder, and Givens

Classical Gram–Schmidt subtracts projections from each new column, but it can lose orthogonality when columns are nearly dependent. Modified Gram–Schmidt improves numerical behavior and is useful for understanding QR, though it is not the preferred production default for difficult problems.

Householder QR uses orthogonal reflections and is the standard stable approach for dense, full-rank least-squares problems. Givens rotations are useful for sparse and incremental structures. Column-pivoted QR or SVD helps diagnose numerical rank deficiency.

**Implementation choice:** the companion lab uses **Householder reflections**, not classical Gram–Schmidt. It is a small educational standard-library implementation, not a replacement for a mature numerical library.

## 4. Worked financial example: three factors and two hedges

Let rows be RATE, CREDIT, and LIQUIDITY and columns be BOND_A and BOND_B:

\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}
1&0\\
0&1\\
1&1
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
b=\begin{bmatrix}2\\3\\6\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The first instrument affects RATE and LIQUIDITY; the second affects CREDIT and LIQUIDITY. The target cannot be matched exactly: any attainable vector satisfies LIQUIDITY = RATE + CREDIT, but \(6\ne2+3\).

The normal equations are

\[
\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}x
=\begin{bmatrix}8\\9\end{bmatrix},
\]

which yield

\[
\boxed{x^*=(7/3,10/3)^\mathsf T}.
\]

The achieved exposure is \((7/3,10/3,17/3)\), and the signed residual \(r=b-Ax^*\) is

\[
r=(-1/3,-1/3,1/3)^\mathsf T.
\]

Check \(A^\mathsf Tr=(0,0)\). The squared residual norm is \(1/3\), so \(J(x^*)=1/6\).

A Householder QR solver obtains this solution by orthogonal transformations and a triangular solve, without forming the normal equations.

## 5. Derivation of a Householder reflection

For a vector \(x\), choose a sign to avoid cancellation and construct

\[
v=x+\operatorname{sign}(x_1)\|x\|_2e_1,\qquad
H=I-2\frac{vv^\mathsf T}{v^\mathsf Tv}.
\]

Then \(H\) is symmetric and orthogonal (\(H^\mathsf TH=I\)), and it maps \(x\) to a vector whose entries below the first are zero. Apply successive reflections to eliminate entries below each diagonal in \(A\), applying the same reflections to \(b\). The resulting upper-triangular leading block is \(R\); the transformed leading target entries equal \(Q^\mathsf Tb\).

## 6. Solver validation and rank guardrails

A reliable least-squares report should include:

- Matrix and target dimensions, factor labels, instrument labels, and units.
- Rank assumptions and numerical rank thresholds.
- Computed positions, achieved exposures, signed residuals, and objective.
- Orthogonality check \(A^\mathsf Tr\), scaled by input magnitudes.
- A distinction between **mathematical optimality** and **business-policy acceptance**.

If a pivot is too small relative to the matrix scale, the educational solver **rejects** the system as numerically rank deficient instead of silently returning an unstable allocation. Production workflows should use rank-revealing QR/SVD and explicit conditioning diagnostics.

## 7. Worked exercises

**1.** State reduced QR.  
*Answer:* \(A=QR\), with \(Q^\mathsf TQ=I\) and \(R\) upper triangular.

**2.** What is the shape of reduced \(Q\) for a \(5\times2\) matrix?  
*Answer:* \(5\times2\).

**3.** What is the shape of \(R\)?  
*Answer:* \(2\times2\).

**4.** Why does QR preserve residual norms?  
*Answer:* Orthogonal transformations preserve Euclidean length.

**5.** Which system determines the full-rank least-squares coordinates?  
*Answer:* \(Rx=Q^\mathsf Tb\).

**6.** Why avoid explicitly inverting \(A^\mathsf TA\)?  
*Answer:* Forming normal equations can square the condition number.

**7.** What does \(A^\mathsf Tr=0\) signify?  
*Answer:* The residual is orthogonal to the column space.

**8.** Name a robust dense QR algorithm.  
*Answer:* Householder reflections.

**9.** Name a rank-revealing alternative.  
*Answer:* Singular value decomposition.

**10.** What does column pivoting help detect?  
*Answer:* Numerical rank deficiency.

**11.** For the example, compute \(A^\mathsf TA\).  
*Answer:* \(\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\).

**12.** Compute \(A^\mathsf Tb\).  
*Answer:* \((8,9)^\mathsf T\).

**13.** Find the optimal BOND_A position.  
*Answer:* \(7/3\).

**14.** Find the optimal BOND_B position.  
*Answer:* \(10/3\).

**15.** Find the achieved LIQUIDITY exposure.  
*Answer:* \(17/3\).

**16.** Find the residual vector.  
*Answer:* \((-1/3,-1/3,1/3)\).

**17.** Find the minimum objective \(J\).  
*Answer:* \(1/6\).

**18.** Does a small residual guarantee a stable coefficient vector?  
*Answer:* No.

**19.** Does a QR solution automatically satisfy position limits?  
*Answer:* No.

**20.** What is the next topic after stable solvers?  
*Answer:* Weighted least squares and factor units.

## 9. Operational interpretation

The objective uses **unweighted** Euclidean exposure distance. Different factor units can distort priorities; a small objective is not a risk approval. The solver does not enforce no-short, quantity, concentration, or liquidity constraints. Such controls belong in an explicit portfolio feasibility layer.
