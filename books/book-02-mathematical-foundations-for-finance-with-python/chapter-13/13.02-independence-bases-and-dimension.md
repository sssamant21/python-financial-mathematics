## Learning objectives

Test linear independence, identify a basis, calculate subspace dimension, distinguish the number of instruments from the number of independent exposure directions, and interpret redundancy in financial hedging systems.

## 1. Linear independence

Vectors \(v_1,\ldots,v_k\) are **linearly independent** if

\[
c_1v_1+\cdots+c_kv_k=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
c_1=\cdots=c_k=0.
\]

If a nonzero coefficient vector satisfies the homogeneous equation, the vectors are **dependent**. Equivalently, at least one vector is a linear combination of the others.

For example,

\[
v_1=(1,2)^\mathsf T,\quad v_2=(2,4)^\mathsf T
\]

are dependent because \(2v_1-v_2=0\). They do not provide two distinct risk directions.

## 2. Matrix rank and independence

Put vectors into columns of \(A=[v_1\ \cdots\ v_k]\). The columns are independent exactly when

\[
\operatorname{rank}(A)=k.
\]

A set of more than \(m\) vectors in \(\mathbb R^m\) is necessarily dependent. A square \(n\times n\) matrix has independent columns exactly when its determinant is nonzero.

Rank depends on the underlying mathematical matrix. In floating-point arithmetic, the *numerically estimated* rank depends on tolerances and scaling.

## 3. Basis and dimension

A **basis** for a vector space \(V\) is a set of vectors that both spans \(V\) and is linearly independent. Every vector in \(V\) has a unique representation in that basis.

The **dimension** of \(V\) is the number of vectors in any basis for \(V\). The dimension of the column space of a matrix equals its rank.

Examples:

- \(\mathbb R^2\) has dimension 2; the standard basis is \(e_1=(1,0)\), \(e_2=(0,1)\).
- A line through the origin generated by a nonzero vector has dimension 1.
- The zero subspace has dimension 0; its basis is the empty set.
- Three instruments can span only a two-dimensional exposure space if their sensitivities are constrained to two factors.

## 4. Financial example: independent hedge instruments

Use factor sensitivities

\[
v_A=(2,1)^\mathsf T,\qquad v_B=(1,-1)^\mathsf T.
\]

Then

\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \det(A)=-3.
\]

The columns are independent and form a basis of \(\mathbb R^2\). For target \(b=(5,1)^\mathsf T\), the unique coordinates in this basis are \((2,1)\). These coordinates correspond to signed quantities of BOND_A and BOND_B.

## 5. Financial example: redundant hedge instruments

Add an instrument \(v_C=2v_A=(4,2)^\mathsf T\). Then

\[
H=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&4\\1&-1&2\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Although there are three instruments, the rank is 2, so the exposure space has dimension 2. The set \(\{v_A,v_B\}\) is a basis. The third instrument does not expand the span.

The target \(b=(5,1)\) can be represented by infinitely many three-instrument portfolios:

\[
(q_A,q_B,q_C)=(2-2t,1,t),\qquad t\in\mathbb R.
\]

All such portfolios have the same modeled factor exposures, but potentially different liquidity, funding, transaction costs, and concentration. **A basis identifies independent risk directions; it does not select the economically best portfolio.**

## 6. Pivot columns and basis extraction

Gaussian elimination identifies pivot columns, which indicate a subset of original columns forming a basis for the column space. The basis vectors must be taken from the **original** matrix, not from its row-reduced columns.

For the example \(H\), the first two columns are pivots, so \(v_A,v_B\) form a basis. Reordering instrument columns can change which valid basis is selected, but not the dimension of the span.

## 7. Numerical independence and scaling

Exact independence is an algebraic property. Nearly proportional columns can be mathematically independent yet numerically difficult to distinguish.

Consider \(u=(1,1)\) and \(v=(1,1+10^{-10})\). Their determinant is \(10^{-10}\), not zero, but the system is highly sensitive. A floating-point rank classifier should report its tolerance and avoid treating a heuristic numerical decision as an exact mathematical proof.

In production, use rank-revealing QR or SVD and well-defined unit normalization. Section 13.6 develops QR-based methods.

## 8. Basis choice versus constrained feasibility

Replacing redundant instruments with a basis preserves the **unrestricted linear span** but may not preserve the **feasible trading set** under nonnegative positions, position caps, transaction costs, or instrument-specific restrictions.

For example, an allocation using \(v_C=2v_A\) may be permitted when an equivalent allocation using \(v_A\) violates a quantity cap. A basis is a mathematical simplification, not an automatic trading-universe reduction.

## 10. Worked exercises

**1.** Define linear independence.  
*Answer:* The zero linear combination has only the all-zero coefficient solution.

**2.** Are \((1,2)\) and \((2,4)\) independent?  
*Answer:* No; the second is twice the first.

**3.** Are \((2,1)\) and \((1,-1)\) independent?  
*Answer:* Yes; their determinant is \(-3\).

**4.** What is a basis?  
*Answer:* A linearly independent spanning set.

**5.** What is the dimension of \(\mathbb R^2\)?  
*Answer:* 2.

**6.** What is the dimension of the zero subspace?  
*Answer:* 0.

**7.** What is the dimension of the span of a single nonzero vector?  
*Answer:* 1.

**8.** Can three vectors in \(\mathbb R^2\) be independent?  
*Answer:* No.

**9.** What is the rank of \(H=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&4\\1&-1&2\end{bmatrix}\)?  
*Answer:* 2.

**10.** Give a basis for its column space.  
*Answer:* The first two columns, \((2,1)\) and \((1,-1)\).

**11.** Is its third column redundant?  
*Answer:* Yes; \(v_C=2v_A\).

**12.** How many independent risk directions do the three instruments provide?  
*Answer:* Two.

**13.** Express \((5,1)\) using \(v_A,v_B\).  
*Answer:* \(2v_A+v_B\).

**14.** Give another representation using all three instruments.  
*Answer:* \(0v_A+v_B+v_C\).

**15.** Give the general family of allocations.  
*Answer:* \((2-2t,1,t)\).

**16.** Why are coordinates unique in a basis?  
*Answer:* Two distinct coordinate representations would yield a nontrivial dependence relation.

**17.** What does the rank of a matrix equal geometrically?  
*Answer:* The dimension of its column space.

**18.** Does instrument count necessarily equal risk-space dimension?  
*Answer:* No; instruments may be redundant.

**19.** Can nearly proportional columns be independent in exact arithmetic?  
*Answer:* Yes.

**20.** Why can dropping a redundant instrument change feasible trading outcomes?  
*Answer:* Instrument-specific limits and costs can make economically feasible sets differ despite equal spans.

## 11. Applied assessment

Given two independent hedge columns and a third proportional column, identify a basis and dimension, prove redundancy, calculate two distinct allocations for the same target, and explain why those allocations need not be economically equivalent. Repeat with reordered instrument columns; dimension must remain invariant even if the chosen pivot basis changes.

**Acceptance:** rank 2, dimension 2, two basis instruments, third redundant, and valid alternative allocations with zero modeled residual.
