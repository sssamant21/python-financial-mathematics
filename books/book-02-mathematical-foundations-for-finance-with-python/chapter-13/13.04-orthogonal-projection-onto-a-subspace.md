## 1. Orthogonality and geometry

Two vectors \(u,v\in\mathbb R^m\) are orthogonal when their Euclidean inner product vanishes:

\[
u^\mathsf Tv=\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_iv_i=0.
\]

For nonzero \(u\), the orthogonal projection of a target \(b\) onto the line \(\operatorname{span}\{u\}\) is

\[
\boxed{p=\frac{u^\mathsf Tb}{u^\mathsf Tu}u}.
\]

The residual \(r=b-p\) satisfies \(u^\mathsf Tr=0\). The target decomposes into an attainable component \(p\) and an orthogonal unattainable component \(r\).

## 2. Why projection is the closest point

For any scalar \(t\),

\[
\|b-tu\|_2^2=\|r\|_2^2+\|p-tu\|_2^2.
\]

Because the second term is nonnegative, \(p\) uniquely minimizes Euclidean distance from \(b\) to the line. The uniqueness concerns the **projected exposure vector**; when multiple instruments have redundant columns, allocation coordinates need not be unique.

## 3. Financial worked example

A single hypothetical instrument has standardized RATE/CREDIT exposure \(u=(1,2)^\mathsf T\). The desk's target is \(b=(3,7)^\mathsf T\). The available instrument spans only the line \(\{(t,2t):t\in\mathbb R\}\); therefore the target is not exactly attainable.

\[
u^\mathsf Tb=17,\qquad u^\mathsf Tu=5,\qquad t^*=\frac{17}{5}=3.4.
\]

Hence

\[
p=(3.4,6.8)^\mathsf T,\qquad r=(-0.4,0.2)^\mathsf T.
\]

The residual is orthogonal to the instrument direction because \(-0.4+2(0.2)=0\). The Euclidean distance is

\[
\|r\|_2=\sqrt{0.2}\approx0.4472136.
\]

The residual indicates a RATE overexposure of 0.4 and CREDIT underexposure of 0.2 relative to the target (under the signed convention \(r=b-p\)). It does **not** imply that these factor deviations have equal monetary importance.

## 4. Projection onto a subspace

If \(A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\) has linearly independent columns, the orthogonal projection of \(b\) onto \(\operatorname{col}(A)\) is

\[
p=A(A^\mathsf TA)^{-1}A^\mathsf Tb.
\]

The corresponding coordinates solve the **normal equations**

\[
A^\mathsf TAx=A^\mathsf Tb.
\]

The residual satisfies \(A^\mathsf T(b-Ax)=0\): it is orthogonal to every column of \(A\).

This formula is a useful derivation, **not** a recommendation to explicitly invert \(A^\mathsf TA\) in production. Forming normal equations can square the condition number; stable QR or SVD solvers are preferred for numerical least squares. Section 13.6 develops QR methods.

For any subspace, the projected vector \(p\) is unique. If \(A\) is rank deficient, the coordinates \(x\) may be nonunique; the projection is still well-defined.

## 5. Projection onto a two-direction plane

For

\[
v_A=(2,1)^\mathsf T,\quad v_B=(1,-1)^\mathsf T,
\]

the two columns are independent and span all of \(\mathbb R^2\). Every two-factor target lies in this span, so its projection is the target itself and the residual is zero. This is why projection becomes especially useful for undercomplete or constrained exposure universes.

## 6. Projection versus policy acceptance

The nearest attainable vector under Euclidean distance may still breach factor-specific risk limits, liquidity restrictions, or gross-position limits. An orthogonal residual is a **mathematical optimality condition** for an unconstrained Euclidean objective, not a business approval.

In practice, factor sensitivities may have different units or economic scales. A weighted inner product or a transformed factor space may be more appropriate; Section 13.7 develops weighted least squares and units.

## 7. Numerical and governance cautions

- Validate labels and units before any dot product.
- Reject zero projection directions, nonfinite inputs, and invalid shapes.
- Record the residual sign convention \(r=b-p\).
- Use a scale-aware tolerance to verify orthogonality.
- Do not interpret a tiny residual as proof of a well-conditioned allocation.
- Use tested numerical libraries for production-scale models.

## 9. Twenty worked exercises

**1.** When are two vectors orthogonal?  
*Answer:* Their dot product is zero.

**2.** Evaluate \((1,2)^\mathsf T(3,7)\).  
*Answer:* 17.

**3.** Evaluate \((1,2)^\mathsf T(1,2)\).  
*Answer:* 5.

**4.** What scalar projects \((3,7)\) onto \((1,2)\)?  
*Answer:* \(17/5=3.4\).

**5.** Find the projection vector.  
*Answer:* \((3.4,6.8)\).

**6.** Find the signed residual \(b-p\).  
*Answer:* \((-0.4,0.2)\).

**7.** Verify residual orthogonality.  
*Answer:* \((1,2)\cdot(-0.4,0.2)=0\).

**8.** Find the squared residual distance.  
*Answer:* \(0.16+0.04=0.2\).

**9.** Find the residual distance.  
*Answer:* \(\sqrt{0.2}\).

**10.** Is the target exactly attainable using only \((1,2)\)?  
*Answer:* No.

**11.** Project \((4,8)\) onto \(\operatorname{span}\{(1,2)\}\).  
*Answer:* \((4,8)\), with coefficient 4.

**12.** Project \((1,-0.5)\) onto \(\operatorname{span}\{(1,2)\}\).  
*Answer:* Zero, because the dot product is zero.

**13.** What happens if the projection direction is zero?  
*Answer:* The line formula divides by zero; reject the input or handle the zero subspace separately.

**14.** State the normal equations.  
*Answer:* \(A^\mathsf TAx=A^\mathsf Tb\).

**15.** State the residual orthogonality condition.  
*Answer:* \(A^\mathsf Tr=0\).

**16.** Is the projected vector unique?  
*Answer:* Yes, for a fixed subspace and Euclidean inner product.

**17.** Are instrument coordinates always unique?  
*Answer:* No, not with dependent columns.

**18.** Why avoid explicit normal-equation inversion in production?  
*Answer:* It can worsen numerical conditioning.

**19.** Does Euclidean proximity imply financial acceptability?  
*Answer:* No; units, risk tolerances, and constraints matter.

**20.** What does a zero projection residual mean?  
*Answer:* The target belongs to the projected subspace.

## 10. Applied acceptance

Given \(u=(1,2)\), \(b=(3,7)\), and factor tolerances RATE \(0.5\), CREDIT \(0.1\), the projection has residuals \((-0.4,0.2)\). RATE passes but CREDIT fails, so the policy result is REJECT even though the projection is mathematically optimal.
