## Learning objectives

Formulate an ordinary least-squares objective, derive its gradient and normal equations, interpret its geometric projection, distinguish residual optimality from risk-policy acceptance, and calculate a reproducible financial example.

## 1. From exact matching to approximate matching

For an exposure matrix \(A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\), instrument positions \(x\in\mathbb R^n\), and target exposures \(b\in\mathbb R^m\), exact matching requires \(Ax=b\). If \(b\notin\operatorname{col}(A)\), no exact solution exists.

Instead, define the residual

\[
r(x)=b-Ax
\]

and the ordinary least-squares objective

\[
\boxed{J(x)=\tfrac12\|b-Ax\|_2^2
=\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^{m}(b_i-(Ax)_i)^2}.
\]

The factor \(1/2\) simplifies differentiation and does not affect the minimizer. Squaring penalizes large deviations more heavily than small ones, and makes the objective smooth and nonnegative.

## 2. Derive the gradient

Expanding the quadratic gives

\[
J(x)=\tfrac12 b^\mathsf Tb-x^\mathsf TA^\mathsf Tb
+\tfrac12 x^\mathsf TA^\mathsf TAx.
\]

Its gradient and Hessian are

\[
\nabla J(x)=A^\mathsf T(Ax-b),\qquad
\nabla^2J(x)=A^\mathsf TA.
\]

Because \(z^\mathsf TA^\mathsf TAz=\|Az\|_2^2\ge0\), the Hessian is positive semidefinite. Therefore \(J\) is convex, and every stationary point is a global minimizer.

## 3. Normal equations

At a minimum,

\[
\nabla J(x)=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\boxed{A^\mathsf TAx=A^\mathsf Tb}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
A^\mathsf Tr=0.
\]

Thus the residual is orthogonal to every instrument exposure direction. The achieved exposure \(Ax\) is the orthogonal projection of \(b\) onto \(\operatorname{col}(A)\).

**Numerical warning:** These equations are for mathematical derivation. Forming \(A^\mathsf TA\) can square the condition number; use QR or SVD in production.

## 4. Worked financial example: one hedge, two factors

Suppose the only available hedge has standardized sensitivities

\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix},\qquad
b=\begin{bmatrix}3\\7\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The scalar position \(q\) minimizes

\[
J(q)=\tfrac12[(3-q)^2+(7-2q)^2].
\]

Differentiate:

\[
J'(q)=-(3-q)-2(7-2q)=5q-17.
\]

The optimum is \(q^*=17/5=3.4\). The achieved exposure and signed residual are

\[
Aq^*=(3.4,6.8)^\mathsf T,\qquad
r=(-0.4,0.2)^\mathsf T.
\]

The squared residual norm is \(0.2\), so

\[
\boxed{J(q^*)=0.1}.
\]

For comparison, \(q=3\) gives \(r=(0,1)\) and \(J(3)=0.5\). The optimum improves the total squared mismatch but sacrifices perfect RATE matching to reduce CREDIT mismatch.

## 5. Existence and uniqueness

A least-squares minimizer always exists in finite-dimensional Euclidean space. If the columns of \(A\) are linearly independent, \(A^\mathsf TA\) is positive definite and the minimizer is unique.

If \(A\) is rank deficient, there can be infinitely many minimizers \(x^*+z\) with \(z\in\ker(A)\), but **all minimizers have the same achieved exposure** \(Ax^*\) and the same minimum objective value.

For example, if two instruments have identical factor vectors, the objective may determine their total quantity but not its division between instruments.

## 6. Residual and objective are different quantities

The residual vector \(r\) retains factor-level sign and units. The squared norm \(\|r\|_2^2\) aggregates deviations after squaring, and \(J=\tfrac12\|r\|_2^2\) is a scalar objective.

If RATE and CREDIT sensitivities use incompatible units or scales, unweighted least squares may be economically inappropriate. Weighted least squares and unit normalization are covered in Section 13.7.

## 7. Optimization is not risk acceptance

For the reference example, suppose RATE tolerance is \(0.5\) and CREDIT tolerance is \(0.1\). The optimal residual \((-0.4,0.2)\) passes RATE but fails CREDIT. The **mathematically optimal** solution must therefore be **rejected** by this illustrative factor-limit policy.

Constraints such as long-only holdings, gross notional, funding, transaction costs, or liquidity may require constrained optimization rather than ordinary least squares.

## 10. Twenty worked exercises

**1.** Define ordinary least squares.  
*Answer:* Minimize the sum of squared residuals.

**2.** Write the objective with a convenient factor of one half.  
*Answer:* \(J(x)=\frac12\|b-Ax\|_2^2\).

**3.** What is the residual sign convention?  
*Answer:* \(r=b-Ax\).

**4.** Find the gradient of \(J\).  
*Answer:* \(A^\mathsf T(Ax-b)\).

**5.** Find its Hessian.  
*Answer:* \(A^\mathsf TA\).

**6.** Why is \(J\) convex?  
*Answer:* Its Hessian is positive semidefinite.

**7.** State the normal equations.  
*Answer:* \(A^\mathsf TAx=A^\mathsf Tb\).

**8.** State the residual orthogonality condition.  
*Answer:* \(A^\mathsf Tr=0\).

**9.** For \(A=(1,2)^\mathsf T\) and \(b=(3,7)^\mathsf T\), compute \(A^\mathsf TA\).  
*Answer:* 5.

**10.** Compute \(A^\mathsf Tb\).  
*Answer:* 17.

**11.** Find the optimal scalar position.  
*Answer:* \(17/5=3.4\).

**12.** Calculate the achieved exposures.  
*Answer:* \((3.4,6.8)\).

**13.** Calculate the signed residual.  
*Answer:* \((-0.4,0.2)\).

**14.** Calculate the minimum objective.  
*Answer:* \(0.1\).

**15.** Calculate \(J(3)\).  
*Answer:* \(0.5\).

**16.** Does the optimal solution perfectly match RATE?  
*Answer:* No; it overexposes RATE by 0.4.

**17.** What ensures uniqueness of the coefficient vector?  
*Answer:* Full column rank.

**18.** Can rank-deficient models have multiple minimizers?  
*Answer:* Yes, differing by null-space vectors.

**19.** Do multiple minimizers have different achieved exposures?  
*Answer:* No; their projected exposure vector is unique.

**20.** Does the minimum objective imply financial policy acceptance?  
*Answer:* No; individual factor limits and trading constraints may still fail.
