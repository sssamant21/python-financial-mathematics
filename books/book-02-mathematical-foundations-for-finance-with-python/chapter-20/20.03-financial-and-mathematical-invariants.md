## Learning objectives

## 1. What is an invariant?

An **invariant** is a property that remains true under a specified operation, transformation, or evolution. In testing, the term is also used more broadly for a mathematical identity or constraint that must hold for every admissible input. An invariant-based check verifies a relationship rather than just one numerical answer.

For example, the present value operator for a fixed continuously compounded rate \(r\) and time \(T\),

\[
P(F;r,T)=Fe^{-rT},
\]

is linear in \(F\). Thus

\[
P(F_1+F_2;r,T)=P(F_1;r,T)+P(F_2;r,T).
\]

This holds for finite signed cash flows when the same rate and date are used. It does **not** assert additivity across different discount dates without individually discounting each cash flow.

## 2. Discount factor identities

Define \(D(r,T)=e^{-rT}\). Then

\[
D(r,0)=1,\quad D(0,T)=1,\quad
D(r,t+s)=D(r,t)D(r,s).
\]

The final identity is the semigroup property for a **constant** continuously compounded rate. It does not hold as written for a time-varying rate if one naively substitutes the same start time into both factors. For a deterministic rate curve \(r(\tau)\), define

\[
D(t_0,t_1)=\exp\left(-\int_{t_0}^{t_1}r(\tau)\,d\tau\right),
\]

and use \(D(t_0,t_2)=D(t_0,t_1)D(t_1,t_2)\).

For \(F>0,T>0\), present value decreases as \(r\) increases. For \(F<0\), the inequality reverses.

## 3. Cash-flow and portfolio additivity

For cash flows \(C_i\) at dates \(t_i\), with a common deterministic discount convention,

\[
PV(\{C_i,t_i\})=\sum_i C_iD(0,t_i).
\]

Splitting one cash flow \(C\) into \(C_a+C_b=C\) at the same date does not change PV. Combining portfolios adds their discounted values, assuming linear valuation and no portfolio-dependent transaction costs, market impact, netting constraints, or nonlinear taxes.

**Example:** A payment of 100 at year 2 has the same PV as payments of 40 and 60 at year 2, but not generally as payments of 40 at year 1 and 60 at year 3.

## 4. Accumulation and discounting as inverses

For the same constant rate and horizon,

\[
FV=PV\,e^{rT},\qquad PV=FV\,e^{-rT}.
\]

Hence

\[
PV(FV(P,r,T),r,T)=P.
\]

The identity is mathematical; in floating-point arithmetic, test within a tolerance. Large exponents may overflow or underflow, making a numerically safe test domain necessary.

## 5. Conservation in cash-flow accounts

For a zero-interest account \(A'=u(t)\),

\[
A(T)-A(0)=\int_0^T u(t)\,dt+\sum_{t_j\le T}J_j.
\]

For nonzero rates, this simple cash-flow conservation law no longer applies to the raw balance because interest contributes to the change. Instead,

\[
A(T)-A(0)=\int_0^T [r(t)A(t)+u(t)]\,dt+\sum J_j.
\]

The integrated identity is a useful reconciliation check, but numerical quadrature and solver errors must be considered.

## 6. Continuous-time composition

For constant \(r,u\), define the exact transition

\[
\Phi_h(A)=Ae^{rh}+u\frac{\operatorname{expm1}(rh)}{r}
\]

for \(r\ne0\), with \(\Phi_h(A)=A+uh\) for \(r=0\).

Then

\[
\boxed{\Phi_{h_2}(\Phi_{h_1}(A))=\Phi_{h_1+h_2}(A)}.
\]

This composition property is a strong check for an account-transition implementation. It assumes the coefficients are unchanged across both subintervals and there is no intervening jump or boundary intervention.

## 7. Matrix invariants

For a symmetric covariance matrix \(\Sigma\),

\[
\Sigma=\Sigma^\mathsf{T},\qquad
x^\mathsf{T}\Sigma x\ge0
\]

for all real vectors \(x\). The second condition is positive semidefiniteness, a mathematical requirement for a valid covariance matrix.

For an orthogonal matrix \(Q\),

\[
Q^\mathsf{T}Q=I,\qquad \|Qx\|_2=\|x\|_2.
\]

These properties are useful independent checks, but a handful of sampled vectors cannot prove a matrix is positive semidefinite. Use appropriate factorization or eigenvalue analysis for a general proof or robust numerical assessment.

## 8. Optimization invariants

A proposed solution to a constrained optimization problem must satisfy its constraints regardless of the solver's success flag. For portfolio weights \(w_i\) under full-investment and long-only constraints,

\[
\sum_i w_i=1,\qquad w_i\ge0.
\]

These are **feasibility** conditions, not proof of optimality. Optimality may additionally require objective comparison, KKT conditions under their assumptions, dual certificates, or independent solution methods.

## 9. Property tests versus known-value tests

A known-value test verifies one derived result. An invariant check verifies a relationship across many inputs. Both are valuable. Two incorrect functions may satisfy an inverse relationship if their errors cancel, and an incorrect valuation implementation can still preserve additivity.

Use independent known values, invariants, boundary checks, and domain review together.

## 10. Numerical tolerances and evidence

For floating-point comparisons use a documented rule such as

\[
|a-b|\le \mathrm{atol}+\mathrm{rtol}|b|.
\]

For vector and matrix identities, specify the norm, scale, and dimensionality. For constraints such as \(w_i\ge0\), define an absolute feasibility tolerance and distinguish small numerical violations from economically unacceptable allocations.

## 11. Twenty worked exercises with solutions

**1.** Define an invariant-based check.  
*Solution:* It tests a relationship that must hold for admissible inputs.

**2.** State present-value additivity.  
*Solution:* \(P(F_1+F_2)=P(F_1)+P(F_2)\) for the same \(r,T\).

**3.** State zero-horizon discounting.  
*Solution:* \(D(r,0)=1\).

**4.** State zero-rate discounting.  
*Solution:* \(D(0,T)=1\).

**5.** State constant-rate discount composition.  
*Solution:* \(D(r,t+s)=D(r,t)D(r,s)\).

**6.** Does the same simple composition apply to an arbitrary time-varying rate?  
*Solution:* No; use interval-specific discount factors.

**7.** What is the PV of a cash flow split into two same-date components?  
*Solution:* The sum of the components' PVs equals the original PV.

**8.** Is splitting a cash flow across different dates generally neutral?  
*Solution:* No.

**9.** State the FV/PV inverse relation.  
*Solution:* \(PV(FV(P,r,T),r,T)=P\).

**10.** What is the zero-interest account conservation equation?  
*Solution:* \(A(T)-A(0)=\int u(t)\,dt+\sum J_j\).

**11.** Why does that equation change when interest is present?  
*Solution:* Interest contributes \(\int r(t)A(t)\,dt\).

**12.** State the exact-transition composition identity.  
*Solution:* \(\Phi_{h_2}(\Phi_{h_1}(A))=\Phi_{h_1+h_2}(A)\).

**13.** When can a jump invalidate naive transition composition?  
*Solution:* When a jump occurs between the two subintervals.

**14.** State covariance symmetry.  
*Solution:* \(\Sigma=\Sigma^\mathsf{T}\).

**15.** State covariance positive semidefiniteness.  
*Solution:* \(x^\mathsf{T}\Sigma x\ge0\) for all \(x\).

**16.** State an orthogonal matrix invariant.  
*Solution:* \(Q^\mathsf{T}Q=I\).

**17.** State long-only portfolio feasibility.  
*Solution:* \(\sum_iw_i=1\) and every \(w_i\ge0\).

**18.** Does feasibility imply optimality?  
*Solution:* No.

**19.** Can an invariant alone prove implementation correctness?  
*Solution:* No; different bugs may preserve the same property.
