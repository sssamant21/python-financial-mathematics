# 20.8 — Review Findings and Release Gates

## Learning objectives

Record a review finding with evidence and an owner; distinguish severity, status, remediation and acceptance; design explicit release gates; and explain why a passing automated check does not replace independent mathematical and business review.

## 1. From test results to decisions

A test result is an observation, not a release decision. A review finding explains a discrepancy or limitation, the affected scope, its potential impact, and the evidence supporting it. A release gate defines what evidence and decisions are required before a particular use is authorized.

For a financial model, a calculation that produces a number is not necessarily correct, appropriate, reliable, or approved for production. The gate should address the intended use, input domain, assumptions and consequences of failure.

## 2. Anatomy of a finding

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| ID and title | Identify and summarize the issue |
| Evidence and source version | Make the observation traceable |
| Expected and actual behavior | Explain the discrepancy |
| Impact and affected scope | State who or what could be affected |
| Severity and rationale | Support prioritization using an agreed scheme |
| Owner and due date | Assign remediation responsibility |
| Status and resolution | Track open, remediated, verified or accepted risk |
| Reviewer and decision | Record independent verification and authority |

A severity scale is a local policy, not a universal mathematical fact. Define criteria before applying it. For example, an incorrect price within the supported domain may have high impact; a documentation typo may be low impact. Assess materiality and likelihood in the actual context.

## 3. Finding lifecycle

A useful lifecycle is: identify → triage → assign → remediate → independently verify → close. If remediation is deferred, an authorized owner may accept a documented risk for a defined scope and expiration under the organization's policy. Acceptance is not a fix, and an exception must not be silently treated as a passing test.

When code changes, rerun relevant tests and re-evaluate the affected evidence. Link the finding to the new commit and verification result. A change in scope, model assumptions, or input source can invalidate prior approval.

## 4. Example: discounting defect

Suppose a function calculates \(PV=F(1+r)^n\) where its specification requires \(PV=F/(1+r)^n\). For \(F=100,r=0.25,n=1\), the independent expected value is 80, while the defective result is 125. Record the exact source version, inputs, observed result, financial convention, test output and scope. Assign a knowledgeable owner, correct the formula, and have an independent reviewer verify the fix and its related tests.

A passing scaling property alone would miss this defect because both formulas are linear in \(F\). A known-value benchmark complements the property test.

## 5. A release gate

An illustrative gate for a model release may require:

1. A versioned specification, intended use, units and domain.
2. Independent analytic benchmarks, boundary tests and justified numerical tolerances.
3. Reproducible execution evidence tied to the exact source and inputs.
4. Independent mathematical and code review, with documented scope.
5. Resolution or authorized, time-limited acceptance of findings according to policy.
6. Business-owner assessment of intended use and material assumptions.
7. Deployment, monitoring and rollback plans where production use is proposed.
8. A recorded decision by the person or body authorized to approve that use.

Each condition needs a named evidence source and decision owner. Missing evidence means the gate is not demonstrated; it is not equivalent to a failed calculation. Treat “pending,” “failed,” and “approved” as different states.

## 6. Automated gates and human judgment

Automation can verify that required fields are present or that a check has a passing status. It cannot establish that an independent reviewer is qualified, evidence is authentic, a tolerance is economically acceptable, or the model fits its use. Claims must be verified against authoritative records. The companion code illustrates a conservative evidence-completeness check, not an approval engine.

A Boolean gate is useful for a narrow checklist, but retain explanatory reasons. Do not infer approval from a successful program exit.

## 7. Twenty worked exercises

1. A test failure is an observation that may lead to a finding.
2. A finding needs evidence and an affected scope.
3. Severity requires a stated local classification rule.
4. A named owner is responsible for remediation, not necessarily independent verification.
5. An unresolved defect should not be labeled fixed.
6. A documented exception is risk acceptance, not remediation.
7. An exception should have an authorized owner, scope and expiration.
8. A changed formula can require the relevant tests to be rerun.
9. A commit identifier links evidence to a source version.
10. \(100/(1.25)=80\) for a one-period effective rate of 25%.
11. \(100(1.25)=125\) is accumulation, not that discounted value.
12. A linearity test alone can miss the defect in exercise 11.
13. Missing evidence is different from evidence of a failed test.
14. A passing test does not establish mathematical review.
15. Mathematical review does not automatically authorize production use.
16. A gate needs an intended use.
17. A production gate may require rollback and monitoring plans.
18. A reviewer should record scope and unresolved findings.
19. An approval should identify its authority and version.
20. A change in inputs or use can require reassessment.

## 8. Twelve-step hands-on lab

1. Specify a model's intended use and domain.
2. Identify a known-value test and its expected result.
3. Record a synthetic discrepancy and its evidence.
4. Classify severity with a written rationale.
5. Assign an owner and due date.
6. Define a remediation and link its source version.
7. Record independent verification of the fix.
8. List required gate evidence.
9. Distinguish missing, failed and passed checks.
10. Record any exception, its scope and expiry under policy.
11. Evaluate the illustrative checklist without granting approval.
12. Document the authorized release decision separately when made.
