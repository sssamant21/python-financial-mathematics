## Learning objectives

Define property and metamorphic checks, derive valid financial relations with explicit assumptions, generate reproducible test cases, select numerical tolerances, and recognize the limits of tests that compare related outputs.

## 1. From examples to properties

A known-value test checks one result against a reference. A property check tests a statement expected to hold for many admissible inputs. For example, continuously discounted present value with a fixed rate and time is linear in its signed cash flow:

\[
P(F;r,T)=Fe^{-rT},\quad
P(F_1+F_2;r,T)=P(F_1;r,T)+P(F_2;r,T).
\]

Its domain and numerical range matter. The relation assumes a common rate and time, linear valuation, and representable results.

A property is not necessarily an invariant under every transformation. Specify what changes, what stays fixed, the expected relation, and assumptions.

## 2. Metamorphic testing

A **metamorphic relation** connects outputs for transformed inputs when a reliable expected output for each case is difficult to obtain. Starting with a source input, transform it and compare the resulting outputs against a derived relation.

| Source and transformation | Expected relation | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Scale cash flow \(F\) by \(c\) | \(P(cF)=cP(F)\) | Same rate and horizon; linear valuation |
| Split \(F\) into \(F_1+F_2\) | Sum of split PVs equals original PV | Same date and discount convention |
| Permute cash-flow pairs | Total PV unchanged | Dates stay paired with amounts |
| Increase rate from \(r_1\) to \(r_2\) | \(P(F;r_2,T)\le P(F;r_1,T)\) | \(F>0,T>0,r_2>r_1\), continuous rates |
| Compose discount intervals | \(D(r,t+s)=D(r,t)D(r,s)\) | Constant rate and nonnegative times |

For a negative cash flow, rate monotonicity reverses. For a time-varying rate, use interval-specific factors rather than applying the constant-rate formula blindly.

## 3. Derivation of rate monotonicity

For \(F>0,T>0\),

\[
\frac{\partial P}{\partial r}=-TF e^{-rT}<0.
\]

Thus increasing the continuously compounded rate decreases present value. At \(T=0\) or \(F=0\), the derivative is zero, so strict decrease does not apply. For \(F<0\), the derivative is positive. This illustrates why a property must include its hypotheses.

## 4. A cash-flow example

Suppose cash flows \((100,1)\) and \((200,2)\) are measured in currency and years at \(r=0\). Their total PV is 300. Reordering these pairs keeps the total unchanged. Splitting the second payment into 80 and 120 at year 2 also keeps the total unchanged. Moving part of it to a different date is a different transformation and generally changes value at a nonzero rate.

A test must transform dates and amounts together. Permuting amounts independently tests a different portfolio, not an ordering property.

## 5. Constructing useful cases

Choose cases to cover positive, negative and zero amounts; zero and nonzero horizons; positive and negative rates; and multiple scales. Include boundaries, near-boundaries, and representative ordinary values. Sampled cases cannot prove a universal statement. Generated tests should use a recorded seed and a bounded domain, with failures retaining their inputs.

Shrink or simplify failing cases where possible. Record the seed, generator, test count, software version, input, transformation, tolerance, and observed discrepancy so a failure can be reproduced.

## 6. Floating-point comparisons

For computed quantities use a justified tolerance. Python's math.isclose uses a symmetric relative scale and an absolute floor. Near zero, an absolute tolerance matters. For sums, order can affect rounding; math.fsum reduces summation error but does not make arbitrary formulas exact. Thresholds should be chosen from a documented error budget and units, not widened merely to make a test pass.

Exclude or deliberately test numerical overflow and underflow. A metamorphic property holds mathematically only under its hypotheses; finite-precision computations can fail to preserve it exactly.

## 7. Avoid false confidence

A function that always returns zero satisfies linearity, scaling, and permutation checks, but produces incorrect prices. Two functions with compensating errors can pass an inverse check. Pair metamorphic checks with independent known-value benchmarks, input-domain tests, mathematical review, and implementation review.

Test a property by intentionally introducing a plausible defect: for example, dropping one cash flow should be caught by a known-value test. Mutation experiments provide evidence of test sensitivity, not proof of complete coverage.

## 8. Twenty worked exercises

1. A property check tests a relation over admissible inputs.
2. A metamorphic test compares outputs for related inputs.
3. At zero rate, PV of 100 after two years is 100.
4. At the same rate and date, PV of 40 plus PV of 60 equals PV of 100.
5. Multiplying a cash flow by 2 multiplies its PV by 2 under linear discounting.
6. Multiplying by -1 reverses the sign of PV.
7. With positive cash flow and positive time, increasing a continuous rate reduces PV.
8. For a negative cash flow, the direction in exercise 7 reverses.
9. At time zero, changing the rate does not change PV.
10. A zero cash flow has zero mathematical PV.
11. Reordering amount-date pairs leaves total PV unchanged mathematically.
12. Reordering amounts while holding dates fixed generally changes PV.
13. Splitting a payment at the same date preserves PV.
14. Moving one part to another date generally does not preserve PV.
15. Constant-rate discount factors compose over consecutive intervals.
16. A variable-rate curve needs interval-specific discount factors.
17. An always-zero implementation passes scaling but fails a nonzero known-value benchmark.
18. A fixed random seed supports reproduction but does not prove correctness.
19. A tolerance needs an error rationale and units.
20. A feasibility check does not prove optimality.

## 9. Twelve-step hands-on lab

1. Define the continuous discounting model and units.
2. List valid ranges and numerical limits.
3. Implement scalar present value.
4. Implement cash-flow valuation with paired amount and time.
5. Check a zero-rate known value independently.
6. Check scaling for positive and negative factors.
7. Split one payment at the same date.
8. Reverse the order of amount-date pairs.
9. Check positive-cash-flow rate monotonicity.
10. Check constant-rate discount composition.
11. Generate bounded deterministic cases using a recorded seed.
12. Retain inputs, tolerance, results, runtime and review status when executing validation.
